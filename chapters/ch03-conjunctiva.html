Chapter 3

Diseases of the Conjunctiva

Conjunctivitis (bacterial, viral, allergic), trachoma, vernal keratoconjunctivitis, pterygium — common outpatient and board-level topics.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Conjunctival Anatomy

UG
▼
  • Palpebral conjunctiva: Covers inner surface of eyelids; highly vascular, loose tissue; adherent to tarsal plate
  • Forniceal conjunctiva: Transitional zone between palpebral and bulbar; forms upper and lower fornices (cul-de-sacs)
  • Bulbar conjunctiva: Covers anterior sclera up to corneal limbus; loosely attached except at limbus; contains Tenon's capsule
Conjunctival epithelium: Stratified columnar (goblet cells interspersed); produces mucin layer of tear film. Highly vascular (conjunctival circulation from ophthalmic artery).

Limbus: Border between cornea and sclera; contains stem cells for corneal epithelial regeneration; avascular zone medially.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis

UGPG
▼

Acute Bacterial Conjunctivitis

  • Common organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus aegyptius
  • Presentation: Rapid onset (24–48 h); purulent discharge; lid sticking in morning
  • Examination: Chemosis, papillary reaction (upper tarsus), follicles absent (bacterial) vs present (viral)
  • Management: Topical antibiotics (fluoroquinolone, aminoglycosides); eye wash; hygiene; usually self-limiting (2–3 weeks)

Hyperacute Bacterial Conjunctivitis

  • Cause: Neisseria gonorrhoeae (sexually transmitted; newborn ophthalmia neonatorum); Neisseria meningitidis
  • Presentation: Explosive onset (<24 h); copious purulent discharge; severe pain; pseudomembrane
  • Danger: Corneal involvement → opacity, scarring, perforation if untreated
  • Management: Systemic + topical antibiotics (ceftriaxone IV/IM); hospital admission; newborn prophylaxis (silver nitrate 1%, tetracycline, erythromycin)
️ Prevention (Newborn): Silver nitrate 1%, tetracycline 1%, or erythromycin 0.5% within 1 hour of delivery (Credé prophylaxis) prevents gonococcal ophthalmia. If maternal gonorrhea/chlamydia + newborn symptoms → treat systemically.

Chronic Bacterial Conjunctivitis

  • Cause: Staphylococcus aureus (most common); associated with blepharitis, meibomian gland disease
  • Features: Mild inflammation, mucopurulent discharge, papillary reaction; may progress to chronic blepharoconjunctivitis
  • Treatment: Lid hygiene, warm compress, topical antibiotics (tetracycline 1% ointment at night)

Viral Conjunctivitis

UGPG
▼

Acute Hemorrhagic Conjunctivitis (AHC)

  • Agents: Enterovirus 70, Coxsackie A24 (summer/autumn; pandemic potential)
  • Features: Sudden onset; subconjunctival hemorrhages; watery discharge; follicular reaction (limbal); preauricular lymphadenopathy
  • Duration: 5–7 days; self-limiting
  • Management: Symptomatic; cold compress; avoid contact; strict hand hygiene

Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (EKC)

  • Agents: Adenovirus types 8, 19, 37 (highest contagiosity); healthcare workers at risk
  • Presentation: Preauricular lymphadenopathy (pathognomonic), pseudomembrane, fine punctate keratitis (heals in 1–2 weeks)
  • Subepithelial infiltrates: May persist for months → transient vision reduction
  • Management: Supportive; topical NSAIDs for discomfort; avoid touching; infection control crucial
Mnemonic — Viral Agents
AHC = EV70, Coxsackie A24 · EKC = Adeno 8, 19, 37
AHC hemorrhages; EKC subepithelial infiltrates + preauricular LAD

Trachoma — Leading Infectious Cause of Blindness

UGPG
▼

Overview

  • Agent: Chlamydia trachomatis (serovars A, B, Ba, C = blinding trachoma; serovars D–K = non-blinding); obligate intracellular
  • Epidemiology: Affects 2 billion globally; endemic in arid, poor sanitation regions (sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East, Asia)
  • Transmission: Eye-to-eye (fingers, fomites), vertical transmission (neonatal inclusion conjunctivitis)
Fig 3.1 — MacCallan's classification of trachoma stages

WHO SAFE Strategy (Elimination)

  • S — Surgery: Trichiasis correction, entropion repair (TT stage)
  • A — Antibiotics: Azithromycin mass drug administration (MDA) for active TF/TS cases
  • F — Facial cleanliness: Health education; water access; sanitation
  • E — Environmental improvement: Latrine construction, water supply, hygiene
Elimination Target: TF <5% in children 1–9 years, TT <0.2% in adults (WHO validation criteria)

Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC)

UGPG
▼
  • Epidemiology: Children/adolescents; warm climate; seasonal exacerbation (spring/summer); self-limited (resolves by puberty)
  • Etiology: IgE-mediated hypersensitivity (Type I); associated with atopy
  • Features: Intense itching, photophobia, watery discharge (mucoid), giant papillae on upper tarsus (cobblestone appearance)

Pathological Signs

  • Horner-Trantas dots: White dots at limbus (eosinophilic infiltrates); diagnostic
  • Giant papillae (cobblestone): >1 mm, flattened tops; upper tarsus primarily
  • Shield ulcer: Epithelial defect upper central cornea (mechanical trauma + allergic mediators); risk of secondary infection
  • Vernal plaque: Mucus stringy discharge; can occlude pupil if neglected

Management (Stepwise)

  • Tier 1: Cold compress, lubricants, avoid allergen
  • Tier 2: Topical mast cell stabilizers (cromolyn), antihistamines (levocabastine)
  • Tier 3: Topical corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1%, dexamethasone; short course to avoid side effects)
  • Tier 4 (severe/refractory): Topical calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine 1–2%), immunosuppressants
  • Surgical: Mechanical debridement of giant papillae if severe; reserved for refractory cases
️ Shield Ulcer Risk: Monitor closely; risk of scarring, astigmatism, vision loss. If present, use topical antibiotics + lubricants, consider soft contact lens or therapeutic lenses.

Allergic, Parinaud's, and Structural Disorders

UGPG
▼

Allergic Conjunctivitis (Seasonal/Perennial)

  • Etiology: Type I hypersensitivity (pollen, dust, drugs, contact lens solutions)
  • Features: Itching (prominent), watery discharge, conjunctival injection, mild papillary reaction
  • Management: Allergen avoidance, topical antihistamine (ketotifen), mast cell stabilizers (cromolyn)

Parinaud's Oculoglandular Conjunctivitis (PCPF)

  • Definition: Granulomatous conjunctivitis + preauricular/submandibular lymphadenopathy
  • Causes: Cat scratch disease (Bartonella henselae), toxoplasmosis, syphilis, TB, brucellosis, tularemia
  • Examination: Follicular reaction, giant papillae, granulomatous inflammation; systemic evaluation essential

Pterygium vs Pinguecula

FeaturePterygiumPinguecula
CompositionFibrovascular tissue + hyalinized collagenHyalinized collagen deposit (no vessels)
LocationEncroaches onto cornea (nasal > temporal)Bulbar conjunctiva (nasal side); does NOT invade cornea
GrowthActively progressive; can cause astigmatismStatic; rarely changes
EtiologyUV exposure, chronic irritation, inflammatoryDegeneration (age, UV)
TreatmentSurgical excision (+ intra-op MMC or radiation to ↓ recurrence); glasses for induced astigmatismUsually observation; surgery if cosmetically bothersome or causes irritation
Pterygium expansion: Initially static for years, then rapid growth (invading 1–2 mm/year into cornea). Beta radiation or mitomycin C (MMC) at surgery ↓ recurrence from 40% → 5%.

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Discuss the pathogenesis, clinical features, and management of trachoma. Highlight the WHO SAFE strategy. (10 marks)
Pathogenesis: Chlamydia trachomatis serovars A–C (intracellular), transmitted eye-to-eye, endemic in arid regions. Stages: TF (follicles, intact limbus) → TS (intense inflammation, limbal damage) → TA (scarring, Arlt's line) → TT (trichiasis, entropion, blindness). University Clinical Exam
A 10-year-old child presents with intense itching, photophobia, and giant papillae on upper tarsal surface. Horner-Trantas dots visible at limbus. Diagnosis and management? (6 marks)
Diagnosis: Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC). Management: Cold compress, lubricants (avoid allergen) → Mast cell stabilizers (cromolyn) → Topical corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1%, short course) → Calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine 1–2%) if severe. Monitor for shield ulcer (upper central corneal epithelial defect). Mechanical debridement of papillae only if refractory.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis Summary

  • Acute: Rapid onset (24–48 h), purulent discharge, papillary reaction, S. aureus/S. pneumoniae
  • Hyperacute: Explosive (<24 h), copious pus, pseudo-membranes, N. gonorrhoeae (systemic Rx needed)
  • Chronic: Mild mucopurulent, S. aureus (linked to blepharitis); tetracycline ointment
  • Credé prophylaxis: Silver nitrate 1%, tetracycline 1%, erythromycin 0.5% within 1 hr birth (prevents gonococcal)

Viral Conjunctivitis Agents

  • AHC (Enterovirus 70, Coxsackie A24): Subconjunctival hemorrhages, watery, follicular (limbal), 5–7 days
  • EKC (Adeno 8, 19, 37): Preauricular LAD (pathognomonic), pseudomembrane, subepithelial infiltrates (persist months)
  • HVK (HSV-1/2): Vesicles on eyelid, unilateral, dendrite keratitis, pain

Trachoma — MacCallan's Stages

  • TF: Trachomatous Inflammation-Follicular (≥5 follicles, limbus intact)
  • TS: Trachomatous Inflammation-Intense (>50% tarsal obscured, limbal damage)
  • TA: Trachomatous Scarring (Arlt's line, white lines)
  • TT: Trachomatous Trichiasis (≥1 inturned lash, entropion, blindness)
  • Serovars A, B, Ba, C = blinding; D–K = non-blinding

VKC & Other Conditions

  • VKC: Children/adolescents, warm climate, spring/summer exacerbation, cobblestone papillae, Horner-Trantas dots, shield ulcer risk
  • Pterygium: Fibrovascular, progressive, invades cornea, causes astigmatism; excision + MMC/radiation ↓ recurrence
  • Pinguecula: Hyalinized, static, does NOT invade cornea
  • Parinaud's PCPF: Granulomatous + preauricular LAD; causes: bartonella (cat scratch), toxo, syphilis, TB
