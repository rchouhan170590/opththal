Diseases of the Conjunctiva

Conjunctivitis in its many forms, trachoma, vernal keratoconjunctivitis, and pterygium — some of the most common conditions seen in any outpatient clinic and consistently tested at every level of examination.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Five cards covering the highest-yield conjunctival topics. Trachoma, VKC, and the bacterial vs viral distinction appear in almost every examination at every level.

Conjunctival Anatomy

The conjunctiva lines the inner surface of the eyelids (palpebral), reflects into the fornices, and covers the anterior sclera to the limbus (bulbar). Its epithelium is stratified columnar with goblet cells that produce the mucin layer of the tear film. The bulbar conjunctiva is loosely attached to the underlying Tenon's capsule everywhere except at the limbus, which is the junction with the cornea and the site of limbal stem cells critical for corneal epithelial regeneration.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis — Key Types

Acute (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae): purulent discharge, lid sticking, papillary reaction — topical fluoroquinolone suffices. Hyperacute (N. gonorrhoeae): explosive onset in under 24 hours, copious pus, pseudomembrane, and real corneal perforation risk — treat with systemic ceftriaxone urgently. Chronic (S. aureus with blepharitis): mild mucopurulent discharge, lid hygiene and tetracycline ointment. The hyperacute form is the dangerous one that must never be missed.

Viral Conjunctivitis — AHC vs EKC

AHC (Enterovirus 70, Coxsackie A24): sudden onset, subconjunctival haemorrhages, watery discharge, follicular reaction at limbus. Self-limiting in 5–7 days. EKC (Adenovirus 8, 19, 37): preauricular lymphadenopathy is pathognomonic, pseudomembrane, fine punctate keratitis, and subepithelial infiltrates that can persist for months causing transient visual blurring. Highly contagious — an infection control problem in clinics. Both are self-limiting but EKC leaves a longer clinical tail.

Trachoma — MacCallan Stages

The leading infectious cause of blindness globally. Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis serovars A, B, Ba, C. MacCallan's classification: TF = ≥5 follicles on upper tarsus with intact limbus; TS = intense inflammation obscuring more than half the tarsal surface; TA = scarring (Arlt's line, white lines on tarsus); TT = trichiasis with ≥1 lash touching globe. TT leads to corneal opacification and blindness. The SAFE strategy (Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement) targets elimination.

Conjunctival Anatomy & Physiology

UG

Regions of the Conjunctiva

The palpebral conjunctiva is highly vascular, adherent to the tarsal plate, and is where follicles and papillae are most easily seen on eversion of the lid. The forniceal conjunctiva is the loose transitional zone that forms the cul-de-sac, allowing globe movement without tethering. The bulbar conjunctiva covers the anterior sclera from the fornix to the limbus and is loosely attached to Tenon's capsule except at the limbus itself, where it fuses firmly with the underlying tissue.

Epithelium and Goblet Cells

Conjunctival epithelium is stratified columnar, not squamous, which distinguishes it from corneal epithelium. Goblet cells are interspersed throughout and produce mucin, the innermost layer of the tear film. In conditions like vitamin A deficiency and cicatricial pemphigoid, goblet cell loss leads to dry eye and squamous metaplasia. The density of goblet cells is highest in the inferior nasal fornix and lowest at the limbus.

Limbus and Stem Cells

The limbus is not simply a border — it is the site of the palisades of Vogt, anatomical structures that house the limbal stem cells responsible for continuous corneal epithelial renewal. When stem cells are destroyed by chemical burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, or severe trachoma, the resulting limbal stem cell deficiency leads to conjunctivalisation of the cornea (conjunctival epithelium growing over the corneal surface with vascularization), a serious cause of visual loss.

The conjunctival stroma contains accessory lacrimal glands of Krause (in the fornices) and Wolfring (near the tarsal borders). These contribute to basal tear secretion and can partially compensate for reduced main lacrimal gland function.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis

UGPG
▼

Acute Bacterial Conjunctivitis

The classic presentation is a rapid onset over 24–48 hours of purulent or mucopurulent discharge, lid sticking in the morning, and conjunctival injection. The upper tarsal conjunctiva shows a papillary reaction — follicles are absent in bacterial conjunctivitis, which helps distinguish it from viral and chlamydial infection. Common organisms include Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus aegyptius (the Egyptian eye fly). The condition is generally self-limiting over 2–3 weeks, but topical fluoroquinolone or aminoglycoside drops hasten resolution and reduce spread.

Hyperacute Bacterial Conjunctivitis

This is the dangerous form. Neisseria gonorrhoeae causes an explosive-onset conjunctivitis within 24 hours of exposure, with copious purulent discharge that reaccumulates rapidly after wiping, severe pain, pseudomembrane formation, and the very real risk of corneal perforation if treatment is delayed. In newborns, gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum presents between days 1 and 5 of life after passage through an infected birth canal. Neisseria meningitidis can cause a similar picture. Treatment requires systemic ceftriaxone (intramuscular or intravenous) in addition to topical antibiotics and aggressive eye irrigation. This is a clinical emergency.

Credé prophylaxis — instillation of silver nitrate 1%, tetracycline 1%, or erythromycin 0.5% within the first hour of delivery — was designed specifically to prevent gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum. Modern erythromycin-based prophylaxis also provides some cover against chlamydial infection.

Chlamydial Conjunctivitis (Inclusion Conjunctivitis)

Chlamydia trachomatis serovars D–K cause a chronic, low-grade follicular conjunctivitis in sexually active adults, often accompanied by a mucopurulent discharge and preauricular lymphadenopathy. Unlike trachoma, it does not scar the cornea in adults. In newborns, chlamydial ophthalmia neonatorum presents later than gonococcal disease — typically at 5–14 days — and requires systemic erythromycin rather than topical therapy alone, as the infant likely has concomitant respiratory chlamydial infection.

Any newborn with purulent eye discharge requires conjunctival scraping for Gram stain and culture, not just topical drops. Missing gonococcal conjunctivitis in a neonate can result in corneal perforation within 24–48 hours.

Viral Conjunctivitis

UGPG
▼

Acute Haemorrhagic Conjunctivitis (AHC)

AHC is caused by Enterovirus 70 and Coxsackie A24, both of which can cause epidemic spread, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. The hallmark is the sudden appearance of subconjunctival haemorrhages — often alarming to the patient — accompanied by a watery (not purulent) discharge and a follicular reaction predominantly at the limbus. Preauricular lymphadenopathy is mild. The condition resolves spontaneously within 5–7 days. Management is supportive: cold compresses, lubricants, and strict hand hygiene to prevent spread. No antiviral treatment is available.

Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (EKC)

EKC is one of the most contagious eye conditions encountered in clinical practice. Adenovirus types 8, 19, and 37 are responsible, with type 8 carrying the highest contagiosity. Healthcare workers in ophthalmology clinics are at particular risk from contaminated instruments. The clinical picture unfolds in two phases: an early conjunctival phase with watery discharge, follicular reaction, pseudomembrane formation, and the pathognomonic preauricular lymphadenopathy; followed by a keratitic phase 7–10 days later with fine punctate epithelial keratitis that coalesces into subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs). These SEIs represent an immune response to viral antigen in the stroma and can persist for months, causing glare and transient reduction in best-corrected visual acuity. Treatment is supportive; topical corticosteroids suppress SEIs if visually significant but can prolong the infectious period and are used cautiously.

Follicles are the hallmark of viral (and chlamydial) conjunctivitis. They represent germinal centre-like lymphoid aggregates in the substantia propria — visible as translucent, avascular, dome-shaped elevations. Papillae, in contrast, have a central vascular core and indicate a non-specific inflammatory or allergic response. Distinguishing them is a fundamental clinical skill.

Trachoma

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Pathogen and Epidemiology

Trachoma is caused by Chlamydia trachomatis serovars A, B, Ba, and C — the blinding serovars. Serovars D through K cause the non-blinding genital form (inclusion conjunctivitis, discussed separately). Trachoma remains the leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide, affecting communities in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and parts of Latin America — wherever water is scarce, sanitation is poor, and flies are abundant. Transmission is eye-to-eye via fingers, fomites, and face flies. Repeated reinfection during childhood drives the progressive scarring that causes blindness in adulthood.

MacCallan's Classification

The WHO-adopted MacCallan staging describes the disease in four stages. In TF (Trachomatous Inflammation-Follicular), there are at least five follicles of at least 0.5 mm diameter on the upper tarsal conjunctiva, with the limbal architecture still intact. In TS (Trachomatous Inflammation-Intense), the inflammation becomes intense enough to obscure more than half the deep tarsal vascular pattern, and the limbus begins to be damaged. TA (Trachomatous Scarring) is characterised by white fibrous linear scars on the upper tarsus — Herbert's pits at the limbus (from resolved limbal follicles) and Arlt's line (a horizontal scar on the tarsal conjunctiva). TT (Trachomatous Trichiasis) means at least one inturned eyelash touches the globe. This mechanical corneal abrasion drives progressive corneal opacification and eventual blindness.

WHO SAFE Strategy

The SAFE strategy has guided global elimination efforts. Surgery targets TT — trichiasis correction through bilamellar tarsal rotation prevents further corneal abrasion. Antibiotics means azithromycin mass drug administration (MDA) to the entire community in endemic areas, targeting active TF and TS stages. Facial cleanliness addresses transmission by improving hygiene, particularly around children's eyes. Environmental improvement — building latrines, improving water supply, reducing fly breeding sites — addresses the root determinants of transmission. The WHO validation criteria for elimination are TF prevalence below 5% in children aged 1–9 years, and TT prevalence below 0.2% in adults over 15 years.

SAFE Strategy
Surgery (trichiasis correction) · Antibiotics (azithromycin MDA) · Facial cleanliness · Environmental improvement. In that order — treat the advanced disease first, then attack transmission.

Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC)

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Overview and Pathophysiology

VKC is a chronic, bilateral, recurrent conjunctival inflammation driven primarily by a type I IgE-mediated hypersensitivity response, with contributions from type IV delayed hypersensitivity. It affects children and adolescents predominantly, with a marked male predominance in younger age groups, and a strong association with atopic conditions including asthma and eczema. Warm climates and spring and summer seasons are associated with exacerbations — the name "vernal" reflects this seasonality. The condition typically resolves by or shortly after puberty, though some patients continue into adulthood.

Clinical Features and Pathological Signs

The dominant symptom is intense itching, often out of proportion to what the examination reveals. Photophobia and a stringy mucoid discharge are characteristic — the discharge in VKC is distinctly mucoid rather than watery or purulent. On upper lid eversion, the tarsal conjunctiva shows the giant papillae — each more than 1 mm in diameter with a flat top and a fibrovascular core — giving the characteristic cobblestone appearance. The limbal form of VKC shows Horner-Trantas dots, which are white, chalky accumulations of degenerated eosinophils at the limbus; these are pathognomonic of VKC. Reilly's line — a ring of superior limbal follicular hypertrophy — may also be seen.

Shield Ulcer

The shield ulcer is the most serious complication of VKC. It is a sterile epithelial defect in the upper central cornea, formed by the combined mechanical trauma from the giant papillae abrading the corneal surface and the toxic effects of eosinophil-derived mediators on the epithelium. The ulcer has a characteristic oval shape with a plaque of mucus and fibrin in the base. It is not infected primarily, but carries a significant risk of secondary bacterial infection and corneal scarring if not managed. Management involves aggressive lubrication, topical antibiotics to prevent secondary infection, a bandage contact lens to reduce mechanical trauma, and addressing the underlying allergic inflammation.

Management Stepwise

Treatment follows a tiered approach. Most patients are managed with cold compresses, lubricants, and removal from the provoking environment. Topical mast cell stabilisers such as sodium cromoglycate and olopatadine serve as first-line preventive therapy. When symptoms break through, topical antihistamines and NSAIDS provide relief. Topical corticosteroids — prednisolone 0.5–1% or dexamethasone — are highly effective for acute exacerbations but must be used in short courses because of the risk of steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract in children. For severe, corticosteroid-dependent cases, topical cyclosporine 1–2% (a calcineurin inhibitor) has become the preferred long-term steroid-sparing agent.

Topical corticosteroids should not be used long-term in children with VKC without careful monitoring of intraocular pressure and lens clarity. Children are particularly susceptible to steroid-induced glaucoma, and the pressure rise can be rapid and severe.

Pterygium, Pinguecula & Other Conjunctival Disorders

UGPG
▼

Pterygium

A pterygium is a wing-shaped fibrovascular growth of the conjunctiva that encroaches onto the corneal surface, typically from the nasal side. It arises in response to chronic ultraviolet exposure, which induces limbal stem cell dysfunction and a localised degenerative-inflammatory process. Pterygium begins at the limbus and grows across Bowman's layer onto the cornea; the point where it attaches to the cornea is called the head, and the body lies over the sclera. As it grows, it may cause induced astigmatism, foreign body sensation, and cosmetic concern. Surgical excision is indicated when the lesion threatens the visual axis, causes significant astigmatism, or is symptomatic. The major surgical complication is recurrence, which historically occurred in about 30–40% of cases; adjunctive use of intraoperative mitomycin C (MMC) or postoperative beta irradiation reduces this to under 5%.

Pinguecula

A pinguecula is a yellow-white deposit on the bulbar conjunctiva, typically nasal but sometimes temporal, that represents hyaline degeneration of the conjunctival stroma. Unlike pterygium, it does not invade the cornea. It is a benign, generally static finding associated with ageing and UV exposure. Treatment is usually unnecessary, though lubricants and UV-blocking glasses slow progression. Surgical removal is rarely performed and only for significant cosmetic concern or persistent irritation.

Parinaud's Oculoglandular Syndrome

This is a granulomatous conjunctivitis accompanied by regional (preauricular or submandibular) lymphadenopathy. It is caused by organisms that tend to produce granulomatous inflammation: Bartonella henselae (cat scratch disease) is the most common cause, followed by Francisella tularensis (tularaemia), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum, and Toxoplasma gondii. The diagnosis requires thinking beyond the eye and always asking about animal contact, travel, and systemic symptoms. Treatment is directed at the underlying organism.

The distinction between a follicle and a papilla has real diagnostic implications. Follicles — avascular, dome-shaped, translucent lymphoid aggregates — indicate viral or chlamydial infection (or drug toxicity from topical medications used long-term). Papillae — vascularised, with a central fibrovascular core and a surrounding ring of vessels — indicate bacterial infection, allergy, or mechanical irritation. Giant papillae (over 1 mm) are seen in VKC and contact lens-related giant papillary conjunctivitis.

Three practical skills that map directly to conjunctival pathology in the clinic. Each is structured as a sequence of steps to guide your thinking at the slit lamp and in the examination room.

Systematic Examination of the Conjunctiva
Clinical Technique
1
Inspect the anterior segment without touching the patient

Begin at arm's length before the slit lamp. Note the character of any discharge — watery suggests viral, purulent bacterial, mucoid allergic, or bloody in AHC. Look at the lid margins for crusting, telangiectasia, or meibomian gland plugging that suggests blepharitis underlying a chronic conjunctivitis.

2
Lower lid eversion and bulbar conjunctiva

Pull the lower lid down gently and ask the patient to look up. Examine the palpebral conjunctiva of the lower lid and the inferior fornix. Look for follicles, papillae, symblepharon (adhesions between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva), and any foreign bodies in the fornix. Then sweep across the bulbar conjunctiva — note injection patterns (limbal flush suggests uveitis, generalised injection suggests conjunctivitis), the limbus for Horner-Trantas dots, and any limbal follicles.

In EKC, the preauricular node is palpable before you even sit at the slit lamp. Make it a habit to palpate the preauricular region in every patient presenting with a red watery eye.
3
Upper lid eversion

This is the critical step — most students evert the upper lid incorrectly and then do not look systematically at what they have exposed. The technique: ask the patient to look down, grasp the upper lashes between thumb and forefinger, place a cotton bud on the skin just above the upper tarsal border, and fold the lid upward over the bud while the patient keeps looking down. Hold this position and release the bud. Now examine the upper tarsal plate methodically for follicles, papillae, membranes, and scars. Giant papillae in VKC are unmistakable — pale, flat-topped mountains crowding the entire tarsal surface.

4
Fluorescein staining and corneal examination

Install fluorescein and examine with blue light. Corneal involvement changes your diagnosis and your urgency. Punctate epithelial erosions (PEEs) over the entire cornea suggest viral or toxic; a dendrite suggests herpetic disease; a shield-shaped ulcer in the upper third suggests VKC; and a contact lens-related corneal infiltrate or ulcer requires urgent culture. Fluorescein also highlights a pseudomembrane if present.

Always compare the fellow eye. Most conjunctival infections are bilateral, though one eye may lag behind. Unilateral disease should always prompt you to consider alternative diagnoses: herpetic disease, lacrimal obstruction, or a foreign body.
Recognising and Staging Trachoma in the Field
Community Ophthalmology
1
Use appropriate lighting and a loupe

Field grading of trachoma uses a 2.5x binocular loupe and a focal light source. More than half of trachoma cases globally are graded in field conditions, not at slit lamps. Good lighting is essential — inadequate lighting misses follicles and scars.

2
Grade the upper tarsal conjunctiva

Evert the upper lid and examine the central third of the tarsal plate. TF requires at least five follicles measuring 0.5 mm or more in the central part of the tarsal plate — count them. TS is diagnosed when the overlying inflammation is so intense that you cannot see more than half the deep tarsal vessels. TA is diagnosed by the presence of white fibrous linear bands or sheets on the tarsus (Arlt's line).

3
Assess for trichiasis and corneal opacity

TT is graded by examining whether any eyelashes — of any follicle group — touch the globe, or by the presence of evidence of epilation (removal of inturned lashes). CO (corneal opacity) is the end stage, documented as any opacity over the pupil dense enough to be easily visible, reducing visual acuity to less than 6/18. These two grades represent the irreversible, vision-threatening stage of the disease.

In field surveys, always record the WHO grade for each eye separately. The eye-level prevalence drives decision-making for MDA, and the individual-level TT prevalence drives surgical programme targets.
Managing the VKC Patient in Clinic
Clinical Management
1
Assess severity and look for a shield ulcer

Begin by asking about the dominant symptom — itching severity on a 0–10 scale — and recent steroid use. Evert the upper lid and look at the tarsal papillae size and appearance. Then fluorescein stain the cornea carefully. A shield ulcer will show as an oval, fluorescein-positive epithelial defect in the upper one-third to one-half of the cornea, often with a white mucus plaque adherent to the base. This changes your management immediately.

If you find a shield ulcer, resist the urge to scrape it or add steroid drops reflexively. The ulcer is sterile initially. Protect it with a bandage soft contact lens and aggressive lubrication while you address the underlying allergic inflammation.
2
Check intraocular pressure before prescribing steroids

If the patient is on or about to start topical steroids, baseline IOP is mandatory. Children are high responders to steroid-induced IOP elevation — some respond within days. Any IOP rise beyond 21 mmHg on steroids requires either switching to a lower-penetrating steroid (loteprednol, fluorometholone) or substituting cyclosporine entirely.

3
Set up a monitoring plan and counsel on seasonality

VKC tends to recur. Counsel parents and patients that the condition is chronic, that exacerbations are predictable around spring and summer, and that mast cell stabilisers work best when started two weeks before the expected season rather than after symptoms have erupted. Plan follow-up visits at 4–6 weekly intervals during active disease, and at each visit explicitly check IOP, lens clarity, and corneal status.

Document the papilla grade at each visit — improvement in papilla size is a useful objective measure of treatment response that supplements subjective symptom scores.

In EKC, subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs) persist months after the acute infection resolves. What is the pathological basis of SEIs?
PG

B Immune-mediated inflammatory infiltrate to adenoviral antigen in the stroma

Explanation
SEIs represent a delayed immune response to adenoviral antigen deposited in the corneal stroma. The virus itself has cleared, but viral protein deposits trigger lymphocytic infiltration. This is why topical corticosteroids suppress SEIs — they are immune, not infectious. However, stopping steroids often leads to recurrence of SEIs because the antigen persists, which is the clinical dilemma in managing EKC.

PG Question 4
A patient with long-standing VKC is started on topical cyclosporine 1%. Compared to topical corticosteroids, the main advantage of cyclosporine in this setting is:
PG
A No risk of steroid-induced glaucoma or cataract
B Greater anti-inflammatory potency
C It eradicates the underlying IgE-mediated allergy

Explanation
Cyclosporine is a calcineurin inhibitor that blocks T-cell activation and IL-2 production, suppressing the allergic inflammatory response. Its major advantage over steroids in chronic VKC in children is the absence of steroid-related side effects — specifically steroid-induced glaucoma and posterior subcapsular cataract. Children are high steroid-responders and long-term steroid use carries serious risks. Cyclosporine is not necessarily more potent, but it is safe for long-term use.

Parinaud's oculoglandular syndrome (granulomatous conjunctivitis with preauricular lymphadenopathy) in a patient who recently adopted a kitten is most likely caused by:

PG Question 8
In pterygium surgery, mitomycin C (MMC) is applied to the bare sclera to reduce the recurrence rate. Its mechanism of action is:
PG
A Alkylation of DNA causing apoptosis of proliferating fibroblasts
B Anti-VEGF activity blocking new vessel formation
C Inhibition of T-cell activation at the surgical site
D Beta radiation damaging the superficial subconjunctival vessels
Explanation
Mitomycin C is an alkylating antibiotic that cross-links DNA strands, inhibiting replication and causing apoptosis of rapidly proliferating fibroblasts. In pterygium surgery, the recurrence is driven by fibroblast proliferation from the residual subconjunctival tissue. Applying MMC to the bare sclera after excision kills these fibroblasts, reducing recurrence from approximately 35–40% to under 5%. Beta radiation works differently — it directly damages the DNA of dividing cells through ionising radiation.
PG Question 9
Follicles are seen in all of the following EXCEPT:
PG
A Viral conjunctivitis
B Chlamydial conjunctivitis
C Acute bacterial conjunctivitis
D Toxic follicular conjunctivitis from topical medications
Explanation
Acute bacterial conjunctivitis produces a papillary reaction, not follicles. Follicles require an intact immune response to a specific antigen and represent lymphoid aggregates with germinal centre-like activity. They are seen in viral conjunctivitis, chlamydial disease, toxic reactions to long-term topical drugs (neomycin, antivirals, preservatives), and molluscum contagiosum. Bacterial conjunctivitis produces non-specific papillary inflammation.
PG Question 10
A patient with trachomatous trichiasis undergoes bilamellar tarsal rotation surgery. The purpose of this procedure is to:
PG
A Treat the active chlamydial infection in the eyelid
B Clear the limbal stem cell deficiency
C Reverse trachomatous corneal opacity
D Rotate the eyelid margin outward so that lashes no longer abrade the cornea
Explanation
The bilamellar tarsal rotation procedure divides the tarsal plate horizontally and rotates the anterior lamella (with the lashes) outward (everted), moving the lashes away from the corneal surface. It does not treat infection (that is done by azithromycin), does not restore limbal stem cells, and does not clear existing corneal opacity. It is purely a mechanical intervention to prevent ongoing corneal abrasion by the inturned lashes.

Three long-answer questions at the 10-mark level, structured with the scoring breakdown a university examiner would expect.

Long Answer 1
Describe the pathogenesis, clinical stages, and management of trachoma. Include the WHO SAFE strategy.
10 Marks
Pathogen and Pathogenesis (2 marks)

Chlamydia trachomatis serovars A, B, Ba, C — obligate intracellular, transmitted eye-to-eye via fingers, fomites, and face flies. Repeated childhood reinfection drives progressive conjunctival scarring through chronic inflammation and fibroblast activation. The tarsal conjunctiva bears the brunt of scarring. Serovars D–K cause genital disease (inclusion conjunctivitis), not blinding trachoma.

MacCallan's Classification (4 marks)

TF (Trachomatous Inflammation-Follicular): ≥5 follicles ≥0.5 mm on upper tarsal conjunctiva, limbus intact, active but early disease. TS (Trachomatous Inflammation-Intense): dense inflammation obscuring more than 50% of tarsal vascular pattern, limbal damage begins. TA (Trachomatous Scarring): white fibrous scars on tarsus — Arlt's line, Herbert's pits at limbus. TT (Trachomatous Trichiasis): ≥1 inturned lash touching globe, leading to corneal abrasion, opacity, and blindness.

WHO SAFE Strategy (3 marks)

Surgery (bilamellar tarsal rotation for TT), Antibiotics (azithromycin MDA for TF/TS — single oral dose, repeated annually), Facial cleanliness (health education, water access), Environmental improvement (latrine construction, water supply). Elimination criteria: TF below 5% in children 1–9 years, TT below 0.2% in adults over 15 years.

Global Impact (1 mark)

Once the leading cause of infectious blindness affecting hundreds of millions, now substantially reduced through SAFE implementation. Several countries have achieved WHO validation. Remaining endemic countries concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.

Long Answer 2
Describe the clinical features, pathological signs, and management of vernal keratoconjunctivitis.
10 Marks
Definition and Epidemiology (1 mark)

Bilateral chronic recurrent conjunctivitis in children and adolescents driven by type I IgE-mediated and type IV delayed hypersensitivity. Male predominance, warm climate, spring/summer exacerbations. Usually resolves by puberty. Associated with atopy.

Clinical Features (2 marks)

Intense itching (dominant symptom), photophobia, stringy mucoid discharge, bilateral. Two forms: palpebral (giant cobblestone papillae on upper tarsus) and limbal (Horner-Trantas dots, limbal follicular hypertrophy — Reilly's line).

Pathological Signs (3 marks)

Giant papillae: more than 1 mm diameter, flat-topped, fibrovascular core on upper tarsal conjunctiva — cobblestone appearance. Horner-Trantas dots: white eosinophilic deposits at limbus — pathognomonic. Shield ulcer: sterile oval epithelial defect in upper central cornea from mechanical trauma and eosinophil mediator toxicity. Vernal plaque: dense mucous deposit.

Management (4 marks)

Tier 1: cold compresses, lubricants, allergen avoidance. Tier 2: topical mast cell stabilisers (cromolyn, olopatadine), antihistamines. Tier 3: topical corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1%, short course; monitor IOP and lens). Tier 4: topical cyclosporine 1–2% (calcineurin inhibitor) for steroid-dependent cases. Shield ulcer: bandage soft contact lens, aggressive lubrication, topical antibiotic, remove mechanical trauma. Surgical debridement of papillae only if refractory.

Long Answer 3
Compare and contrast bacterial and viral conjunctivitis under the headings: aetiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and management.
10 Marks
Aetiology (2 marks)

Bacterial: acute — S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, H. aegyptius; hyperacute — N. gonorrhoeae (systemic urgency); chronic — S. aureus with blepharitis. Viral: EKC — Adenovirus 8, 19, 37; AHC — Enterovirus 70, Coxsackie A24; herpetic — HSV-1.

Clinical Features (3 marks)

Bacterial: purulent or mucopurulent discharge, lid sticking morning, papillary reaction (not follicles), no preauricular lymphadenopathy. Hyperacute: explosive onset under 24 hours, copious pus reaccumulating immediately after wiping, corneal involvement risk. Viral: watery discharge, follicular reaction, preauricular lymphadenopathy (EKC pathognomonic), subconjunctival haemorrhages (AHC). EKC produces pseudomembrane and later subepithelial infiltrates.

Diagnosis (2 marks)

Bacterial: conjunctival scraping for Gram stain and culture (mandatory in hyperacute/neonatal). Viral: clinical diagnosis in most cases; PCR for adenovirus if in doubt. Slit lamp distinguishes papillae from follicles.

Management (3 marks)

Acute bacterial: topical fluoroquinolone or aminoglycoside; self-limiting in 2–3 weeks. Hyperacute: systemic ceftriaxone + topical antibiotic + irrigation + hospitalisation. Viral: supportive (cold compress, lubricants); topical steroids cautiously for EKC SEIs; strict hygiene for infection control. Herpetic: topical aciclovir 3% or trifluridine; avoid steroid monotherapy.

Five questions from postgraduate and undergraduate entrance examinations with brief model answers.

AIIMS PG 2019
Trachoma is caused by Chlamydia trachomatis. Which serovars cause the blinding form?
AIIMS PG

Serovars A, B, Ba, and C cause blinding trachoma. Serovars D through K cause non-blinding genital infection (inclusion conjunctivitis). This distinction is a favourite factual question. The blinding serovars preferentially infect the conjunctival epithelium and drive the follicular-scarring-trichiasis sequence. Answer: A, B, Ba, C.

NEET PG 2022
Which of the following is the pathognomonic sign of epidemic keratoconjunctivitis?
NEET PG

Preauricular lymphadenopathy is pathognomonic of EKC. The options typically include pseudomembrane, subepithelial infiltrates, and subconjunctival haemorrhages — these may occur in EKC but are not pathognomonic. The preauricular node, by contrast, points specifically to EKC (adenovirus) among the viral conjunctivitides. Answer: Preauricular lymphadenopathy.

DNB 2021
A child with VKC develops a shield ulcer. What is the mechanism of formation?
DNB

Shield ulcer forms from two mechanisms acting together: mechanical abrasion of the upper central corneal epithelium by the giant papillae during blinking, and direct toxic damage from eosinophil-derived mediators (major basic protein, eosinophil cationic protein, and histamine) released by the allergic inflammatory response. The ulcer is initially sterile but at risk of secondary bacterial infection. Answer: Mechanical trauma from giant papillae combined with toxic allergic mediators from eosinophils.

PGI 2020
What does MacCallan's Stage TA (Trachomatous Scarring) signify?
PGI

TA (Trachomatous Scarring) represents the stage at which irreversible fibrous scarring has appeared on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. The characteristic findings are Arlt's line (a horizontal fibrous scar across the upper tarsus) and Herbert's pits at the limbus (rounded depressions representing healed limbal follicles). This stage is not active disease but marks permanent damage that will progress to trichiasis (TT) if the process continues. Answer: Irreversible fibrous scarring of upper tarsal conjunctiva; Arlt's line and Herbert's pits.

University Final MBBS
Describe the differences between pterygium and pinguecula under any four headings.
University

Four useful headings: (1) Composition — pterygium is fibrovascular tissue with abnormal elastin; pinguecula is hyalinised, avascular collagen degeneration. (2) Corneal invasion — pterygium crosses the limbus and grows over Bowman's layer; pinguecula stays on bulbar conjunctiva and never invades the cornea. (3) Visual consequences — pterygium causes induced astigmatism and may threaten the visual axis; pinguecula does not affect vision. (4) Treatment — pterygium requires surgical excision with adjunctive MMC if symptomatic; pinguecula is managed by observation and lubricants in most cases. Surgical removal of pinguecula is rarely performed.
