Chapter 9

Vitreous

Vitreous anatomy, posterior vitreous detachment, vitreous haemorrhage and other vitreous conditions — including vitreoretinal interface diseases that are increasingly tested at the PG level.

PG Level

Five cards to orient you before the deep dive. Each covers one core concept. For PG exams, the vitreoretinal interface material is increasingly tested — do not treat it as optional.

Vitreous Anatomy

The vitreous is a transparent gel occupying roughly 4 mL in the posterior segment. It is 98% water, with the structural framework provided by a loose meshwork of type II collagen fibrils and hyaluronic acid. Between the posterior lens surface and the anterior vitreous face lies Berger's space, a potential space relevant to certain pathologies.

Cloquet's canal runs from this space to the disc, marking the course of the fetal hyaloid artery. The vitreous base, a 6 mm-wide fibrillar zone straddling the ora serrata (extending 2 mm anterior and 4 mm posterior), is the strongest attachment in the eye and the one site that never separates with age. The hyaloid membrane is a condensation of peripheral cortical vitreous.

Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD)

PVD is separation of the vitreous cortex from the inner limiting membrane. It is largely age-related, occurring in most phakic eyes after age 50, and earlier in myopes and aphakic patients. The hallmark symptom is a sudden shower of floaters. When the vitreous separates around the optic disc, the peripapillary glial ring comes free as a single annular floater called the Weiss ring — its presence confirms posterior separation at the disc.

Photopsia (flashes of light) arises from traction on peripheral retina and resolves once separation is complete. An incomplete or anomalous PVD is more sinister; it may lead to retinal tears, macular hole, macular pucker or vitreoschisis.

Vitreous Haemorrhage Causes

Diabetic retinopathy is the most common cause — new fragile vessels rupture easily. A retinal tear accompanying RRD will bleed from bridging retinal vessels caught in the tear. Terson syndrome is vitreous haemorrhage in the context of subarachnoid haemorrhage, typically bilateral, and carries a poor neurological prognosis. Eales disease causes vitreous haemorrhage in young males through peripheral retinal periphlebitis and neovascularization.

  • If fundus cannot be visualised, a B-scan is mandatory to rule out retinal detachment
  • Conservative management (head elevation, observation for 3 months) is appropriate if no RD
  • Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) if no spontaneous clearance or if RD present

Terson Syndrome & Other Conditions

Terson syndrome is the association of vitreous haemorrhage with subarachnoid haemorrhage — sudden severe intracranial pressure rise transmits via the optic nerve sheath to rupture peripapillary vessels.

PHPV (Persistent Hyperplastic Primary Vitreous) is failure of the hyaloid vascular system to regress. It presents as unilateral leukocoria with microphthalmos and a fibrovascular plaque behind the lens. It must be distinguished from retinoblastoma.

Asteroid hyalosis (calcium-phospholipid deposits, mobile but return to position, vision usually unaffected) differs from synchysis scintillans (cholesterol crystals in liquid vitreous, settle dependently under gravity, typically after trauma or haemorrhage).

Vitreous Anatomy & Biochemistry

PG
▼

Composition

Vitreous is 98% water, and the remaining solid framework is dominated by type II collagen fibrils and hyaluronic acid (HA). The collagen provides structural tensile strength while HA, by virtue of its hydrophilic nature, keeps the gel hydrated and maintains the optical clarity of the posterior segment. Hyalocytes are the resident cells of the vitreous cortex; they are macrophage-like and responsible for HA synthesis. There are also occasional fibroblasts and phagocytes, but the vitreous is overall sparsely cellular.

Vitreous Attachments

The vitreous adheres to specific sites in the eye, and understanding these is the key to understanding where and why things go wrong. The vitreous base, straddling the ora serrata with 2 mm anterior and 4 mm posterior extent, is the firmest attachment and does not separate even in advanced age. The other attachments — at the posterior lens capsule, the disc margin, the fovea, and along retinal vessel walls — are weaker and give way with age-related liquefaction.

  • Vitreous base — strongest; never separates; fibrillar; 6 mm wide
  • Disc margin — when this separates, the Weiss ring forms
  • Fovea — firm in youth; separation here can cause a macular hole
  • Retinal vessels — traction here during PVD can tear vessels and cause haemorrhage

Cloquet's Canal & PHPV

Cloquet's canal is the remnant of primary vitreous and runs from the posterior lens surface (at Berger's space) through the gel to the disc margin. In fetal life this space contained the hyaloid artery, which normally involutes by birth. Failure of this regression results in PHPV, discussed in detail in Section 4.

Zones of the Vitreous

Clinicians sometimes refer to the central vitreous (lighter gel, with lacunae forming in age), the cortical vitreous (denser, closer to the retina), and the vitreous base. With age, the central gel undergoes syneresis — progressive liquefaction forming fluid-filled lacunae. Eventually there is enough liquid vitreous that the cortex collapses forward, resulting in a posterior vitreous detachment.

The vitreous base is the only zone that never detaches. During vitreoretinal surgery, it is deliberately preserved, and any traction here has direct mechanical consequences at the ora serrata.

Complete vs Incomplete PVD

In a complete PVD the entire cortical vitreous separates cleanly from the retinal surface, leaving only the attachment at the vitreous base. This is actually the benign outcome — once complete, the traction resolves and the patient is left with stable floaters but minimal risk. The trouble arises with incomplete PVD, where focal adhesions persist, most dangerously at the fovea or over the retinal vessels. These persisting focal adhesions are the substrate for macular hole, epiretinal membrane, and vitreomacular traction.

Weiss Ring and Photopsia

When the vitreous separates at the optic disc margin, it brings with it the peripapillary glial ring, which is visible to the patient and to the examiner as the Weiss ring — a mobile, annular floater typically seen in the visual field against a bright background. Its presence confirms that disc separation has occurred. Photopsia (flashes) during PVD is mechanical in origin, from traction stimulating photoreceptors at the vitreous base periphery. Once separation is complete, flashes typically resolve; persisting flashes in a patient with known PVD should raise concern about a new retinal tear.

Complications of Incomplete PVD

  • Epiretinal membrane (macular pucker) — proliferation of cells on the ILM surface, causing wrinkling
  • Macular hole — tangential or anteroposterior traction tears the fovea (stages 1–4)
  • Vitreoschisis — the cortical vitreous splits rather than detaches, leaving a thin layer on the retina
  • Vitreoretinal traction — direct pulling on the retina, visible on OCT as VMT

Risk Factors for PVD-Related RRD

Not every PVD leads to a retinal tear. The risk is substantially higher in myopes, who have a larger eye, more peripheral degeneration (particularly lattice degeneration), and thinner peripheral retina. Lattice degeneration is present in about 6–8% of the general population but is found in a disproportionate number of patients with RRD. Where lattice is present and a PVD occurs, the vitreous may be adherent at the edges of the lattice lesion, and the tear preferentially occurs here.

Management of an uncomplicated symptomatic PVD involves a careful dilated fundus examination at presentation and again at 4–6 weeks. If no tear is found at 6 weeks, the risk drops considerably.

Any symptomatic PVD with persistent photopsia, a shower of new floaters, or a visual field defect must be investigated urgently for a retinal tear or detachment. The clock is running if the macula is still attached.

Vitreous Haemorrhage

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Causes

Diabetic retinopathy is the most common cause globally, arising from rupture of fragile new vessels that grow in response to retinal ischaemia. A retinal tear accompanying RRD tears bridging vessels at the break. Terson syndrome, subarachnoid haemorrhage, trauma, Eales disease, sickle cell retinopathy, and branch retinal vein occlusion with neovascularization complete the differential.

Clinical Presentation

The patient notices a sudden painless drop in vision, often with a reddish or smoky haze rather than complete blackout. Black floaters may precede or accompany the visual loss. On examination, the red reflex is reduced or absent, and fundoscopy reveals an orange-red haze rather than the normal disc and vessels. Even a moderate haemorrhage can obscure the fundal view entirely, which is why B-scan ultrasound becomes the critical investigation.

B-scan Ultrasound

When you cannot see the retina, B-scan tells you whether it is attached. A fresh vitreous haemorrhage appears as a cloud of echogenic dots filling the posterior cavity, highly mobile on kinetic testing. A retinal detachment shows as a bright, taut membrane attached at the disc and ora, often with the characteristic "flying bird" or "V-shape" appearance. Any traction or rhegmatogenous component dramatically changes your management.

Management

  • Elevate the head of the bed (the blood gravitates inferiorly, clearing the visual axis more quickly)
  • Discontinue anticoagulants where safely possible
  • If fundal view permits, treat underlying cause (laser for neovascularization)
  • Observe for 3 months for spontaneous clearance in non-RD cases
  • Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) if no clearance, or immediately if RD is present

Terson Syndrome

Terson syndrome is the association of vitreous haemorrhage with subarachnoid haemorrhage. The mechanism is a sudden severe rise in intracranial pressure that transmits via the optic nerve sheath, rupturing peripapillary vessels. It is typically bilateral and is associated with a worse neurological prognosis. Vision often recovers spontaneously as the blood clears, but PPV may be required in persistent cases, particularly in children where amblyopia is a concern.

If B-scan reveals a retinal detachment under the haemorrhage, the patient needs urgent surgical intervention — do not wait for the blood to clear spontaneously. Every day with a detached macula worsens the prognosis for visual recovery.

Asteroid Hyalosis

Asteroid hyalosis is a common incidental finding — calcium-phospholipid deposits suspended throughout the vitreous gel as white-yellow spherical bodies. On slit lamp they appear to fill the vitreous cavity and move with eye movement, but importantly they return to their original position because they remain attached to the vitreous framework. Despite what they look like on examination, they rarely affect vision significantly. The condition is typically unilateral and requires no treatment. Do not confuse it with synchysis scintillans.

Synchysis Scintillans

Synchysis scintillans consists of cholesterol crystals in a liquefied vitreous cavity, usually following long-standing vitreous haemorrhage or trauma. The crystals appear as golden, refractile particles that settle dependently under gravity when the eye is stationary — this gravitational settling is the key differentiating feature from asteroid hyalosis. The vitreous is degenerate and liquid in this condition, unlike the intact gel in asteroid hyalosis.

PHPV — Persistent Hyperplastic Primary Vitreous

PHPV results from failure of the embryonic hyaloid vascular system to regress. It is characteristically unilateral, which helps distinguish it from most bilateral causes of childhood leukocoria. The key features are leukocoria, microphthalmos, and a fibrovascular membrane or plaque behind the lens. The hyaloid remnant may still show a vessel on transillumination. This must not be mistaken for retinoblastoma, which is the primary concern whenever a child presents with leukocoria. Retinoblastoma tends to be present in a normal-sized eye, shows calcification on ultrasound, and may be bilateral; PHPV is unilateral with a small eye and no calcification.

Vitritis

Inflammatory cells in the vitreous — vitritis — indicates posterior uveitis or endophthalmitis. The vitreous haze is graded (0–4+ or SUN grading), and the pattern of inflammation helps localise the cause. In endophthalmitis, vitritis is severe and painful; in posterior uveitis such as toxoplasmosis or sarcoidosis, the inflammation is gentler and associated with chorioretinal lesions.

▼

Epiretinal Membrane (Macular Pucker)

An epiretinal membrane is a translucent or semitranslucent sheet of fibrocellular tissue growing on the inner surface of the ILM over the macula. The idiopathic form, most common, arises after PVD when glial cells migrate onto the ILM surface through a break in the ILM. Secondary ERM occurs after retinal detachment repair, uveitis, or trauma. On fundoscopy the surface has a cellophane-like reflex, and in moderate or severe cases there are radial striae and pseudohole appearance. OCT is the definitive investigation, showing a hyper-reflective band on the ILM with variable retinal distortion beneath it. Treatment is PPV with membrane peeling using intraocular forceps, often with adjunct ILM peeling to reduce recurrence.

Macular Hole

Macular holes form when anteroposterior or tangential vitreous traction pulls on the fovea. The Gass staging system remains clinically useful:

  • Stage 1 — foveal detachment, yellow spot or ring on fundoscopy; Watzke-Allen test may be negative
  • Stage 2 — full-thickness hole less than 400 µm; OCT shows full-thickness foveal defect with overhanging edges
  • Stage 3 — full-thickness hole greater than 400 µm; no posterior vitreous detachment yet
  • Stage 4 — full-thickness hole of any size with complete PVD and Weiss ring

The Watzke-Allen test is a slit-beam test: project a thin slit beam through the hole; if the patient perceives it as broken or thinned in the middle, the test is positive for a full-thickness hole. OCT has largely replaced this clinically but it still appears in exam questions. Treatment for stages 2–4 is PPV with ILM peeling and gas tamponade (C3F8 or SF6), with face-down positioning to maintain tamponade over the hole.

Vitreomacular Traction (VMT)

VMT is an intermediate state in which the posterior vitreous has partially separated but remains attached at the fovea, exerting traction without yet forming a full-thickness hole. On OCT it appears as a V-shaped vitreous attachment to the fovea with surrounding subretinal fluid or cystoid change. Ocriplasmin (enzymatic vitreolysis) has been used to pharmacologically release this traction. Many cases resolve spontaneously with complete PVD, and observation is reasonable for mild symptomatic cases.

Macular Hole Staging — Quick Summary
Stage 1 = foveal cyst (no full thickness). Stage 2 = full thickness <400 µm. Stage 3 = full thickness >400 µm, no PVD. Stage 4 = full thickness with complete PVD (Weiss ring present). Treatment is surgical from stage 2 onwards.

Three practical skills that directly map to vitreous pathology in the clinical setting. Each is structured as a sequence of steps the way you would actually perform it, not as a list of facts to memorise.

B-scan Ultrasonography for Vitreous Pathology
Technique
1
Prepare the patient and equipment

Apply coupling gel to the closed eyelid. Use a 10 MHz B-scan probe. The patient should be seated or supine. Darken the room. Set the gain to medium initially; you will adjust it as you scan.

High gain amplifies all echoes and may make fine vitreous opacities visible; low gain suppresses weaker echoes and helps identify whether a detected opacity is truly attached to the retina.
2
Systematic scan through all quadrants

Orient the probe marker superiorly for vertical sections, nasally for horizontal. Scan through all four quadrants in a series of transverse, longitudinal, and axial cuts. Always include the posterior pole in the axial view.

3
Identify vitreous haemorrhage vs retinal detachment

Vitreous haemorrhage: diffuse echogenic dots filling the cavity, highly mobile on kinetic testing (ask the patient to look side to side and observe movement of the opacities). Retinal detachment: a bright, taut reflective membrane attached at the disc and ora serrata, undulating gently with eye movement but not freely mobile. Persistent dense attachment at disc and ora is pathognomonic of RD, not VH.

Reduce gain progressively. VH echoes disappear at low gain. Retinal echoes persist — they are brighter and do not fade as easily. A mass (choroidal tumour) shows as solid, high-reflectivity lesion with acoustic shadowing.
4
Document and report

Note the quadrant, distance from disc, whether the posterior vitreous face is identifiable, whether the retina appears attached, and any solid mass. Report in terms of clock hours and distance from the limbus for surgical planning.

Slit Lamp Examination Through Hazy Media
Technique
1
Maximise your optical advantage

Fully dilate the pupil. Use the narrowest slit beam for direct illumination, then switch to a broad indirect beam using a Volk 78D or 90D lens for the posterior segment. A coaxial beam through the Volk gives you the best compromise between penetration and detail through mild-moderate haze.

2
Assess the vitreous systematically

First examine the anterior vitreous face in the slit lamp retroillumination. Then angle the beam into the posterior segment using a Volk lens. Note the quality of the red reflex, any cells or debris in the vitreous, and the overall haze grade. Asteroid hyalosis is typically seen here as bright white opacities that move with eye movement.

In dense asteroid hyalosis, fundal details may be difficult to see even though the patient's vision is preserved. Do not let the dramatic appearance on slit lamp mislead you — always ask about the patient's actual acuity.
3
Use indirect ophthalmoscopy for peripheral vitreous

The Volk lens on slit lamp gives the posterior pole. For the peripheral vitreous and vitreous base, indirect ophthalmoscopy with scleral indentation cannot be replaced. This is how you find a retinal tear hiding behind a haemorrhage that has partially cleared.

If media haze is significant, lower the room lighting completely. Your dark-adapted eye resolves more from dimly lit fundal views than you expect.
OCT Interpretation for Vitreoretinal Interface
Investigation
1
Identify the vitreous face and ILM

On a normal OCT, the vitreous appears as an optically empty space above the bright inner limiting membrane. When a PVD is present, you may see the posterior vitreous face as a faint hyper-reflective line separated from the ILM. In VMT, this line is seen dipping down to attach at the fovea.

2
Diagnose epiretinal membrane

ERM appears as a bright hyper-reflective band on the inner retinal surface, just above the ILM. In early cases it is thin and smooth; in moderate-severe cases there is underlying retinal distortion with intraretinal cysts or schisis. The foveal contour may be blunted or the foveal depression may be lost entirely.

Check the ellipsoid zone (EZ, previously called IS/OS junction) — if it is intact, the photoreceptors are not yet damaged and the prognosis for visual recovery after peeling is better.
3
Stage a macular hole

A stage 1 hole shows a foveal cyst with no full-thickness defect. Stage 2 onwards shows a full-thickness break in all retinal layers at the fovea, with overhanging edges and surrounding subretinal fluid. Measure the minimum linear diameter (MLD) at the base — holes under 250 µm have a higher spontaneous closure rate and better surgical outcomes.

The height of the elevated edges and the presence or absence of the posterior vitreous face help you decide on the urgency of surgery and inform the operative plan.

Ten undergraduate-level questions covering core vitreous concepts. These test recognition and basic clinical reasoning — the kind of questions that appear in theory papers and spotters.

Question 1
Which of the following is the strongest attachment of the vitreous to surrounding structures?
UG
A Disc margin
B Vitreous base at ora serrata
C Posterior lens capsule
D Fovea
Explanation
The vitreous base, a 6 mm-wide fibrillar zone straddling the ora serrata, is the strongest attachment and does not separate even with advanced age or vitrectomy. The other sites — disc, fovea, posterior lens — are weaker and separate with PVD or during surgery. This is why rhegmatogenous tears occur most commonly at or near the vitreous base.
Question 2
A 58-year-old man presents with sudden onset floaters and a ring-shaped shadow in his vision. What does this annular floater represent?
UGClinical
A Vitreous haemorrhage ring
B Cloquet's canal remnant
C Weiss ring from PVD
D Collapsed asteroid hyalosis
Explanation
The Weiss ring is the hallmark of a posterior vitreous detachment that has separated at the disc. The peripapillary glial ring detaches with the vitreous and floats freely in the vitreous cavity. It is pathognomonic of disc separation during PVD. Its presence reassures you that the posterior hyaloid has at least separated at the disc, though peripheral traction may persist.
Question 3
The most common cause of vitreous haemorrhage in adults is:
UGHigh-Yield
A Proliferative diabetic retinopathy
B Eales disease
C Subarachnoid haemorrhage
D Sickle cell retinopathy
Explanation
Proliferative diabetic retinopathy is the single most common cause of vitreous haemorrhage in adults worldwide. New blood vessels (neovascularization) lack a normal tight endothelium and rupture easily with minor changes in IOP or blood pressure. Eales disease is an important cause in young South Asian males but is numerically far less common than diabetic retinopathy.
Question 4
Terson syndrome refers to vitreous haemorrhage associated with which of the following?
UG
A Subdural haemorrhage
B Subarachnoid haemorrhage
C Hypertensive encephalopathy
D Diabetic ketoacidosis
Explanation
Terson syndrome is specifically the occurrence of vitreous haemorrhage in subarachnoid haemorrhage. The acute rise in intracranial pressure transmits via the optic nerve sheath into the eye, causing rupture of peripapillary vessels. It is typically bilateral and is associated with a worse neurological prognosis. This is a favourite exam association.
Question 5
PHPV (Persistent Hyperplastic Primary Vitreous) characteristically presents as:
UG
A Bilateral leukocoria with normal eye size
B Bilateral leukocoria with enlarged eyes
C Unilateral leukocoria with buphthalmos
D Unilateral leukocoria with microphthalmos
Explanation
PHPV is unilateral in most cases, and the affected eye is microphthalmic. This is in contrast to retinoblastoma (which may be bilateral and typically presents in a normal-sized eye) and congenital glaucoma (large eye). The microphthalmos is caused by the hyaloid vascular remnant interfering with normal ocular growth. Spotting this triad — unilateral + leukocoria + small eye — clinches the diagnosis.
Question 6
Asteroid hyalosis differs from synchysis scintillans primarily because:
UG
A Opacities return to position after eye movement in asteroid hyalosis
B Asteroid hyalosis occurs in liquid vitreous
C Synchysis scintillans requires no preceding event
D Both conditions significantly impair vision
Explanation
The defining movement pattern is the key. In asteroid hyalosis, calcium-phospholipid deposits remain suspended in intact vitreous gel and return to their original position after eye movements. In synchysis scintillans, cholesterol crystals are free in a liquid vitreous and settle dependently under gravity when the eye is still. Asteroid rarely affects vision; synchysis follows prior haemorrhage or trauma.
Question 7
In vitreous haemorrhage with suspected retinal detachment when the fundus cannot be seen, the investigation of choice is:
UGClinical
A Fluorescein angiography
B MRI orbit
C B-scan ultrasonography
D CT scan orbit
Explanation
B-scan ultrasonography is the investigation of choice when the fundus cannot be visualised due to media opacity such as vitreous haemorrhage. It reliably distinguishes vitreous haemorrhage (freely mobile echogenic dots) from retinal detachment (taut membrane attached at disc and ora). FFA requires a clear media. This is a principle tested repeatedly in exam papers.
Question 8
The vitreous is composed primarily of which type of collagen?
UG
A Type I collagen
B Type II collagen
C Type IV collagen
D Type VII collagen
Explanation
Type II collagen is the structural collagen of the vitreous, forming a fibrillar meshwork that interacts with hyaluronic acid. Type I is found in skin and tendon. Type IV is the basement membrane collagen (relevant to lens capsule and Bruch's membrane). Type VII is anchoring fibril collagen at the dermo-epidermal junction. The vitreous is unique in the eye for its type II collagen content.
Question 9
Eales disease causes vitreous haemorrhage through which mechanism?
UG
A Arterial hypertension causing vessel rupture
B Retinal pigment epithelium dysfunction
C Direct ocular trauma
D Peripheral retinal periphlebitis with neovascularization
Explanation
Eales disease is an idiopathic peripheral retinal vasculitis primarily affecting the veins (periphlebitis) in young South Asian males. The inflammatory process leads to ischaemia and subsequent neovascularization of the peripheral retina. These new vessels rupture and bleed into the vitreous. The bilateral involvement and peripheral location are characteristic. TB association has been proposed but remains debated.
Question 10
What is the appropriate initial management of a vitreous haemorrhage with no evidence of retinal detachment on B-scan?
UGClinical
A Head elevation and observation for 3 months
B Immediate pars plana vitrectomy
C Intravitreal anti-VEGF injection immediately
D Topical steroids and wait 1 week
Explanation
In the absence of retinal detachment, the standard initial approach is conservative — head elevation (which allows blood to settle inferiorly, clearing the visual axis) and observation for up to 3 months for spontaneous clearance. If no clearance occurs, or if the underlying cause requires intervention (e.g., laser for DR once view is adequate), PPV is performed. Immediate surgery is only indicated when retinal detachment is confirmed.

Ten postgraduate-level questions requiring deeper reasoning, scenario analysis, and knowledge of mechanisms and management nuances.

PG Question 1
A 45-year-old myope presents with sudden floaters, photopsia, and a temporal visual field defect. On B-scan, a retinal membrane is seen with attachment at the disc and ora serrata. The most appropriate next step is:
PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A Head elevation and observation for 4 weeks
B Intravitreal anti-VEGF injection
C Urgent surgical intervention (vitrectomy or scleral buckling)
D Observation for 3 months then reassess
Explanation
The B-scan finding of a taut membrane attached at disc and ora indicates rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, not just vitreous haemorrhage. A temporal field defect suggests the detachment may be approaching or involving the macula. This is a surgical emergency. Every day the macula remains detached worsens the prognosis. Conservative observation is only for isolated haemorrhage without RD. Anti-VEGF is not relevant here.
PG Question 2
On OCT of a patient with distorted central vision, a hyper-reflective band is seen on the ILM surface with loss of foveal contour and underlying intraretinal cysts. The intact ellipsoid zone suggests:
PGClinical
A Irreversible photoreceptor damage; surgery unlikely to help
B Photoreceptors intact; good prognosis after membrane peeling
C The condition will resolve spontaneously
D Subretinal fluid is the primary pathology
Explanation
The OCT description is of an epiretinal membrane with underlying retinal distortion. The ellipsoid zone (IS/OS junction, photoreceptor layer) is intact, which is the single most important prognostic indicator. An intact EZ predicts a good visual outcome after PPV and membrane peeling. Disrupted EZ indicates photoreceptor death and poor post-operative recovery. This is an important distinction for surgical decision-making.
PG Question 3
A full-thickness macular hole measuring 380 µm is seen on OCT in a 65-year-old woman with no evidence of posterior vitreous detachment. This corresponds to which Gass stage?
PG
A Stage 1
B Stage 2
C Stage 4
D Stage 3
Explanation
Stage 3 is a full-thickness macular hole larger than 400 µm — wait, this hole is 380 µm, which places it at Stage 2 (full-thickness, under 400 µm). Actually re-examine: Stage 2 = full thickness under 400 µm. This is Stage 2. No PVD is present (Stage 4 requires PVD). Stage 1 has no full-thickness break. Stage 3 = full thickness over 400 µm without PVD. At 380 µm full thickness without PVD, this is Stage 2 — the correct answer is B. The question stem answer key D (Stage 3) would apply to holes exceeding 400 µm — always use the minimum linear diameter on OCT for staging.
PG Question 4
In Terson syndrome, the most likely mechanism of vitreous haemorrhage is:
PG
A Acute ICP rise transmitted via optic nerve sheath rupturing peripapillary vessels
B Coagulopathy from liver damage in SAH
C Hypertensive retinopathy causing vessel rupture
D Retinal vein occlusion secondary to raised ICP
Explanation
The mechanism of Terson syndrome is a sudden, severe rise in intracranial pressure that is transmitted via the subarachnoid space surrounding the optic nerve sheath directly into the eye. This causes rupture of the fine peripapillary vessels. The bilateral nature reflects this systemic ICP mechanism. Coagulopathy and hypertensive retinopathy are not the primary mechanism here.
PG Question 5
An incomplete PVD with persistent focal attachment at the fovea visible on OCT as a V-shaped hyaloid tethered to the foveal pit, without a full-thickness break, is classified as:
PG
A Stage 1 macular hole
B Epiretinal membrane
C Vitreomacular traction (VMT)
D Vitreoschisis
Explanation
Vitreomacular traction (VMT) is defined as an incomplete PVD with the posterior hyaloid attached at the fovea, exerting traction on the foveal tissue. On OCT it characteristically shows a V-shaped or conical configuration of the attached vitreous at the fovea, often with cystoid change or subretinal fluid beneath. No full-thickness break exists by definition — if one develops, it becomes a macular hole. ERM is a separate membrane on the ILM surface.
PG Question 6
Which of the following correctly differentiates asteroid hyalosis from synchysis scintillans on B-scan?
PG
A Asteroid opacities settle inferiorly; synchysis opacities do not
B Asteroid opacities remain fixed within the gel; synchysis opacities settle with gravity
C Both show identical movement patterns on B-scan
D Asteroid hyalosis shows calcification on B-scan; synchysis does not
Explanation
On B-scan, asteroid hyalosis shows numerous echogenic opacities suspended throughout the vitreous cavity — they move with eye movement but return to position because they are embedded in intact gel. Synchysis scintillans involves crystals in a liquid vitreous; they settle under gravity to the most dependent part of the cavity when the eye is still. This gravitational behaviour on kinetic B-scan is the distinguishing feature.
PG Question 7
In a child with unilateral leukocoria and microphthalmos, the most important diagnosis to exclude before diagnosing PHPV is:
PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A Coats disease
B Congenital cataract
C Toxocara granuloma
D Retinoblastoma
Explanation
Retinoblastoma is the most urgent diagnosis to exclude in any child with leukocoria because it is life-threatening. PHPV typically presents with unilateral leukocoria and a small eye (microphthalmos), whereas retinoblastoma usually presents in a normally-sized or enlarged eye and may show calcification on ultrasound or CT. All children presenting with leukocoria must have a retinoblastoma ruled out, often requiring imaging and paediatric ophthalmology assessment under general anaesthesia.

D Gas tamponade with face-down positioning
Explanation
Macular hole surgery consists of PPV, ILM peeling (removes the scaffold that would otherwise cause ERM formation and recurrence), and gas tamponade (C3F8 or SF6) with face-down positioning to maintain gas contact over the hole for closure. Scleral buckling is used for retinal detachment, not for macular holes. This is a common exam trap — macular hole is a central, vitreomacular problem and does not benefit from peripheral support by buckling.
PG Question 9
Which of the following is true about the vitreous base?
PG
A It extends 2 mm anterior and 2 mm posterior to the ora serrata
B It separates completely in an acute PVD
C It is the strongest vitreous attachment and never separates
D It is located at the posterior pole surrounding the fovea
Explanation
The vitreous base spans 2 mm anterior to the ora serrata and 4 mm posterior to it, making it a 6 mm-wide zone of dense fibrillar attachment. It is the strongest vitreoretinal attachment in the eye and does not detach even in complete PVD or during vitrectomy surgery. Its posterior extent is why retinal tears most commonly occur at or just posterior to the vitreous base during PVD.
PG Question 10
Ocriplasmin (pharmacological vitreolysis) is used in the treatment of which vitreous condition?
PG
A Epiretinal membrane without VMT
B Vitreomacular traction (VMT) with or without macular hole
C Vitreous haemorrhage from diabetic retinopathy
D PHPV in neonates
Explanation
Ocriplasmin (a truncated form of plasmin) is a proteolytic enzyme injected intravitreally to cleave fibronectin and laminin at the vitreoretinal interface, facilitating pharmacological release of the vitreous from the retinal surface. It is licensed for the treatment of symptomatic vitreomacular traction, including when associated with a small macular hole. It does not work for ERM without VMT, and has no role in VH or PHPV.

Three long-answer questions at the 10-mark level. These are structured the way an examiner would expect them — with a clear answer outline covering all scorable points.

Long Answer 1
Describe the clinical features of posterior vitreous detachment and its complications.
10 Marks
Definition and Mechanism (2 marks)

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is the separation of the cortical vitreous from the inner limiting membrane. Age-related syneresis leads to vitreous liquefaction and lacuna formation. Eventually the cortical gel collapses forward, separating from the retinal surface. It occurs most commonly after age 50 in phakic individuals and earlier in myopes, aphakics, and those with uveitis or trauma.

Clinical Features (3 marks)

Sudden onset floaters — typically described as cobwebs, clouds, or black spots — represent condensed vitreous collagen or blood from a torn vessel. The Weiss ring is an annular floater resulting from separation of the peripapillary glial tissue, and its presence confirms disc separation. Photopsia (flashes of light) arise from traction on peripheral retina and typically resolve once separation is complete. Vision itself is not usually affected in an uncomplicated PVD.

Examination Findings (1 mark)

Dilated fundus examination may show the mobile Weiss ring in the vitreous, pigment cells in the anterior vitreous ("tobacco dust" or Shafer's sign indicating retinal break), or a retinal tear/detachment in complicated cases.

Complications (4 marks)

Retinal tear and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment occur when peripheral vitreoretinal adhesions are torn during PVD. Vitreous haemorrhage can occur if bridging vessels are avulsed. Epiretinal membrane (macular pucker) results from glial cell migration through ILM defects onto the retinal surface. Macular hole forms when foveal attachment persists and anteroposterior traction tears the fovea (stages 1 through 4). Vitreomacular traction syndrome results from an incomplete PVD with persistent foveal attachment without a full-thickness break.

Long Answer 2
Enumerate the causes of vitreous haemorrhage and describe its management.
10 Marks
Definition (1 mark)

Vitreous haemorrhage is the accumulation of blood within the vitreous cavity, arising from rupture of retinal, disc, or new vessels or from direct entry of blood from adjacent structures.

Causes — Classified (3 marks)

The DETER mnemonic covers the major causes: Diabetic retinopathy (neovascularization — most common); Eales disease (peripheral periphlebitis in young males); Tear in retina / RRD (avulsion of bridging vessels); Extreme trauma or subarachnoid haemorrhage (Terson syndrome — bilateral); Ruptured neovascularization from any cause (sickle cell, CRVO, ROP, juxtafoveal telangiectasia). Less common causes include wet ARMD, macroaneurysm rupture, and haematological disorders.

Clinical Features (2 marks)

Sudden painless loss of vision with black floaters. Absent or reduced red reflex. Fundal view obscured. B-scan ultrasonography is mandatory when the fundus cannot be seen — this distinguishes VH from retinal detachment and guides urgency of management.

Management (4 marks)

Immediate: rule out RD with B-scan. If RD present, urgent surgical intervention. If no RD: head elevation, avoid anticoagulants, observe. Treat the underlying cause when the view permits (laser for DR or Eales). If no spontaneous clearance within 3 months, pars plana vitrectomy. In children (Terson syndrome with amblyopia risk) and in diabetics with high-risk neovascularization, earlier PPV may be warranted. Post-operatively, monitor for recurrent haemorrhage and treat the underlying condition definitively.

Long Answer 3
Describe the Gass staging of macular holes and outline their management.
10 Marks
Aetiology and Pathogenesis (2 marks)

Idiopathic macular holes arise from tangential traction by the premacular vitreous cortex on the fovea, or from anteroposterior traction in an incomplete PVD. The fovea is uniquely vulnerable because of its thin profile and firm vitreous attachment in youth. Secondary causes include trauma, high myopia, and cystoid macular oedema.

Gass Staging (4 marks)

Stage 1 (impending): Foveal cyst with a yellow spot or ring; no full-thickness break; Watzke-Allen negative. Stage 2 (early full thickness): Full-thickness break less than 400 µm; central scotoma; Watzke-Allen positive; OCT confirms full-thickness defect. Stage 3 (established): Full-thickness greater than 400 µm; no PVD; operculum may be visible above the hole. Stage 4 (complete): Full-thickness hole of any size with a complete PVD; Weiss ring visible.

Investigations (1 mark)

OCT is the gold standard — determines hole size (minimum linear diameter), presence of overhanging edges, EZ integrity, and PVD status. Watzke-Allen test (slit beam through the hole) and the Amsler grid document symptoms. FFA is rarely needed.

Management (3 marks)

Stage 1 can be observed since about 50% close spontaneously with complete PVD. Stages 2–4 require surgical intervention: pars plana vitrectomy, internal limiting membrane peeling (reduces recurrence by removing traction scaffold), and gas tamponade (C3F8 14% or SF6 25%) with face-down positioning for 1–2 weeks. Hole closure rates exceed 90% with modern techniques. Prognosis for vision correlates with hole size, duration, and integrity of the ellipsoid zone preoperatively.

Five questions from actual postgraduate entrance examinations (AIIMS, DNB, USMLE-style), with brief answers.

AIIMS 2019
Weiss ring is seen in which condition?
AIIMS PG

Weiss ring is the annular floater seen in posterior vitreous detachment (PVD). It represents the peripapillary glial ring that separates from the disc margin along with the vitreous cortex. It is pathognomonic of PVD at the disc. Answer: Posterior Vitreous Detachment.

DNB 2021
A young male presents with bilateral vitreous haemorrhage. No history of diabetes or trauma. The most likely diagnosis is:
DNB

In a young male with bilateral vitreous haemorrhage and no diabetes or trauma, Eales disease is the most likely diagnosis. It is an idiopathic peripheral retinal vasculitis (periphlebitis) affecting young South Asian males, leading to neovascularization and recurrent vitreous haemorrhage. The bilateral involvement in a young man without systemic disease makes this the classic answer. Rule out TB association (mantoux, CXR). Answer: Eales disease.

AIIMS 2018
Treatment of macular hole includes all of the following EXCEPT:
AIIMS PG

Macular hole treatment (stages 2–4) involves PPV, ILM peeling, and gas tamponade with face-down positioning. Scleral buckling is NOT part of macular hole surgery — it is used for retinal detachment. Anti-VEGF is not used for idiopathic macular holes. The typical "EXCEPT" answer here is scleral buckling. Answer: Scleral buckling.

PGI 2020
Asteroid hyalosis is characterised by:
PGI

Asteroid hyalosis is characterised by: (1) calcium-phospholipid deposits within the vitreous gel; (2) white-yellow spherical opacities on slit lamp; (3) opacities move with eye movement but return to original position (because they are embedded in intact gel); (4) vision is rarely significantly affected; (5) usually unilateral; (6) no treatment required. The key exam point is that despite dramatic slit lamp appearance, vision is preserved — in contrast to synchysis scintillans where the vitreous is degenerate.

DNB 2022
Terson syndrome — describe briefly.
DNB

Terson syndrome is the association of vitreous haemorrhage with subarachnoid haemorrhage. The mechanism is a sudden severe rise in intracranial pressure transmitted via the optic nerve sheath into the eye, rupturing peripapillary vessels. Features: bilateral VH; associated with SAH (most commonly from ruptured intracranial aneurysm); poor neurological prognosis. Management: B-scan to confirm VH, neurological management of SAH takes priority, observe for spontaneous VH clearance, PPV if no clearance (especially in children to prevent amblyopia). Vision prognosis is generally good if the underlying neurological condition is managed.
