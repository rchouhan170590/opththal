Chapter 9

The Vitreous

Anatomy, posterior vitreous detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, endophthalmitis, PHPV — critical for retinal pathology understanding.

UG Level PG Level

Vitreous Anatomy & Composition

UG
▼
Key Fact: The vitreous is a gel-like, avascular structure comprising 99% water, with collagen type II and hyaluronic acid as structural components.

Composition

  • 99% water: Maintains transparency; avascular
  • Collagen (type II): Main structural protein; provides gel structure
  • Hyaluronic acid (hyaluronate): Holds water; gel-forming glycosaminoglycan
  • Other molecules: Proteins, glucose, ions, cells (scavenger cells, occasional white blood cells)

Vitreous Structure

  • Base (pars plana region): Firmly attached to internal limiting membrane (ILM)
  • Body: Loose attachment to retina except at optic disc and blood vessels
  • Posterior: Attached via cortical vitreous; less adherent with age
  • Vitreous condensations: Cloquet's canal (remnant of hyaloid artery) — clinically normal finding

Vitreous Volume & Size

  • Volume: ~4 mL (fills posterior segment)
  • Accounts for ~80% of eye volume
  • Transparent, colourless; supports retinal position

Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD)

UGPG
▼

Definition & Pathophysiology

  • PVD: Separation of the posterior vitreous cortex from the internal limiting membrane (ILM)
  • Mechanism: Vitreous collagen/HA degeneration with age; tractional separation due to liquefaction and syneresis
  • Incidence: ~75% by age 75 years
  • Risk factors: Age, myopia, trauma, inflammation, previous eye surgery

Clinical Symptoms

  • Flashes (photopsia): Due to vitreous traction on retina; brief, peripheral, lightning-like
  • Floaters: Sudden onset of new floaters (vitreous opacities visible); may be extensive
  • Visual symptoms: Sudden increase in floaters is RED FLAG for RD or vitreous haemorrhage

On Examination

  • Weiss ring: Annular opacity at posterior pole; glial condensation around optic disc — pathognomonic for PVD
  • Vitreous liquefaction: Seen on slit lamp as fluid (Tyndall-like effect in vitreous)
  • Fundus findings: May see optic nerve head elevation; no other findings if uncomplicated

Associations & Complications

  • Retinal breaks: PVD traction → retinal tears (horseshoe, atrophic holes); ~10–15% have breaks
  • Retinal detachment (RD): PVD is major risk factor for RD; fluid seeps through break
  • Vitreous haemorrhage: If break damages retinal vessel; obscures fundus view

NEW FLASHES + FLOATERS = Eye Emergency (R/O RD)

Vitreous Haemorrhage (VH)

UGPG
▼

Causes

  • Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR): Most common; neovascularization bleeds easily
  • Retinal detachment: Breaks damage vessels
  • Ocular trauma: Blunt/penetrating injury to retinal vessels or neovascular membrane
  • Terson syndrome: VH secondary to intracranial haemorrhage (subarachnoid, epidural) — rare
  • Other: Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), retinitis, bleeding disorders

Clinical Presentation

  • Sudden vision loss: Severity depends on volume and location of blood
  • Floaters & "shower": Red/brown particles in vitreous
  • Absent red reflex: If extensive; fundus details obscured
  • Pain: Absent unless secondary glaucoma develops

Diagnosis

  • Clinical examination: Loss of red reflex; difficulty visualizing retina
  • B-scan ultrasonography: CRUCIAL if dense VH obscures retina; detects RD, mass, posterior pathology
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT): Limited use if media opaque; may assess if clearing occurs

Management

  • Observation: Many small VH resolve spontaneously over weeks to months (resorption)
  • Anti-VEGF: Intravitreal bevacizumab/ranibizumab speeds resolution if due to PDR/neovascular causes
  • Vitrectomy: Indicated if:
    • VH persists >3–4 months (dense opacity)
    • Vision-threatening (light perception or worse)
    • Associated RD or other retinal pathology requiring visualisation
    • Secondary glaucoma developing
  • Treat underlying cause: Laser PRP for PDR; other retinal lesion management

Persistent Hyperplastic Primary Vitreous (PHPV)

PG
▼

Definition & Classification

  • PHPV: Non-regressing hyaloid vasculature in infants; results from failed regression of primitive vitreous
  • Anterior: Persistent pupillary membrane, elongated ciliary processes, cataract, iris hypoplasia
  • Posterior: Retrolental fibrous membrane, persistent hyaloid artery, hypoplastic macula, optic nerve hypoplasia
  • Combined: Features of both anterior and posterior

Clinical Features

  • Presentation: Unilateral (most common; 90%); rarely bilateral
  • White pupil (leukocoria): Retrolental fibrous tissue appears white behind lens
  • Microphthalmia: Small eye due to failed anterior segment growth
  • Prominent hyaloid vessels: May be visible as vascular strands in vitreous
  • Cataract: Variable; may be posterior polar or total
  • Retinal detachment: Traction RD due to fibrous membrane

Diagnosis

  • Clinical: Unilateral white pupil; microphthalmia; prominent vessels
  • B-scan ultrasound: Dense retrolental echoes; traction RD if present
  • Differential: Must exclude retinoblastoma (bilateral PHPV rare; RB more common bilateral)

Management

  • Anterior PHPV: Largely cosmetic; manage cataract if visually significant
  • Posterior PHPV: Poor visual prognosis; may require vitrectomy if traction RD develops
  • Prevent phthisis: Monitor for secondary glaucoma, neovascularization

Bacterial & Fungal Endophthalmitis

PG
▼

Definition & Classification

  • Endophthalmitis: Intraocular inflammation/infection; serious sight-threatening emergency
  • Post-surgical: Most common; follows cataract surgery, vitrectomy, glaucoma surgery, IOL implantation
  • Post-traumatic: Following open globe injury; penetrating trauma increases risk
  • Endogenous: Haematogenous spread from systemic infection; less common in developed countries

Microbiology

  • Post-surgical (most common organisms):
    • Staphylococcus epidermidis: Most common (coagulase-negative staph); often low virulence
    • Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Propionibacterium acnes
  • Post-traumatic:
    • Bacillus cereus: HIGH risk; fulminant course; poor prognosis
    • Clostridium, gram-negatives (Pseudomonas)
  • Endogenous:
    • Candida: Most common fungal; in immunocompromised (diabetes, HIV, prolonged antibiotics)
    • Aspergillus, Cryptococcus (rare in developed countries)

Clinical Presentation

  • Acute endophthalmitis: Onset days to weeks post-op/trauma; severe pain, photophobia, vision loss
  • Vitritis: White appearance, dense infiltrate; Tyndall effect
  • Anterior chamber: Hypopyon (WBC sediment); fibrin
  • Retina: May be obscured; focal infiltrates may be seen if media clear enough
  • Chronic endophthalmitis: Insidious; indolent course (e.g., P. acnes); low-grade inflammation months to years

Diagnosis

  • Clinical: High suspicion based on timing, pain, vision loss post-op
  • Culture: Vitreous tap/biopsy (PPV vitrectomy); aqueous tap if needed; Gram stain, culture
  • PCR: Rapid detection of pathogens if available

Treatment

  • Intravitreal antibiotics: Empiric broad-spectrum pending culture:
    • Vancomycin (gram-positive, especially MRSA) + Ceftazidime (gram-negative)
    • OR: Meropenem alone (covers most organisms)
  • Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS) trial guidance:
    • Vitrectomy indicated: Vision <light perception; dense vitritis; gram-positive cocci/rods suspected (Bacillus)
    • Vitreous tap alone: Vision ≥light perception; gram-negative (if sensitivity known); no concerns about organism virulence
  • Topical antibiotics: Frequent dosing (fortified drops)
  • Systemic antibiotics: If systemic infection suspected; poor vitreous penetration with IV alone
  • Corticosteroids: May reduce inflammation; controversial timing (some avoid initially if bacteria suspected)
  • Second injection: Repeat intravitreal antibiotics at 48–72 hours if no improvement
️ Bacillus cereus: Post-traumatic infection; fulminant course; very poor prognosis (often light perception or worse); requires aggressive treatment, often vitrectomy.

MCQ Bank — Vitreous

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
Vitreous is composed primarily of:
A99% water, collagen type II, hyaluronic acid
B70% protein, 30% water
CLipids and cholesterol crystals
DPrimarily cells and fibrin
Correct Answer: A — 99% water, collagen type II, hyaluronic acid
The vitreous is an avascular gel with 99% water content. Collagen type II and hyaluronic acid are the main structural macromolecules maintaining gel structure. Avascular nature allows transparency.
Q2 UG
A 60-year-old patient reports sudden onset of multiple floaters and occasional flashes in the peripheral vision. On examination, an annular opacity is seen at the posterior pole. What is the diagnosis?
ARetinal detachment
BPosterior vitreous detachment (PVD) with Weiss ring
CEndophthalmitis
DAsteroid hyalosis
Correct Answer: B — Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) with Weiss ring
Weiss ring is pathognomonic for PVD — an annular opacity at the posterior pole representing glial condensation around the optic disc. Flashes + floaters are classic PVD symptoms. Requires dilated exam to look for retinal breaks (RD risk ~10–15%).
Q3 PG
A 35-year-old diabetic patient presents with sudden vision loss and floaters. Examination shows loss of red reflex and inability to visualize the fundus. Which investigation is MOST useful in this scenario?
AMRI orbit
BOptical coherence tomography (OCT)
CB-scan ultrasonography
DComputed tomography (CT) orbit
Correct Answer: C — B-scan ultrasonography
Dense vitreous haemorrhage obscures fundus view. B-scan ultrasound penetrates opaque media and can detect retinal detachment, intraocular masses, and posterior pathology. OCT is limited if media is opaque. MRI/CT not needed for diagnostic purpose.
Q4 UG
Most common cause of vitreous haemorrhage in developed countries:
AProliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)
BBranch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO)
COcular trauma
DTerson syndrome
Correct Answer: A — Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)
PDR with neovascularization accounts for majority of VH cases. Neovascular vessels bleed easily. Other causes: RD, trauma, BRVO, Terson syndrome. High prevalence of diabetes makes PDR the most common.
Q5 PG
Most common organism causing post-surgical endophthalmitis:
AStaphylococcus aureus
BPseudomonas aeruginosa
CStaphylococcus epidermidis
DBacillus cereus
Correct Answer: C — Staphylococcus epidermidis
S. epidermidis (coagulase-negative staph) is most common post-surgical organism; skin flora. Often low virulence but requires treatment. Bacillus cereus is most common in post-traumatic cases with poor prognosis.
Q6 PG
According to the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS), vitrectomy is MOST indicated in post-operative endophthalmitis when:
AAny bacteria isolated
BVision <light perception OR gram-positive cocci/rods suspected (e.g., Bacillus)
CAll cases of hypopyon present
DOnly if cultures positive for gram-negatives
Correct Answer: B — Vision <light perception OR gram-positive cocci/rods suspected (e.g., Bacillus)
EVS: Vitrectomy indicated if vision worse than light perception, or if high-virulence organism (Bacillus, gram-positive rods) is suspected. Tap alone is reasonable if vision ≥light perception and organism low-virulence (S. epidermidis, gram-negatives).
Q7 UG
Asteroid hyalosis is characterized by:
AMobile iridescent cholesterol crystals
BWhite starlike opacities; usually benign; minimal visual impact
CRed-brown blood in vitreous
DLiquefied vitreous with mobile particles
Correct Answer: B — White starlike opacities; usually benign; minimal visual impact
Asteroid hyalosis: calcium oxalate/phosphate crystals on collagen strands creating "starfield" appearance. Benign; incidental finding. Synchysis scintillans (cholesterol): mobile, more symptomatic. Neither require treatment usually.
Q8 PG
A newborn presents with unilateral microphthalmia, white pupil (leukocoria), and visible vascular strands in the vitreous. What is the diagnosis?
ARetinoblastoma
BPersistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (PHPV)
CCongenital cataract
DToxoplasmosis
Correct Answer: B — Persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (PHPV)
PHPV presents with unilateral (90%) white pupil, microphthalmia, and prominent hyaloid vessels. Failed regression of primitive vitreous. Retinoblastoma is usually unilateral but less commonly bilateral; PHPV typically unilateral. B-scan helps exclude RB.
Q9 PG
Post-traumatic endophthalmitis due to Bacillus cereus is concerning because:
AIt is resistant to vancomycin
BIt causes minimal inflammation
CIt has fulminant course with poor visual prognosis (often light perception or worse)
DIt only affects the vitreous, sparing retina
Correct Answer: C — It has fulminant course with poor visual prognosis (often light perception or worse)
Bacillus cereus is a gram-positive rod; highly virulent. Post-traumatic infection with Bacillus is associated with rapid progression, extensive necrosis, and poor outcomes despite aggressive treatment. Requires immediate vitrectomy.
Q10 UG
Cloquet's canal in the vitreous represents:
ARemnant of the hyaloid artery; normal anatomical finding
BPathological liquefaction zone
CBlood vessel indicating vitreous haemorrhage
DScar tissue from old inflammation
Correct Answer: A — Remnant of the hyaloid artery; normal anatomical finding
Cloquet's canal (also called Cloquet's tunic) is the remnant of the fetal hyaloid artery which normally regresses before birth. Visible on slit lamp or during vitrectomy; clinically insignificant; normal anatomical variant.

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Describe PVD, its symptoms, signs, and clinical significance. Discuss the management of post-PVD retinal break. (8 marks)
PVD = separation of posterior vitreous cortex from ILM. Symptoms: flashes, floaters. Signs: Weiss ring. Risk: 10–15% have retinal breaks; RD risk. Management: dilated exam for breaks; laser/cryo for breaks; observation if no breaks; regular follow-up.

Vitreous Composition

  • 99% water; avascular
  • Collagen type II (structural)
  • Hyaluronic acid (water-holding)
  • Volume: ~4 mL; fills posterior cavity

PVD Clinical Pearl

  • Symptoms: Flashes + new floaters = RED FLAG
  • Weiss ring: Pathognomonic; glial condensation at disc
  • Incidence: ~75% by age 75
  • Risk: 10–15% have breaks; need dilated exam

Endophthalmitis Organisms

  • Post-surgical: S. epidermidis (most common)
  • Post-traumatic: Bacillus cereus (HIGH risk; poor prognosis)
  • Endogenous: Candida (immunocompromised)

EVS Trial Guidance

  • Vitrectomy: Vision <LP OR suspected Bacillus/gram-positive rods
  • Tap alone: Vision ≥LP + low-virulence organism (S. epidermidis, gram-negatives)
  • Antibiotics: Vancomycin + ceftazidime (empiric)
