Chapter 14

Lacrimal System & Disorders

Anatomy, dry eye, epiphora, dacryocystitis, DCR, lacrimal tumours — investigation and surgical management.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Lacrimal System Anatomy

UG
▼

Lacrimal Gland

  • Anatomy: Orbital lobe (main, ~80% tears) + palpebral lobe (smaller, ~20%)
  • Innervation: Parasympathetic secretomotor: CN VII (facial) → greater petrosal nerve → pterygopalatine ganglion → zygomatic branch → lacrimal nerve → gland
  • Tear secretion: Continuous (basal) + reflex (emotion, irritation)

Lacrimal Drainage System

  • Puncta lacrimal ia: Upper (~0.5 mm) + lower (~0.5 mm) on lid margin at medial canthus
  • Canaliculi: Upper (~8 mm horizontal) + lower (~9 mm horizontal); superior and inferior unite to form common canaliculus
  • Lacrimal sac: Dilated portion; ~12 mm height; mucocutaneous junction
  • Nasolacrimal duct (NLD): ~12 mm; runs along nasal floor; opens into inferior meatus of nose via plica lacrimalis (Hasner's valve)

Tear Film Composition

  • Lipid layer (outermost): Meibomian gland secretion; prevents evaporation
  • Aqueous layer (middle): Lacrimal gland + accessory lacrimal glands; IgA, lysozyme, lactoferrin (antibacterial)
  • Mucous layer (innermost): Conjunctival goblet cells; adheres to epithelium

Dry Eye Disease (TFOS DEWS II Classification)

UGPG
▼

Classification by Mechanism

  • Aqueous-deficient:
    • Sjögren's syndrome: Autoimmune; anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La antibodies; dry eye + dry mouth; female predominance
    • Non-Sjögren's: Systemic disease (RA, SLE, graft-vs-host disease), lacrimal gland damage (radiation, chemotherapy), congenital absence
  • Evaporative:
    • Meibomian gland disease (MGD): Dysfunction → lipid layer deficiency → increased evaporation (MOST COMMON cause of dry eye)
    • Ocular surface disease: Atopy, blepharitis, chemical burn, Stevens-Johnson syndrome

Investigations

  • TBUT (Tear Break-Up Time): Normal >10 sec; <10 sec = abnormal (evaporative defect)
  • Schirmer's test I (without anaesthetic): Measures reflex + basal secretion; normal >15 mm in 5 min
  • Schirmer's test II (with anaesthetic): Measures basal secretion only; normal >5 mm in 5 min (aqueous-deficient if <5)
  • Lissamine green / Rose Bengal: Vital dye staining; highlights damaged epithelial cells
  • Tear osmolarity: >305 mOsm/L = abnormal
  • MMP-9 test: Marker of ocular surface inflammation

Management (Stepwise)

  • First-line: Artificial tears (preservative-free), lid hygiene, warm compress (MGD)
  • Aqueous-deficient: Cyclosporine A 0.05% drops (T-cell inhibitor; increases goblet cell density)
  • Evaporative: IPL (intense pulsed light) + meibomian gland expression, LipiFlow (thermal treatment)
  • Punctal plugs: Temporary or permanent; blocks tear drainage, increases tear retention

Epiphora — Causes & Investigation

UGPG
▼

Two Main Categories

  • Hypersecretion (excess tear production): Reflex tearing from irritation (dry eye paradoxically, foreign body, atopy); less common
  • Drainage failure (obstruction/dysfunction): NLD stenosis, canalicular obstruction, lid malposition (entropion, ectropion); common

Congenital NLD Obstruction (Most Common Cause of Epiphora in Infants)

  • Pathology: Incompletely canalized NLD; Hasner's valve doesn't open at birth; 95% resolve by age 12 months
  • Presentation: Epiphora, mucoid discharge; usually unilateral; presents in first 4–8 weeks
  • Treatment:Crigler's massage — downward pressure over medial canthus/lacrimal sac (3–5 times daily); unclogs duct. Probing at 9–12 months if no resolution. Punctal plug if needed for comfort

Diagnostic Tests

  • Syringing and probing: Saline syringe into lacrimal punctum; if flows into nasal cavity = patent NLD; resistance/reflux = obstruction
  • ROPLAS test: Rose Bengal or Lissamine green instilled; appears in nose within 5 min = patent; delayed/absent = obstruction
  • Dacryocystography/CT scan: For complex anatomy

Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) — Indications & Technique

PG
▼

Indications

  • Chronic dacryocystitis (recurrent infections)
  • NLD stenosis/obstruction (confirmed by syringing or ROPLAS)
  • Symptomatic epiphora (watering eye not responding to conservative treatment)

Principle

  • Creates artificial fistula between lacrimal sac and nasal cavity (inferior meatus)
  • Bypasses obstructed NLD
  • Success rate: 90–95% (lower if canalicular obstruction)

Approaches

  • External DCR: Incision over lacrimal sac; easier to learn; traditional; more postoperative swelling; scar
  • Endoscopic (endonasal) DCR: Endoscope through nose; no facial scar; better cosmetics; requires specialized equipment and training; growing preference

Outcome

  • Success: Epiphora resolves; patent ostium maintained
  • Complications: Scarring/stenosis of ostium (5–10%), infection, residual epiphora (canalicular disease)

Lacrimal Gland Tumours

PG
▼

Benign Tumours

  • Pleomorphic adenoma (mixed tumour) — MOST COMMON benign lacrimal tumour
    • Location: Lateral third of upper eyelid (from orbital lobe)
    • Presentation: S-shaped ptosis (ptosis with lateral mass); painless; slow-growing; firm
    • Treatment: Surgical excision (superficial or total parotidectomy-style approach with intact capsule)
    • Risk: Malignant transformation (~2–5%); handle carefully to avoid rupture

Malignant Tumours

  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) — MOST COMMON malignant lacrimal tumour
    • Aggressive; perineural invasion (spreads along nerves)
    • Prognosis: Poor; 5-year survival ~20%; high recurrence
    • Treatment: Radical excision (orbit, globe if needed) + adjuvant radiotherapy
  • Lacrimal carcinoma (ductal origin): Less common; similar aggressive behavior
Key Distinction: Pleomorphic adenoma = painless, slow-growing, benign (but with malignant potential). ACC = aggressive, painful, poor prognosis, early perineural spread.

MCQ Bank — Lacrimal System

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
A 6-week-old infant presents with unilateral epiphora and mild mucoid discharge from the left eye. Syringing shows no patency into the nose. What is the diagnosis and first-line management?
AAcute dacryocystitis; IV antibiotics and urgent DCR
BCongenital NLD obstruction; Crigler's massage and observation until age 12 months
CCanalicular obstruction; lacrimal probing at 2 months
DDacryolith; immediate DCR
Correct Answer: B — Congenital NLD obstruction; Crigler's massage
Congenital NLD obstruction is most common cause of epiphora in infants. 95% resolve by 12 months with Crigler's massage (downward pressure over lacrimal sac). Probing reserved for age 9–12 months if no resolution.
Q2 UG
A 45-year-old woman with Sjögren's syndrome presents with dry eye. Which test directly measures the aqueous secretion component?
ATBUT (tear break-up time)
BSchirmer I test (without anesthetic)
CSchirmer II test (with anesthetic)
DLissamine green staining
Correct Answer: C — Schirmer II test
Schirmer II (with anesthetic) measures basal tear secretion only (aqueous layer). Normal >5 mm in 5 min. Schirmer I measures both reflex + basal. Sjögren's = low basal secretion.
Q3 PG
A 55-year-old woman develops acute pain, swelling, and purulent discharge at the medial canthus with fever. Diagnosis: acute dacryocystitis. What is the appropriate emergency management?
AImmediate incision and drainage of the abscess
BWarm compress, topical antibiotics, and analgesics only
CIV antibiotics (cephalosporin or fluoroquinolone), warm compress, analgesics; DCR after acute phase resolves
DImmediate DCR to relieve obstruction
Correct Answer: C — IV antibiotics + warm compress; DCR later
Acute dacryocystitis is a medical emergency. Do NOT perform I&C (risk of fistula). IV antibiotics immediately. Warm compress and analgesics. Once acute inflammation resolves (4–6 weeks), proceed with elective DCR.
Q4 UG
The parasympathetic innervation of the lacrimal gland follows which pathway?
ACN V (trigeminal) → zygomatic nerve → lacrimal gland
BCN VII (facial) → greater petrosal nerve → pterygopalatine ganglion → lacrimal nerve
CCN III (oculomotor) → ciliary ganglion → lacrimal gland
DCN IX (glossopharyngeal) → otic ganglion
Correct Answer: B — CN VII pathway
Lacrimal secretion is controlled by CN VII parasympathetic fibres: CN VII → greater petrosal nerve → pterygopalatine ganglion → zygomatic/lacrimal branches → lacrimal gland.
Q5 PG
A 60-year-old presents with painless swelling at the lateral aspect of the upper eyelid with S-shaped ptosis. Imaging reveals a mass in the orbital lobe of the lacrimal gland. What is the most likely diagnosis and treatment?
AAdenoid cystic carcinoma; radical orbital exenteration
BPleomorphic adenoma; careful surgical excision with intact capsule
CSjögren's syndrome; immunosuppression
DSarcoid; systemic steroids
Correct Answer: B — Pleomorphic adenoma
Painless, slow-growing orbital mass with S-shaped ptosis = pleomorphic adenoma (most common benign lacrimal tumour). Handle carefully to avoid rupture (malignant transformation risk). ACC would be painful and aggressive.
Q6 UG
An adult with chronic bilateral dry eye has TBUT <5 sec and Schirmer II test showing 3 mm. What is the primary defect?
AEvaporative defect due to meibomian gland dysfunction
BAqueous-deficient dry eye with reduced lacrimal secretion
CReflex hypersecretion causing epiphora
DCorneal ulcer secondary to trauma
Correct Answer: B — Aqueous-deficient
Schirmer II test (basal secretion) = 3 mm (<5 mm = abnormal) = aqueous-deficient dry eye. Low TBUT might be secondary. Suggests lacrimal gland disease (Sjögren's, non-Sjögren's) or damage.
Q7 PG
A 50-year-old woman with chronic nasolacrimal duct stenosis unresponsive to conservative therapy is referred for DCR. Which statement about DCR is INCORRECT?
ADCR creates an artificial fistula between lacrimal sac and nasal cavity
BEndoscopic DCR has a lower success rate than external DCR
CSuccess rate is 90–95% if NLD is obstructed; lower if canalicular obstruction
DEndoscopic DCR avoids facial scar and has better cosmetics
Correct Answer: B — False (Endo-DCR has comparable success to external)
Endoscopic DCR has comparable or better success rates (90–95%) compared to external DCR. It's less traumatic, better cosmetics. Statement B is INCORRECT — hence the answer.
Q8 UG
Which lacrimal gland tumour is known for perineural invasion and aggressive behaviour with poor prognosis?
APleomorphic adenoma
BOncocytoma
CAdenoid cystic carcinoma
DLymphoma
Correct Answer: C — Adenoid cystic carcinoma
ACC is the most common malignant lacrimal tumour. Hallmark = perineural invasion (spreads along nerves). Aggressive; poor prognosis (5-year survival ~20%). Requires radical surgery + radiotherapy.
Q9 PG
In the TFOS DEWS II classification, what is the most common CAUSE of dry eye disease globally?
AMeibomian gland dysfunction (evaporative)
BSjögren's syndrome (aqueous-deficient)
CCorneal dystrophy
DStevens-Johnson syndrome
Correct Answer: A — Meibomian gland dysfunction
MGD is the most common cause of dry eye (~60% of cases), leading to evaporative defect. Sjögren's is important but less common. TFOS DEWS II emphasizes MGD as primary target for management.
Q10 UG
The tear film consists of three layers. Which layer is secreted by the lacrimal gland?
ALipid layer (outermost)
BAqueous layer (middle) — contains IgA, lysozyme
CMucous layer (innermost)
DAll three layers equally
Correct Answer: B — Aqueous layer
Lacrimal gland secretes the aqueous (middle) layer, which contains immune proteins (IgA, lysozyme, lactoferrin). Lipid = meibomian glands. Mucous = conjunctival goblet cells.

Discuss the anatomy of the lacrimal drainage system. Describe the pathophysiology and management of congenital NLD obstruction. (5 marks)
Anatomy: puncta → canaliculi → lacrimal sac → NLD → nose. Congenital: incompletely canalized NLD; Hasner's valve unopened; Crigler's massage downward pressure over sac (9–12 months); probing if persists.

Lacrimal Anatomy & Function

  • Lacrimal gland: Orbital + palpebral lobes; CN VII innervation
  • Drainage: Puncta → canaliculi → sac → NLD → nose
  • Tear film: Lipid (meibomian) + aqueous (lacrimal) + mucous (conjunctiva)

Dry Eye (TFOS DEWS II)

  • Aqueous-deficient: Sjögren's, non-Sjögren's; Schirmer II <5 mm
  • Evaporative: MGD (most common); TBUT <10 sec
  • Treatment: Artificial tears, cyclosporine, IPL, punctal plugs

Epiphora & NLD Obstruction

  • Congenital NLD: Infant epiphora; Crigler's massage; probe at 9–12 mo if persists
  • Acquired NLD stenosis: Syringing, ROPLAS test positive
  • DCR: Creates fistula between sac and nose; success 90–95%

Dacryocystitis & Tumours

  • Acute: Painful, purulent; IV antibiotics + warm compress; NO I&C (fistula risk); DCR after 4–6 weeks
  • Chronic: Painless, recurrent; ROPLAS positive; consider DCR
  • Benign: Pleomorphic adenoma (S-shaped ptosis, slow-growing)
  • Malignant: ACC (perineural invasion, poor prognosis)
