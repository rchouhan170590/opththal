Chapter 14

Lacrimal System

Tear film, dry eye syndrome, dacryocystitis, lacrimal drainage anatomy, and DCR — with clinical assessment methods and practical management.

UG PG

The lacrimal system is examined clinically more often than it appears in textbooks. These cards cover the anatomy, the investigations, the pathology, and the surgery in a format built for rapid revision.

Tear Film Structure

The tear film has three layers, each from a different source, and each with a distinct function.

  • Mucin layer (innermost) — secreted by conjunctival goblet cells; converts the hydrophobic corneal epithelium to a hydrophilic surface, allowing the aqueous layer to spread and adhere
  • Aqueous layer (middle) — secreted by the lacrimal gland; accounts for 98% of tear film thickness; contains lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA, and growth factors
  • Lipid layer (outermost) — secreted by Meibomian glands; retards evaporation; stabilises the tear film between blinks

TBUT (tear break-up time) below 10 seconds indicates an unstable tear film. Schirmer's I below 10 mm in 5 minutes indicates deficient aqueous production.

High-Yield

Dry Eye Classification

  • Aqueous deficient dry eye — Sjögren's syndrome (keratoconjunctivitis sicca + xerostomia + autoimmune disease; anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies); Non-Sjögren's (lacrimal gland disease, reflex secretion loss, drugs — antihistamines, antidepressants)
  • Evaporative dry eye — Meibomian gland dysfunction (most common overall cause), reduced blink rate (screen use), lagophthalmos, contact lens wear, post-LASIK, vitamin A deficiency

DEWS II (Dry Eye Workshop II) grading: mild (symptoms without signs), moderate (symptoms with signs), severe (debilitating symptoms with significant corneal involvement). In practice these overlap, and most patients have a mixed picture.

High-Yield

Acute Dacryocystitis

Acute dacryocystitis is one of those conditions where getting the anatomy right changes the diagnosis. The swelling is below and medial to the medial palpebral ligament — at the lacrimal sac fossa. Swelling at the medial canthus above the ligament suggests preseptal cellulitis or a different diagnosis entirely.

  • Causative organisms: Staphylococcus aureus most common; Streptococcus pneumoniae; Haemophilus influenzae in children
  • Regurgitation test: pressure over the lacrimal sac region forces mucopus through the punctum — confirms NLDO
  • Management: systemic antibiotics; warm compresses; I&D if fluctuant; DCR after complete resolution (not in the acute phase)
High-Yield

DCR Methods

  • External DCR — gold standard; curvilinear skin incision 3 mm medial to the medial canthus; bony ostium (12 mm x 12 mm) created through the lacrimal bone into nasal cavity; anterior and posterior flaps of sac and nasal mucosa anastomosed; silicone stenting; success rate 90 to 95%
  • Endonasal (endoscopic) DCR — same principle via nasal endoscope; no external scar; success 85 to 90%; requires skilled endonasal surgeon; preferred when there is concurrent nasal pathology to address
  • Laser DCR (PANDO-L) — lower success rates (60 to 70%); less tissue damage; useful in anticoagulated patients
  • Probing and syringing — for congenital NLDO in children; performed under GA at 12 to 18 months

Lacrimal Drainage Anatomy — Mnemonic

Tears drain from the puncta through a series of structures before reaching the nasal cavity. The pathway and key dimensions are tested repeatedly in examinations.

  • Puncta — 0.5 mm openings at the medial end of each lid; surrounded by a fibrovascular ring (ampulla); upper and lower puncta are normally applied to the globe (eversion = ectropion)
  • Canaliculi — upper and lower; each has a 2 mm vertical segment then an 8 mm horizontal segment; they join to form the common canaliculus
  • Common canaliculus — joins the lacrimal sac at the valve of Rosenmuller (prevents reflux)
  • Lacrimal sac — 12 to 15 mm long; lies in the lacrimal fossa between the anterior and posterior lacrimal crests; the medial canthal tendon crosses anteriorly
  • Nasolacrimal duct — 18 mm long; descends in a bony canal in the maxilla; opens into the inferior meatus
  • Valve of Hasner — mucosal fold at the lower end of the NLD where it opens into the inferior meatus; fails to open in 30% of neonates causing congenital NLDO

Lacrimal System Anatomy

UGPG
▼

Secretory System

Tear production comes from two sources: the main lacrimal gland and the accessory lacrimal glands. The main lacrimal gland sits in the lacrimal fossa in the superolateral orbit, divided by the lateral horn of the levator aponeurosis into a larger orbital lobe and a smaller palpebral lobe. It is a serous gland whose 10 to 15 ducts open into the superior conjunctival fornix. It is responsible for reflex tearing — the secretion triggered by emotional stimuli, corneal irritation, or the trigeminolacrimal reflex (V1 stimulation reflexively activating lacrimal secretion via the greater petrosal nerve and pterygopalatine ganglion).

The accessory lacrimal glands are responsible for basal tear secretion — the background, unstimulated production that keeps the ocular surface continuously moist. The glands of Krause (42 to 48 in the upper fornix, 6 to 8 in the lower) and Wolfring (2 to 5 at the upper border of the upper tarsus, 2 at the lower border of the lower tarsus) are histologically identical to the main gland. This distinction between basal (accessory glands) and reflex (main gland) secretion is important when interpreting Schirmer's tests — Schirmer's I (without anaesthetic) tests both; Schirmer's II (with anaesthetic to abolish reflex) tests basal secretion only.

Tear Film Composition

The aqueous layer of the tear film contains water (98%), electrolytes, glucose, urea, dissolved oxygen, and a rich array of proteins. Lysozyme is one of the most abundant tear proteins and has antibacterial properties by cleaving the peptidoglycan of Gram-positive bacteria. Lactoferrin chelates iron, depriving bacteria of a necessary growth factor. Secretory IgA provides immunological defence. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) and other growth factors promote corneal epithelial healing. Tear film osmolarity is approximately 302 mOsm/L in normal eyes; hyperosmolarity (above 308 mOsm/L) is a biomarker of dry eye and contributes to ocular surface inflammation by activating stress response pathways in epithelial cells.

Drainage Anatomy in Surgical Detail

The puncta (0.5 mm in diameter) sit at the medial end of each lid margin, surrounded by a fibrovascular ring (the ampulla) that keeps them patent. They drain into the canaliculi, which have a 2 mm vertical segment before turning medially for an 8 mm horizontal segment. The two canaliculi join in 90% of people to form a common canaliculus (5 to 8 mm) before entering the lacrimal sac — in 10% they enter separately. The junction of the common canaliculus with the sac is guarded by the valve of Rosenmuller, which prevents reflux of sac contents into the canaliculi.

The lacrimal sac (12 to 15 mm long) occupies the lacrimal fossa, bounded by the anterior and posterior lacrimal crests. The medial canthal tendon attaches to both crests and crosses anterior to the sac — this is why swelling of the sac displaces the tendon forward, producing the characteristic swelling that is visible and palpable below the tendon. The angular vein runs just anterior to the sac and must be ligated during external DCR. The nasolacrimal duct (18 mm) descends in a bony canal within the maxilla, passing lateral to and behind the inferior turbinate to open into the inferior meatus at the valve of Hasner.

Dry Eye Syndrome

UGPG
▼

Prevalence and Risk Factors

Dry eye is among the most common conditions seen in any ophthalmic practice. It affects roughly 5 to 35% of the population depending on the diagnostic criteria used, with prevalence increasing with age, female sex, contact lens wear, screen use (blink rate falls from 18 to 4 blinks per minute during concentrated screen work), post-LASIK state, and a range of systemic medications including antihistamines, antidepressants, diuretics, beta-blockers, and isotretinoin.

Symptoms and Signs

Patients describe burning, stinging, grittiness, and a foreign body sensation. A paradoxical epiphora (watering despite a dry eye) is classic — the unstable, hyperosmolar tear film reflexly stimulates reflex secretion from the main lacrimal gland, producing intermittent tearing that coexists with the chronic dryness. Symptoms are typically worse in low humidity environments, in air conditioning, toward the end of the day, or during screen use.

Clinical signs include reduced TBUT (tear film breaks up in less than 10 seconds, seen as dark patches in the fluorescein-stained tear film under blue light), corneal punctate epithelial erosions staining with fluorescein (inferior one-third distribution in aqueous deficiency; interpalpebral distribution in evaporative dry eye), reduced Schirmer's values, and meibomian gland orifice capping or irregular posterior lid margin. Rose Bengal or lissamine green stain dead and devitalised cells in the interpalpebral conjunctiva.

Sjögren's Syndrome

Sjögren's syndrome is an autoimmune exocrinopathy that destroys lacrimal and salivary gland tissue through lymphocytic infiltration. Primary Sjögren's presents with keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eye) and xerostomia (dry mouth) without an associated connective tissue disease. Secondary Sjögren's occurs in the context of rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, systemic sclerosis, primary biliary cirrhosis, or other autoimmune diseases. Serology: anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies are positive in 70 to 75% of Sjögren's patients and anti-La (SSB) in 50 to 60%. The ANA is positive in most. Definitive diagnosis has traditionally required labial salivary gland biopsy (minor salivary gland biopsy from the inner lip) showing focal periductal lymphocytic sialadenitis with a focus score of 1 or more (one or more foci of 50 or more lymphocytes per 4 mm2).

Investigations

Schirmer's test I: a strip of Whatman no. 41 filter paper (35 mm x 5 mm) is folded at the 5 mm mark and placed in the lower fornix at the junction of the middle and outer thirds. After 5 minutes, the length of wetted paper is measured. Without anaesthesia, normal wetting is above 10 mm. Values below 5 mm are definitely abnormal; 5 to 10 mm is borderline. Schirmer's test II (basic secretion test, BST) is performed with topical anaesthetic in place — this abolishes reflex tearing, leaving only basal secretion. Normal BST wetting is above 10 mm in 5 minutes; below 5 mm indicates severely deficient basal secretion.

TBUT is measured by instilling fluorescein, asking the patient to blink three times and then keep the eyes open, while the examiner watches the tear film under blue light for the first dry spot to appear. The average of three measurements is recorded; below 10 seconds is abnormal, below 5 seconds is severely abnormal. Meibography (infrared imaging of Meibomian gland structure) documents gland drop-out and is increasingly used to grade MGD severity. Tear osmolarity measurement (TearLab) above 308 mOsm/L supports the diagnosis.

Management

Management is stepwise based on severity. For mild disease, lifestyle modification — regular breaks from screens, humidified environments, adequate fluid intake, omega-3 supplementation — combined with preservative-free artificial tear substitutes (carboxymethylcellulose, sodium hyaluronate, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose). The viscosity of the artificial tear is chosen based on symptom severity: watery drops for mild, gel drops for moderate, ointments (petroleum-based) for severe and nocturnal use.

Lid hygiene (warm compresses and lid scrubs) is the treatment for the MGD component. Topical cyclosporine 0.05% (Restasis) or 0.1% (Ikervis) is an immunomodulator that reduces T-cell-mediated inflammation of the lacrimal gland — it is particularly effective in inflammatory dry eye and Sjögren's and takes 3 to 6 months to reach full effect. Punctal plugs (collagen — temporary; silicone — permanent) block tear drainage at the punctum, retaining tears on the ocular surface; they are effective but can cause epiphora if oversized, and they are contraindicated if there is concurrent ocular surface inflammation (retained inflammatory mediators worsen the condition). Autologous serum drops (20 to 50% concentration) provide growth factors, fibronectin, and vitamin A that recombinant artificial tears cannot — they are reserved for severe cases and post-LASIK neurotrophic dry eye. For Sjögren's, hydroxychloroquine may help systemic symptoms but has limited direct effect on the ocular surface.

Paradoxical epiphora: A patient who says "my eyes water all the time" may actually have dry eye. The reflex tearing from the main lacrimal gland is triggered by the hyperosmolar, unstable tear film irritating the corneal surface. A TBUT measurement resolves the question — if TBUT is low and Schirmer's is low, you are dealing with dry eye regardless of the watering.

Dacryocystitis

UGPG
▼

Acute Dacryocystitis

Acute dacryocystitis results from bacterial infection of a lacrimal sac that has been obstructed — usually by a nasolacrimal duct obstruction that has allowed stasis and bacterial overgrowth. The presentation is dramatic: sudden onset of painful, red, tender swelling in the medial canthal region, specifically below the medial palpebral ligament at the lacrimal sac fossa. This anatomical point matters — swelling above the ligament at the inner canthus suggests dacryoadenitis or canaliculitis, not dacryocystitis. Pressure over the sac produces regurgitation of mucopus at the punctum (regurgitation test positive), confirming that the sac is full. The common organisms are Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae.

The swelling may organise into a lacrimal abscess — fluctuant, pointing toward the skin surface. If untreated, the abscess may point and drain spontaneously (creating a fistula to skin) or, more dangerously, rupture posteriorly through the orbital septum causing orbital cellulitis — a vision and life-threatening complication. Management: systemic antibiotics as the priority (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily, or cephalexin, or in penicillin allergy, clindamycin). Hot fomentation several times daily helps localise and point the abscess. Syringing and probing are strictly contraindicated in the acute phase. When the abscess is fluctuant, incision and drainage through the skin gives rapid relief. After the infection has fully resolved — typically 4 to 6 weeks — DCR is performed to establish permanent drainage.

Chronic Dacryocystitis

Chronic dacryocystitis is a more insidious presentation: the patient notices persistent painless watering of one eye with intermittent episodes of mucopus. There is no acute swelling or tenderness. The regurgitation test is positive — gentle pressure over the lacrimal sac (below the medial canthal tendon) produces mucopus or clear mucus at the punctum. Syringing demonstrates obstruction: the irrigating fluid cannot pass into the nose but regurgitates from the opposite punctum. The aetiology is nasolacrimal duct obstruction from stones (dacryoliths), nasal polyps, rhinosinusitis, or idiopathic fibrosis of the duct. The definitive treatment is DCR.

A mucocoele develops when the sac is completely obstructed and the secretions continue to accumulate, gradually distending the sac into a large cystic swelling below the medial canthal tendon. It is not acutely infected, tends to be non-tender, and transilluminates. Treatment is DCR; aspiration alone leads to rapid recurrence.

Never perform probing or syringing in acute dacryocystitis. Forcing fluid through an infected, obstructed, and inflamed sac risks bacteraemia, spreading the infection into the orbit, and damaging the already-inflamed canalicular mucosa. Wait until the acute phase has resolved completely before any instrumentation.

Congenital Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction

UGPG
▼

Pathophysiology and Epidemiology

At birth, the lower end of the nasolacrimal duct — the valve of Hasner — is covered by a thin mucous membrane that normally ruptures at or shortly after birth with the onset of nasal breathing. In approximately 30% of neonates, this membrane persists and the NLD remains obstructed. The vast majority (95%) resolve spontaneously by 12 months of age as the duct canalize and the membrane ruptures. This high rate of spontaneous resolution is the reason why conservative management is first-line: the parents can be counselled honestly that in most cases nothing further will need to be done.

Presentation

The key clinical point is distinguishing congenital NLDO from neonatal conjunctivitis. In NLDO: epiphora (watering) and mucopus begin in the first few weeks of life; the conjunctiva is white — there is no injection; pressing on the lacrimal sac produces a reflux of mucopus at the punctum. In neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum): the conjunctiva is red; there is chemosis; gonococcal infection (24 to 48 hours after birth) or chlamydial infection (5 to 14 days after birth) are the classic causes; this is a medical emergency as gonococcal keratitis can perforate. The absence of conjunctival injection in NLDO is the critical distinguishing feature.

Management

Conservative management (0 to 12 months): Crigler's massage — the parent places the tip of the index finger at the medial canthus below the tendon and applies firm downward pressure with a pumping motion 10 times, four times daily. The aim is to build enough hydrostatic pressure within the sac to rupture the membranous obstruction at Hasner's valve. Parents must be taught the correct technique — the finger needs to be below the tendon to compress the sac, not above it where it compresses nothing useful.

Probing under GA (12 to 18 months if not resolved): a Bowman's probe is passed through the upper or lower canaliculus, through the common canaliculus, into the sac, and down the NLD until the resistance of the membrane is felt and then overcome with gentle pressure. A characteristic give is felt when the membrane ruptures. The NLD is then irrigated to confirm patency. Success rates for a single probing are 90 to 95% if done before 18 months; rates decline with age and after 3 years they fall substantially. If the first probing fails, a second can be attempted or silicone intubation (Crawford tubes) can be performed — a silicone rod and tube is passed through both canaliculi, down the NLD, and retrieved from the nose, where it is tied to maintain the lumen open for 3 to 6 months. If intubation fails, formal DCR (external or endonasal) is performed, generally not before age 3 to 4 years when the nasal anatomy is sufficiently developed.

Counselling parents: The most anxiety-provoking aspect for parents is the mucopus — it looks like an infection but it is not. The absence of conjunctival redness is reassuring. Explain the 95% spontaneous resolution rate clearly. A mother who understands this will be much more adherent to the massage protocol and much less distressed.

Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR)

UGPG
▼

Indications

DCR creates a new drainage pathway from the lacrimal sac directly into the nasal cavity (middle meatus), bypassing the obstructed NLD. Indications include chronic NLDO with epiphora or dacryocystitis, mucocoele of the lacrimal sac, recurrent acute dacryocystitis, and as an adjunct to manage lacrimal sac tumours. It is not indicated for purely canalicular obstruction — that requires a different bypass procedure (Jones tube). DCR is performed after any acute infection has completely resolved.

External DCR

External DCR is the gold standard with the highest success rates (90 to 95%). A curvilinear skin incision is made 3 mm medial to the inner canthus, directly over the anterior lacrimal crest. The angular vessels (angular vein and artery) are identified and ligated or cauterised. The periosteum is elevated from the lacrimal bone and the bony ostium — typically 12 mm x 12 mm — is created using a drill or osteotome. The bone at this site is thin (the lacrimal bone is the thinnest bone in the skull) and little force is needed. The lacrimal sac is then opened with a vertical incision to create anterior and posterior flaps. The nasal mucosa is similarly incised to create corresponding flaps. The posterior flaps are sutured together first, then the anterior flaps, creating a wide anastomosis between sac and nasal mucosa. A Crawford silicone tube is threaded through both canaliculi, down through the new anastomosis, and retrieved from the nose, where it is tied and left for 3 to 6 months. The skin incision is closed in layers.

Endonasal DCR

Endonasal (endoscopic) DCR achieves the same anastomosis from inside the nose, using a nasal endoscope. No external incision is made, so there is no scar. The nasal mucosa overlying the lacrimal bone is incised and elevated, the bone is removed to expose the sac, and the sac medial wall is opened to create the same anastomosis. Success rates of 85 to 90% are slightly lower than external DCR, partly because the posterior flap anastomosis is harder to create endoscopically, and partly because the ostium may scar more readily without anterior lamella support. Endonasal DCR is preferred when the patient strongly objects to a facial scar, when concurrent nasal pathology (deviated septum, polyps) needs addressing simultaneously, or when the surgeon has strong endoscopic skills. Revision after failed external DCR may also be performed endonasally.

Jones Tube for Canalicular Obstruction

When the obstruction is in the canaliculi (rather than the NLD), there is no sac to drain from — the entire bypass from the conjunctiva to the nose must be created. The Jones tube (a pyrex glass tube, 2.5 to 4 mm diameter, 17 to 30 mm long) is inserted from the medial canthal region through the caruncle, through the soft tissue, and into the middle meatus, creating a permanent fistula. Tears drain passively and by capillary action into the tube and into the nose. Jones tubes require diligent maintenance — they need periodic checking to ensure patency and correct positioning — but provide excellent long-term function in experienced hands.

Lacrimal Gland Disorders and Epiphora Workup

PG
▼

Dacryoadenitis

Acute dacryoadenitis is inflammation of the main lacrimal gland. It presents with painful swelling of the superolateral lid, producing an S-shaped ptosis — the lid droops medially from the weight of the swollen gland pulling it down laterally, creating a double curve. The conjunctival fornix may be injected, and the gland is palpable under the lateral upper lid. The most common cause in young adults is viral — mumps, EBV, CMV. Management is supportive (analgesics, warm compresses). Bacterial dacryoadenitis (Staphylococcus, Neisseria) requires systemic antibiotics; abscess formation needs drainage.

Chronic dacryoadenitis has a broader differential and a more insidious presentation. Sarcoidosis classically involves the lacrimal gland bilaterally, causing bilateral dry eye with a firm, non-tender gland. Lymphoma of the lacrimal gland (MALT lymphoma most commonly) presents similarly and can be confused with sarcoidosis clinically — biopsy is required to distinguish them. Tuberculosis, IgG4-related disease, and Wegener's granulomatosis also involve the lacrimal gland. Any chronic, firm, bilateral lacrimal gland enlargement needs a systemic workup and biopsy.

Lacrimal Gland Tumours

Lacrimal gland tumours are classified as epithelial (50%) and non-epithelial (50%). Among epithelial tumours, pleomorphic adenoma (benign mixed tumour) is the most common — 50% of epithelial lacrimal gland tumours, 25% of all lacrimal gland tumours. It presents as a slowly progressive, painless, firm superolateral lid swelling with inferomedial globe displacement and proptosis. CT shows a well-defined oval mass that remodels (but does not destroy) the surrounding orbital bone. Treatment is complete excision including the surrounding pseudocapsule — incisional biopsy is contraindicated because tumour spillage leads to recurrence and malignant transformation into carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma. The entire gland with the tumour must be excised en bloc through a lateral orbitotomy.

Adenoid cystic carcinoma is the most common malignant lacrimal gland tumour. It is an aggressive tumour with characteristic perineural invasion — it grows along nerve sheaths, which explains why pain (from nerve involvement) is a prominent early feature, in contrast to the painless slow growth of pleomorphic adenoma. Pain in any lacrimal gland lesion should strongly suggest adenoid cystic carcinoma. CT shows bone destruction. Treatment is radical surgery (orbital exenteration in most cases) followed by radiotherapy, but prognosis remains poor — 5-year survival is around 50 to 60% and long-term recurrence rates are high.

Workup for Epiphora

Epiphora (excessive watering of the eye) requires a systematic assessment to localise the site of obstruction or dysfunction. History establishes whether watering is constant or intermittent, unilateral or bilateral, whether there is associated mucopus, and any history of trauma, surgery, or nasal disease. Examination includes the regurgitation test, assessment of lid position (punctal eversion, entropion, ectropion), and nasal examination. Syringing and probing is the most informative bedside test: a Bowman probe is passed into the canaliculus, and irrigating fluid is injected; a hard stop (probe meets resistance in the canalicular wall) suggests canalicular obstruction; a soft stop (probe meets resistance before the hard wall) suggests sac or NLD blockage; free flow into the nose confirms patency. CT dacryocystography (CT DCG) — with contrast injected through the punctum — provides detailed imaging of the drainage system and identifies the site and nature of obstruction. Dacryoscintillography (nuclear medicine scan using technetium-99m) is a functional test that shows the physiological flow of tears through the drainage system and is useful when symptoms persist but syringing is patent.

The lacrimal clinical skills are among the most consistently tested procedural topics in practical and viva examinations. Each technique is described as it would be performed in the clinic or theatre.

Schirmer's Test Diagnostic Technique
1
Schirmer's Test I (Without Anaesthesia)

Fold a strip of Whatman no. 41 filter paper (35 mm x 5 mm) at the 5 mm notch. Place the folded end over the lateral third of the lower lid margin at the junction of the middle and outer thirds, with the long end hanging down. The patient sits quietly with eyes gently closed. After exactly 5 minutes, remove both strips simultaneously and measure the total length of wetting from the fold. Normal is above 10 mm. Values below 5 mm indicate severe aqueous deficiency. Test both eyes at the same time for comparability. Schirmer's I tests both basal and reflex secretion — any ocular irritation from the strip itself will trigger reflex tearing from the main lacrimal gland and can produce falsely high readings.

If the strip is saturated (all 30 mm wet) at any point before 5 minutes, note the time and record it as "greater than 30 mm in X minutes." This is still normal and much better than recording 30 mm after 5 minutes where the true value might have been 60 mm at 5 minutes.
2
Schirmer's Test II (Basic Secretion Test)

Instil topical anaesthetic (proxymetacaine 0.5% or oxybuprocaine 0.4%) and wait 5 minutes. Gently blot the conjunctival fornix dry with a cotton bud to remove any residual tear fluid and anaesthetic drops. Then place the Schirmer's strips as described above and measure after 5 minutes. Anaesthesia abolishes the reflex arc, so only basal secretion from the accessory glands (Krause and Wolfring) is measured. Normal basic secretion is above 10 mm in 5 minutes; values below 5 mm indicate severely compromised basal secretion. Compare Schirmer's I and II: if Schirmer's I is low but BST is normal, the problem is reflex secretion; if BST itself is low, basal secretion is fundamentally compromised.

3
Interpretation

Schirmer's I below 10 mm in 5 minutes: aqueous deficient dry eye. Below 5 mm: severe deficiency, Sjögren's possible. The Schirmer's value correlates imperfectly with symptoms — some patients with values of 5 mm have minimal symptoms; others with 8 mm are very symptomatic. Always interpret the number alongside TBUT, corneal staining, and the clinical history. Unilateral low Schirmer's suggests local lacrimal gland disease rather than a systemic cause.

The Schirmer's test has poor reproducibility — values can vary by 30 to 40% between tests on the same patient. This is a known limitation. Multiple measurements and correlation with other tests is the answer.
Tear Break-Up Time (TBUT) Diagnostic Technique
1
Technique

Touch a moistened fluorescein strip to the inferior conjunctival fornix (not the central cornea — central corneal staining will obscure the TBUT pattern). Ask the patient to blink two or three times to spread the fluorescein evenly across the cornea. Then ask them to keep the eyes wide open and avoid blinking. At the slit lamp, switch to the cobalt blue filter and observe the tear film under broad beam illumination. Start a stopwatch from the last blink. Watch for the first black spot or dark streak to appear in the fluorescent green tear film — this is where the tear film has thinned to zero and the fluorescein is no longer visible. Stop the stopwatch at this point. The TBUT is the time between the last blink and the first break.

Take three measurements and average them. Normal is above 10 seconds. Below 10 seconds is abnormal; below 5 seconds is severely abnormal and indicates significant tear film instability.
2
Non-Invasive TBUT (NIBUT)

Fluorescein TBUT is influenced by the fluorescein itself (which can slightly destabilise the tear film) and by the volume of fluorescein instilled. Non-invasive TBUT avoids this by projecting a Placido disc or a grid target onto the cornea (using instruments like the Keratograph) and measuring how quickly the reflection distorts — distortion indicates tear film breakup. NIBUT is more repeatable and is increasingly used as the gold standard in research and dry eye clinics. Values above 10 seconds are normal.

Syringing and Probing of the Lacrimal Passages Diagnostic Procedure
1
Preparation and Dilation

Instil topical anaesthetic into the conjunctival fornix. Ask the patient to look nasally to relax the medial lid tissue. Using a Nettleship punctum dilator, gently insert the tapered tip into the punctum and rotate slightly to dilate it — the punctum needs to accept the syringing cannula without resistance. Do not dilate forcefully; gentle progressive dilation is sufficient.

2
Passing the Cannula and Syringing

Attach a lacrimal cannula (26-gauge blunt-tipped) to a 2 mL syringe filled with normal saline. Pass the cannula vertically into the lower punctum for 2 mm (the vertical part of the canaliculus), then rotate the lid laterally to straighten the horizontal canaliculus and advance the cannula horizontally for 6 to 8 mm until you feel it reach the medial wall of the lacrimal sac. Do not force past this — the sac wall provides gentle resistance. Gently inject the saline and observe the patient's response.

Ask the patient to tell you if they taste salty fluid at the back of their nose or throat. This is the most reliable sign of a patent nasolacrimal system.
3
Interpreting the Results

Several outcomes are possible. Free flow into the nose (patient tastes saline): patent system. Regurgitation from the opposite punctum (fluid comes out of the upper punctum while you syringe the lower): the common canaliculus and canaliculi are patent but there is NLD obstruction — the sac fills and back-pressures fluid up through the opposite canaliculus. Regurgitation from the same punctum (fluid comes back from where you injected): canalicular obstruction between the cannula tip and the common canaliculus. Hard stop: advancing the probe meets a firm resistance before the sac wall — a concretion in the canaliculus or canalicular obliteration. Soft stop: the cannula reaches the sac wall but the wall feels abnormally soft and indentable — suggests periductal fibrosis or sac disease rather than bony obstruction.

Regurgitation Test Bedside Examination
1
Technique

Ask the patient to look upward (to relax the medial lid). Using the tip of your index finger or the blunt end of a cotton bud, press firmly over the lacrimal sac — the soft tissue area just below and medial to the medial palpebral ligament. Apply steady downward pressure for 2 to 3 seconds then release. Observe the lower punctum (and upper punctum) for any material expressing through. A positive regurgitation test shows mucopus or clear mucus appearing at the punctum on pressure — this confirms that the lacrimal sac contains retained secretions and that the NLD below it is obstructed.

Press below the medial palpebral ligament, not above it. The lacrimal sac sits below the tendon; pressing above it compresses nothing relevant. A common error is applying pressure too high and missing the sac entirely.
2
Interpretation

A positive test (mucopus or mucus at the punctum) confirms NLDO and lacrimal sac distension. The character of the regurgitate is informative: clear mucus suggests a mucocoele without infection; mucopus indicates bacterial dacryocystitis. Blood-stained or serosanguineous regurgitate should raise the suspicion of a lacrimal sac tumour and warrants urgent investigation including CT dacryocystography and sac biopsy at the time of DCR.

A negative regurgitation test does not exclude NLDO — if the sac has already partially drained or if the canaliculi are also obstructed, there may be no regurgitate on pressing. Syringing is the more definitive test for patency.
Undergraduate level MCQs — single best answer testing core lacrimal system concepts.
1. The normal value for Schirmer's test I is:
A. More than 5 mm in 5 minutes
B. More than 10 mm in 5 minutes
C. More than 15 mm in 5 minutes
D. More than 20 mm in 5 minutes
Answer: B. Normal Schirmer's I (without anaesthesia, testing basal plus reflex secretion) is above 10 mm wetting in 5 minutes. Below 5 mm is definitely abnormal and suggests severe aqueous deficiency. Values between 5 and 10 mm are borderline. The 10 mm threshold is the standard cut-off used clinically and in examinations.
2. Tears drain into the inferior meatus through the:
A. Valve of Rosenmuller
B. Valve of Taillefer
C. Valve of Hasner
D. Valve of Krause
Answer: C. The valve of Hasner is the mucosal fold at the lower end of the nasolacrimal duct where it opens into the inferior meatus of the nose. Its failure to open at birth in approximately 30% of neonates causes congenital NLDO. The valve of Rosenmuller is at the junction of the common canaliculus with the lacrimal sac (prevents reflux).
3. The most common cause of congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction is:
A. Failure of the valve of Hasner to open
B. Atresia of the puncta
C. Dacryolithiasis
D. Nasal polyp
Answer: A. Congenital NLDO in 95% of cases results from a thin mucous membrane at the valve of Hasner persisting after birth. This membranous obstruction at the lower end of the NLD is the anatomical basis for conservative management with Crigler's massage — hydrostatic pressure from massage ruptures the membrane. The high spontaneous resolution rate (95% by 12 months) also reflects the fragility of this membrane.
4. In acute dacryocystitis, which of the following is contraindicated:
A. Systemic antibiotics
B. Warm compresses
C. Incision and drainage
D. Syringing and probing
Answer: D. Syringing and probing are strictly contraindicated in acute dacryocystitis. Forcing fluid through an infected, obstructed, and inflamed sac risks bacteraemia, spreading the infection into the orbit (causing orbital cellulitis), and damaging the already-inflamed canalicular mucosa. Definitive DCR is performed only after the acute episode has completely resolved, typically 4 to 6 weeks later.
5. Regurgitation test is positive when:
A. Pressure over the lacrimal gland produces tearing
B. Pressure over the lacrimal sac produces mucopus at the punctum
C. Syringing fluid flows freely into the nose
D. Schirmer's test shows less than 5 mm wetting
Answer: B. The regurgitation test is performed by pressing firmly below the medial palpebral ligament over the lacrimal sac. A positive result is the appearance of mucopus or mucus at the punctum, confirming that the sac contains retained secretions and the NLD below is obstructed. It is a simple, no-equipment bedside test that strongly suggests NLDO.
6. The gold standard procedure for NLDO with the highest success rate is:
A. Endonasal DCR
B. Laser DCR
C. External DCR
D. Jones tube insertion
Answer: C. External DCR has the highest success rates (90 to 95%) and remains the gold standard for NLDO. It allows direct visualisation and wide anastomosis of lacrimal sac and nasal mucosa flaps. Endonasal DCR has slightly lower success (85 to 90%) but offers the advantage of no external scar. Jones tube is for canalicular obstruction, not NLD obstruction. Laser DCR has significantly lower success rates (60 to 70%).
7. Tear break-up time (TBUT) below which value is abnormal:
A. 10 seconds
B. 15 seconds
C. 20 seconds
D. 5 seconds only
Answer: A. TBUT below 10 seconds is abnormal and indicates an unstable tear film. Normal TBUT is 10 to 34 seconds. Values below 5 seconds indicate severely unstable tear film, usually with evaporative dry eye or MGD. The 10-second threshold is the standard clinical cut-off.
8. Crigler's massage in congenital NLDO is aimed at:
A. Expressing meibum from the Meibomian glands
B. Stimulating the nasolacrimal reflex
C. Washing out the lacrimal sac with tears
D. Building hydrostatic pressure to rupture the membranous obstruction at Hasner's valve
Answer: D. Crigler's massage applies firm downward pressure over the lacrimal sac region to build hydrostatic pressure within the NLD. This pressure is transmitted to the distal obstructing membrane at Hasner's valve. With repeated application, the membrane may rupture, establishing patency. The technique must be performed correctly — finger below the medial canthal tendon — to compress the sac rather than the tendon itself.
9. The lipid layer of the tear film is secreted by:
A. Goblet cells
B. Meibomian glands
C. Main lacrimal gland
D. Glands of Krause
Answer: B. The outermost lipid layer of the tear film is secreted by the Meibomian glands (tarsal glands). It functions to prevent evaporation of the underlying aqueous layer. MGD causes evaporative dry eye by producing an abnormal or insufficient lipid layer. Goblet cells secrete mucin (innermost layer); the main lacrimal gland and accessory glands produce the aqueous middle layer.
10. In congenital NLDO, probing under GA is typically recommended at:
A. 3 months if symptoms are present
B. 6 months as a preventive measure
C. 12 to 18 months if not resolved with conservative management
D. At birth to prevent recurrent infections
Answer: C. Because 95% of congenital NLDO resolves spontaneously by 12 months with Crigler's massage, probing is deferred until the child has had a reasonable chance for spontaneous resolution. If the obstruction persists at 12 months, probing under GA is performed at 12 to 18 months. Success rates for probing are above 90% if done before 18 months and decline with increasing age.
Postgraduate level MCQs requiring understanding of pathophysiology, surgical detail, and clinical reasoning at MS/DNB examination depth.
1. Anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies are positive in approximately what percentage of patients with Sjögren's syndrome:
A. 40 to 50%
B. 70 to 75%
C. 90 to 95%
D. 100%
Answer: B. Anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies are positive in approximately 70 to 75% of patients with Sjögren's syndrome. Anti-La (SSB) antibodies are positive in about 50 to 60%. The combination of anti-Ro and anti-La with the clinical triad (dry eye, dry mouth, autoimmune disease) is highly specific. AChR antibodies are negative (this is myasthenia). A seronegative patient with typical symptoms may still have Sjögren's — lip biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis in such cases.
2. A Jones tube is indicated specifically for:
A. Nasolacrimal duct obstruction
B. Lacrimal sac mucocoele
C. Chronic dacryocystitis
D. Canalicular obstruction
Answer: D. A Jones tube is a bypass tube inserted from the medial canthal region (at the caruncle) through to the middle meatus, bypassing the entire canalicular system. It is indicated when the canaliculi are obstructed — a situation where standard DCR (which relies on patent canaliculi to drain tears into the anastomosis) will not work. NLD obstruction with patent canaliculi is treated by DCR alone.
3. In syringing of the lacrimal passages, regurgitation of fluid from the opposite (upper) punctum when syringing through the lower punctum indicates:
A. Nasolacrimal duct obstruction with patent canaliculi
B. Common canalicular obstruction
C. Patent nasolacrimal system
D. Upper canalicular obstruction
Answer: A. Regurgitation from the opposite (upper) punctum during syringing of the lower punctum tells you that: the lower canaliculus, common canaliculus, and lacrimal sac are patent (the fluid travelled all this way), but the NLD is obstructed (it could not pass into the nose). Back pressure caused the fluid to reflux through the common canaliculus and up through the upper canaliculus to emerge at the upper punctum. If fluid regurgitated from the same (lower) punctum, the obstruction would be in the lower canaliculus itself.
4. In external DCR, the bone removed to create the ostium is primarily:
A. Frontal bone
B. Nasal bone
C. Lacrimal bone and adjacent maxilla
D. Ethmoid bone
Answer: C. The ostium in external DCR is created through the lacrimal bone and adjacent frontal process of the maxilla. The lacrimal bone is the thinnest bone in the skull and offers minimal resistance. The ostium should be approximately 12 mm x 12 mm to ensure adequate drainage and to resist cicatricial closure. The bony window communicates with the middle meatus of the nose (not the inferior meatus — the NLD opens into the inferior meatus, but the DCR ostium bypasses this and opens into the middle meatus).
5. Cyclosporine 0.05% (Restasis) eye drops act in dry eye syndrome by:
A. Increasing goblet cell number
B. Reducing T-cell-mediated lacrimal gland inflammation
C. Stimulating Meibomian gland secretion
D. Blocking acetylcholinesterase to increase tear production
Answer: B. Topical cyclosporine is an immunosuppressant that inhibits calcineurin, blocking T-cell activation and reducing the lymphocytic infiltration and inflammation of the lacrimal gland that underlies inflammatory aqueous deficient dry eye (particularly Sjögren's). This reduces lacrimal gland damage and eventually improves aqueous secretion. It takes 3 to 6 months to reach full effect, which must be communicated to patients who may otherwise stop too early.
6. Incisional biopsy of a suspected pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland is contraindicated because:
A. It causes unacceptable haemorrhage
B. The diagnosis can be made on CT alone
C. General anaesthesia risk is too high
D. Tumour spillage causes seeding, recurrence, and malignant transformation
Answer: D. Pleomorphic adenoma has a thin, incomplete pseudocapsule. If this capsule is breached during biopsy, tumour cells seed the orbit. This leads to multiple recurrences which are progressively more difficult to excise, and each recurrence carries a risk of malignant transformation into carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma — a much more dangerous tumour. The correct approach is complete excision en bloc including the pseudocapsule via lateral orbitotomy, with the specimen intact.
7. Dacryoscintillography is preferred over CT dacryocystography when:
A. The patient has symptoms of epiphora but syringing is patent
B. Acute dacryocystitis is suspected
C. A lacrimal sac tumour is suspected
D. Canalicular obstruction needs anatomical localisation
Answer: A. Dacryoscintillography (using Tc-99m pertechnetate drops instilled in the eye) is a functional test — it measures the physiological flow of tears through the drainage system, reflecting real-world drainage capacity. When a patient has symptomatic epiphora but syringing under pressure shows a patent system, it means the drainage fails under physiological conditions (normal tear flow and blinking) even though it can be overcome by syringing pressure. Scintillography identifies this functional obstruction. CT DCG provides anatomical information about where a structural blockage is and is preferred when anatomy needs delineating before surgery.
8. Schirmer's II (basic secretion test) differs from Schirmer's I in that it:
A. Uses a longer strip of filter paper
B. Is performed for 10 minutes rather than 5
C. Is performed after instillation of topical anaesthesia, measuring only basal secretion
D. Measures nasolacrimal reflex secretion
Answer: C. Schirmer's II (the basic secretion test) is performed after topical anaesthesia has abolished the corneal reflex arc, so reflex tearing from the main lacrimal gland is suppressed. Only basal secretion from the accessory glands (Krause and Wolfring) is measured. This isolates the basal secretory capacity of the lacrimal system. Normal is above 10 mm in 5 minutes; values below this on BST indicate fundamentally compromised basal secretion — a more severe deficiency than if only Schirmer's I is low.
9. The most common malignant tumour of the lacrimal gland is:
A. Squamous cell carcinoma
B. Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
C. Lymphoma
D. Adenoid cystic carcinoma
Answer: D. Adenoid cystic carcinoma is the most common malignant epithelial tumour of the lacrimal gland and accounts for about 25 to 30% of all lacrimal gland epithelial tumours. Its characteristic features are perineural invasion (pain is a prominent early feature), bone destruction on CT, and a cribriform pattern on histology (Swiss cheese appearance). Prognosis is poor despite aggressive treatment. MALT lymphoma is a common non-epithelial lacrimal gland tumour but the question asks specifically about malignant tumours in the context of lacrimal gland — adenoid cystic is the standard answer.
10. A patient with epiphora undergoes syringing. The irrigating fluid comes back from the same punctum (lower) through which it was injected, and a hard stop is felt. This indicates:
A. NLD obstruction with patent canaliculi
B. Canalicular obstruction
C. Common canalicular obstruction
D. Patent nasolacrimal system with functional obstruction
Answer: B. Regurgitation from the same punctum through which you are syringing (the lower punctum) tells you that the obstruction is between the cannula tip and the lacrimal sac — that is, within the lower canaliculus itself. A hard stop means the cannula met a firm wall rather than the soft sac wall, confirming canalicular rather than sac or NLD obstruction. This pattern would require canaliculoplasty or Jones tube rather than standard DCR, as the canaliculi cannot carry fluid to a DCR anastomosis if they are blocked.
Long answer questions covering the breadth of the lacrimal chapter as it appears in MS/DNB written examinations.
1. Classify dry eye syndrome. Describe the clinical features, investigations, and management. (10 marks)
Classification

Dry eye is classified into aqueous deficient and evaporative subtypes. Aqueous deficient dry eye: Sjögren's syndrome (keratoconjunctivitis sicca, xerostomia, and autoimmune disease — primary or secondary to RA/SLE/systemic sclerosis; anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies) and non-Sjögren's (lacrimal gland infiltration or damage, reflex block from V1 anaesthesia, Riley-Day syndrome). Evaporative dry eye: Meibomian gland dysfunction (most common overall), blink rate reduction, lagophthalmos, contact lens wear, post-LASIK, vitamin A deficiency, and preservative toxicity from eye drops.

Clinical Features

Symptoms include burning, grittiness, foreign body sensation, and paradoxical epiphora (reflex watering from an unstable tear film). Symptoms worsen with screen use, low humidity, air conditioning, and toward evening. Signs: reduced TBUT (below 10 seconds), corneal punctate epithelial staining with fluorescein (inferior one-third in aqueous deficiency, interpalpebral in evaporative), reduced Schirmer's values, rose bengal or lissamine green staining of devitalised interpalpebral conjunctiva, Meibomian gland orifice capping and turbid secretion in MGD, posterior lid margin telangiectasia.

Investigations

Schirmer's I (without anaesthesia — basal and reflex): normal above 10 mm in 5 minutes. Basic secretion test / Schirmer's II (with anaesthesia — basal only): normal above 10 mm. TBUT (fluorescein instilled, time to first dry spot after blink): normal above 10 seconds. For Sjögren's: ANA, anti-Ro, anti-La, RF, lip biopsy (focal periductal lymphocytic sialadenitis, focus score 1 or more). Tear osmolarity: above 308 mOsm/L supports dry eye. Meibography: Meibomian gland structure and drop-out.

Management

Mild: lifestyle modification (screen breaks, humidity), preservative-free artificial tears (carboxymethylcellulose, sodium hyaluronate), lid hygiene for MGD. Moderate: viscous drops or gels, warm compresses and lid scrubs, topical cyclosporine 0.05 to 0.1% (takes 3 to 6 months, reduces lacrimal gland inflammation), oral omega-3 supplements. Severe: topical steroids for acute inflammatory flares (FML, loteprednol — short course to avoid glaucoma/cataract), punctal plugs (only when inflammation is controlled), autologous serum drops 20 to 50% (for neurotrophic and severe Sjögren's dry eye). Scleral contact lenses for refractory cases. Systemic hydroxychloroquine for Sjögren's.

2. What is DCR? Describe the types, indications, steps, and complications of external DCR. (10 marks)
Definition and Indications

Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) creates a new drainage pathway from the lacrimal sac into the nasal cavity (middle meatus), bypassing the obstructed nasolacrimal duct. Indications: chronic NLDO with epiphora, recurrent acute dacryocystitis, lacrimal sac mucocoele, and lacrimal sac surgery where drainage must be re-established.

Types

External DCR (gold standard — 90 to 95% success): skin incision approach, direct sac-nasal anastomosis. Endonasal/endoscopic DCR (85 to 90% success): no scar, endonasal access, slightly lower success. Laser DCR (PANDO-L): 60 to 70% success, useful in anticoagulated patients. Jones tube (bypass): for canalicular obstruction, not NLD obstruction.

Steps of External DCR

1. Curvilinear skin incision 3 mm medial to medial canthus. 2. Identify and ligate angular vessels. 3. Periosteum elevated from anterior lacrimal crest. 4. Bony ostium (12 mm x 12 mm) created through lacrimal bone and frontal process of maxilla into middle meatus. 5. Lacrimal sac opened vertically — anterior and posterior flaps created. 6. Nasal mucosa similarly opened — anterior and posterior flaps. 7. Posterior flaps sutured together first; then anterior flaps. 8. Crawford silicone tube passed through both canaliculi, through the anastomosis, retrieved from nose, tied, and left for 3 to 6 months. 9. Skin closure in layers.

Complications

Intraoperative: haemorrhage (angular vessels, nasal mucosal bleeding), damage to medial rectus or trochlea (rare), cerebrospinal fluid leak (rare). Early postoperative: wound haematoma, infection, silicone tube prolapse. Late: restenosis/closure of the ostium (most common cause of failure), granuloma at the anastomosis, tube extrusion, persistent epiphora. The most common reason DCR fails is cicatricial closure of the ostium — prevention includes making an adequately large ostium, performing a tension-free anastomosis, and stenting with silicone for an adequate duration.

3. Describe acute and chronic dacryocystitis — clinical features, diagnosis, and management of each. (10 marks)
Acute Dacryocystitis

Acute dacryocystitis is bacterial infection of the lacrimal sac, usually complicating chronic NLDO. Presentation: sudden painful, red, tender swelling below the medial palpebral ligament (anatomically at the lacrimal sac fossa — this localisation distinguishes it from preseptal cellulitis which is above the tendon). Pressure over the sac (regurgitation test) produces mucopus at the punctum. Causative organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae. Complications if untreated: lacrimal abscess (fluctuant pointing), orbital cellulitis (if abscess ruptures posteriorly through orbital septum), skin fistula formation.

Management: systemic antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily, or cephalosporins; IV if spreading cellulitis). Warm compresses to localise the abscess. Topical antibiotic drops. Syringing and probing are contraindicated. I&D when abscess is fluctuant. DCR is the definitive treatment, performed 4 to 6 weeks after complete resolution.

Chronic Dacryocystitis

Chronic dacryocystitis presents insidiously with persistent unilateral epiphora and intermittent mucopus. There is no acute swelling or tenderness. Regurgitation test is positive (mucopus on sac pressure). Syringing: fluid regurgitates from the opposite punctum (NLD obstruction with patent canaliculi) and does not pass into the nose. Causes: dacryolithiasis, nasal disease, idiopathic fibrosis, or sequela of previous acute episode. A mucocoele develops when the sac is completely obstructed and distends with retained secretions — a non-tender, compressible swelling below the medial canthal tendon that transilluminates.

Management: syringing confirms the diagnosis. CT DCG if anatomy unclear or tumour suspected. Definitive treatment is external DCR (90 to 95% success). Mucocoele: DCR (aspiration alone leads to rapid recurrence).

4. Describe congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction — aetiology, clinical features, differential diagnosis, and management. (10 marks)
Aetiology

Congenital NLDO results from failure of the valve of Hasner (mucous membrane at the lower end of the NLD opening into the inferior meatus) to rupture at or shortly after birth. This occurs in approximately 30% of neonates and is the most common congenital lacrimal anomaly. In 95% of cases it resolves spontaneously by 12 months. Rarely, more complex bony or canalicular anomalies cause congenital obstruction.

Clinical Features

Epiphora and mucopus are present from the first weeks of life. The conjunctiva is white — there is no injection. Pressure over the lacrimal sac produces mucopus at the punctum (regurgitation test positive). One or both eyes may be affected; bilateral involvement (30%) is common. The child otherwise appears well with no systemic features.

Differential Diagnosis

Ophthalmia neonatorum (neonatal conjunctivitis) must be distinguished: conjunctival injection and chemosis are present; gonococcal ophthalmia (24 to 48 hours, copious purulent discharge) and chlamydial ophthalmia (5 to 14 days, papillary conjunctivitis) are sight-threatening and require urgent systemic treatment. Congenital glaucoma (buphthalmos, photophobia, epiphora from corneal oedema) must also be excluded — the corneal diameter exceeds 12 mm and corneal haziness is present. Simple NLDO has clear corneas and no injection.

Management

Conservative management (birth to 12 months): Crigler's massage (firm downward pumping pressure over the lacrimal sac 10 times, four times daily) to build hydrostatic pressure to rupture the membrane. Topical antibiotic drops during episodes of mucopurulent discharge to prevent secondary infection. Probing under GA at 12 to 18 months if not resolved: Bowman's probe passed through the canalicular system to rupture the membranous obstruction; NLD irrigated to confirm patency; success rate above 90% before 18 months. Second probing or silicone intubation (Crawford tubes) if first probing fails: tubes left for 3 to 6 months to maintain patency. External or endonasal DCR if intubation fails: generally deferred until age 3 to 4 years when nasal anatomy is adequately developed.

Describe the anatomy of the lacrimal drainage system. What is the significance of the valve of Hasner?
The lacrimal drainage system carries tears from the puncta to the inferior meatus of the nose. Upper and lower puncta (0.5 mm openings at medial lid margin) drain into canaliculi: each canaliculus has a 2 mm vertical segment and an 8 mm horizontal segment. The canaliculi join as a common canaliculus (5 to 8 mm) guarded by the valve of Rosenmuller (prevents reflux). The lacrimal sac (12 to 15 mm) lies in the lacrimal fossa between anterior and posterior lacrimal crests. The nasolacrimal duct (18 mm) descends in a bony canal in the maxilla to open into the inferior meatus. The valve of Hasner is the mucosal fold at this opening. Its significance: failure to open at birth in 30% of neonates causes congenital NLDO — the most common congenital lacrimal condition. Crigler's massage aims to rupture this valve by hydrostatic pressure. It resolves spontaneously in 95% of cases by 12 months. It forms the target of lacrimal probing — the probe is advanced down the NLD until the resistance of this membrane is felt and overcome. Understanding its location in the inferior meatus (not middle meatus) distinguishes it from the DCR ostium which opens into the middle meatus.

What is Sjögren's syndrome? How does it affect the eye and how is it diagnosed?
Sjögren's syndrome is an autoimmune exocrinopathy characterised by lymphocytic infiltration and destruction of exocrine glands, primarily lacrimal and salivary. Primary Sjögren's: dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) plus dry mouth (xerostomia). DNB OphthalmologyRecent
What is the three-layer structure of the tear film? Describe dry eye investigations.
The tear film consists of three layers. Innermost mucin layer: secreted by conjunctival goblet cells; converts the hydrophobic corneal surface to hydrophilic, allowing aqueous to spread; deficiency causes rapid tear film breakup. Middle aqueous layer: secreted by main lacrimal gland and accessory glands (Krause and Wolfring); constitutes 98% of tear volume; contains lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA, EGF; basal secretion from accessory glands, reflex secretion from main gland. Outer lipid layer: secreted by Meibomian glands; prevents evaporation; MGD causes evaporative dry eye. Investigations: TBUT (fluorescein instilled, time to first dry spot — normal above 10 seconds, below 5 seconds severe); Schirmer's I — without anaesthesia, tests basal plus reflex secretion, normal above 10 mm in 5 minutes; Schirmer's II (BST) — with anaesthesia, tests basal only, normal above 10 mm; Rose bengal or lissamine green staining — devitalised cells in interpalpebral zone, van Bijsterveld score above 4 abnormal; Corneal fluorescein staining — punctate staining in inferior or interpalpebral zone; Tear osmolarity (TearLab) — above 308 mOsm/L suggests dry eye; Meibography — infrared imaging of Meibomian gland structure and drop-out; Impression cytology — goblet cell density quantification.

What is the Jones tube and when is it indicated? Describe the differences between external and endonasal DCR.
Jones tube is a pyrex glass bypass tube inserted from the medial canthal region (at the caruncle) through the soft tissues of the medial canthus into the middle meatus, creating a permanent direct fistula between the conjunctival sac and the nasal cavity. It bypasses the entire canalicular system. Indication: canalicular obstruction (upper, lower, or common canalicular block) where the canaliculi cannot be used to carry tears to a standard DCR anastomosis. Technique: tube is 2.5 to 4 mm in diameter, 17 to 30 mm long; placed under GA or LA; a fistula is fashioned from the caruncle to the middle meatus, the tube is inserted and secured. Long-term maintenance — periodic checking and irrigation — is required. Comparison of external vs endonasal DCR: External DCR: curvilinear skin incision (leaves small scar), direct visualisation of sac and nasal mucosa, wide posterior and anterior flap anastomosis possible, success 90 to 95%, can be done under local anaesthesia, does not require endoscopic equipment or skill. Endonasal DCR: no external scar (cosmetic advantage), performed under GA with sinus endoscope, same bony ostium and sac opening principle, posterior flap anastomosis harder to create endoscopically (often omitted), success 85 to 90%, allows simultaneous management of nasal pathology (septal deviation, polyps), requires specialised endoscopic skill, preferred for revision of failed external DCR or when patient objects to scar.
