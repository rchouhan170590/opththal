Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (RAPD)

UGPG
▼

Marcus Gunn Pupil (APD)

  • Definition: Unequal pupillary response to light due to afferent (CN II/retinal) defect
  • Mechanism: Affected eye receives input but cannot transmit signal properly → both pupils dilate when light swings to affected side
  • Swinging Flashlight Test:
    • Shine light alternately in each eye (2 sec per eye)
    • Normal: both pupils constrict equally
    • RAPD: affected eye's pupil dilates when light swings to it ("afferent pupil dilates")
  • Quantification: Neutral density filters (0.3–0.9 ND) to find defect magnitude
Mnemonic
RAPD = Relative Afferent Pupil Defect → Optic nerve/Retina disease
NOT from iris or ciliary body (those are efferent pathology)

Causes of RAPD:

  • Optic nerve: Retrobulbar neuritis, optic neuropathy (arteritic/non-arteritic), traumatic optic neuropathy
  • Retinal: Branch/central retinal artery occlusion, acute macular necrosis, severe retinitis

Leber's Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON):

  • Genetics: Mitochondrial DNA mutation (maternally inherited)
  • Presentation: Subacute painless vision loss; optic nerve pseudoedema; males predominantly affected

  • Treatment: Supportive; high-dose thiamine; idebenone (limited evidence)

Papilloedema & Optic Atrophy

UGPG
▼

Papilloedema (Raised ICP)

  • Definition: Optic disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure; BILATERAL
  • Signs:
    • Blurred disc margins (initially superior, then circumferential)
    • Hyperemia (pink) of disc
    • Paton's lines (circumferential retinal folds)
    • Venous pulsation absent (early sign of raised ICP)
    • Cup preserved early → obliteration later
    • Cotton-wool spots, hemorrhages, hard exudates (macular star)
  • Common causes: Brain tumour, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH — obese young women, empty sella), meningitis, encephalitis
  • Chronic papilloedema: Optic atrophy develops → secondary atrophy → visual field defects (peripheral first)

Optic Atrophy

  • Primary atrophy: Post-traumatic, toxic (methanol, ethambutol, isoniazid) → pale disc with sharp margins; cup preserved
  • Consecutive atrophy: After papilloedema, retinal disease → pale disc, blurred margins; cup obliterated
Tricky Point: Venous pulsations absent = earliest sign of raised ICP. Once papilloedema develops, damage accumulates rapidly. Treat underlying cause urgently.

MCQ Bank — Neuro-Ophthalmology

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
A 45-year-old man presents with acute painless vision loss in the right eye. Fundoscopy shows a swollen optic disc with blurred margins, but no obvious retinal changes. VA: light perception only. What is the most likely diagnosis?
ACentral retinal artery occlusion
BNon-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION)
CRetrobulbar optic neuritis
DAcute glaucoma attack
Correct Answer: B — NAION
NAION presents with sudden, painless vision loss and optic disc swelling. CRAO has retinal whitening (cherry-red spot). Retrobulbar neuritis has pain on EOMs and no disc change initially ("patient sees nothing, doctor sees nothing").
Q2 PG
A patient with sudden eye pain and blurred vision presents. Swinging flashlight test shows both pupils dilate when light swings to the left eye, then constrict when light moves to the right eye. Which is CORRECT?
ALeft eye has relative afferent pupillary defect (left optic nerve disease)
BRight eye has relative afferent pupillary defect (right optic nerve disease)
CThe patient has normal pupils; repeat test required
DBoth pupils are fixed; no light reflex
Correct Answer: A — Left eye has RAPD
RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil): Affected eye's pupil DILATES when light swings to it (reduced afferent input). Pupils dilate when light on left = left eye has afferent defect. Pain + vision loss + RAPD = likely retrobulbar neuritis or optic nerve disease.
Q3 PG
A 65-year-old woman on high-dose prednisolone for polymyalgia rheumatica. Which test would most reliably confirm arteritic AION?
ACT head
BFundoscopy alone
CTemporal artery biopsy with elevated ESR >50 mm/hr
DMRI optic nerve
Correct Answer: C — Temporal artery biopsy with ESR elevation
Arteritic AION is a medical emergency. Diagnosis: clinical (sudden VA loss, disc swelling) + ESR/CRP ↑↑↑ + temporal artery biopsy showing giant cells. Start high-dose steroids immediately (within 24 hrs). Imaging is secondary.
Q4 UG
A 30-year-old woman presents with acute ptosis, dilated right pupil (5 mm), and the right eye is in "down-and-out" position. What is the diagnosis?
ARight CN III palsy (suspect posterior communicating aneurysm)
BRight CN IV palsy
CRight CN VI palsy
DHorner syndrome
Correct Answer: A — CN III palsy with pupillary involvement
  • CN III = Down-and-Out + Ptosis + Dilated pupil
  • Dilated (not constricted) pupil = CN III pupillomotor involvement.
  • In young woman with sudden onset = suspect aneurysm (PCom) until proven otherwise. Urgent imaging.
Q5 UG
A patient with bitemporal hemianopia is referred for pituitary tumor evaluation. At what anatomical location is the lesion?
AOptic nerve (retrobulbar)
BOptic chiasm (pressing from below or above)
COptic tract
DVisual cortex
Correct Answer: B — Optic chiasm
Nasal fibres cross at chiasm; compression of chiasm = loss of nasal fields (temporal hemianopia bilaterally). Pituitary tumor grows up and compresses chiasm from below.
Q6 PG
An elderly man presents with small, irregular pupils that react to near but NOT to light (accommodation reflex intact, light reflex absent). Which diagnosis fits?
AAdie's tonic pupil
BArgyll Robertson pupil (neurosyphilis)
CHorner syndrome
DDiabetic third nerve palsy
Correct Answer: B — Argyll Robertson pupil
"Accommodation but not accommodation" = near reflex YES, light reflex NO. Small irregular pupils. Classic for neurosyphilis. Adie's = LARGE pupil initially, reacts to light (just slowly). A-R = SMALL, light reflex gone.
Q7 PG
A patient with a temporal lobe stroke presents with a specific visual field defect. Which is expected?
AContralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")
BContralateral inferior quadrantanopia
CBitemporal hemianopia
DComplete homonymous hemianopia with macular involvement
Correct Answer: A — Superior quadrantanopia
Meyer's loop (inferior fibres of optic radiation) passes through temporal lobe. Lesion here → contralateral superior quadrantanopia = "pie in the sky". Parietal lobe (Baum's loop, superior fibres) → inferior quadrantanopia = "pie on floor".
Q8 UG
A 70-year-old develops acute painless vision loss and is found to have papilloedema bilaterally with blurred disc margins and venous pulsation absent. What is the EARLIEST sign of raised intracranial pressure?
ABlurred disc margins
BAbsent venous pulsation (spontaneous or diastolic)
CPaton's lines and cotton-wool spots
DOptic disc pallor
Correct Answer: B — Absent venous pulsation
Venous pulsations absent = earliest objective sign of raised ICP. Then disc swelling, Paton's lines, and macular star exudate develop. Once papilloedema evident, damage accumulates rapidly.
Q9 PG
A young woman presents with unilateral ptosis and hypertropia (affected eye higher than the normal eye). The Bielschowsky head tilt test worsens the hypertropia when the head is tilted to the opposite side. What is the diagnosis?
ACN III palsy
BCN IV (trochlear) palsy
CCN VI palsy
DLateral rectus weakness
Correct Answer: B — CN IV (trochlear) palsy
CN IV = Superior oblique = intorsion as PRIMARY action. Palsy causes: hypertropia (SO can't depress) + head tilt to opposite side (compensatory). Bielschowsky test POSITIVE = head tilt to affected side minimizes hypertropia, opposite side worsens it. Classic trochlear nerve finding.
Q10 PG
A 35-year-old presents with Horner syndrome (ipsilateral ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis). Cocaine 10% drops are applied: the miotic pupil DOES NOT dilate. What does this indicate?
ACentral or preganglionic (1st or 2nd order) Horner syndrome — need imaging
BPostganglionic (3rd order) Horner syndrome — benign, no urgent imaging
CNormal result; patient doesn't have Horner syndrome
DThe patient has a fixed pupil; repeat test
Correct Answer: A — Central/preganglionic Horner
Cocaine blocks norepinephrine reuptake. In Horner: miotic pupil should dilate if stores of NE are intact (postganglionic). NO dilation = depleted NE stores = central/preganglionic (1st/2nd order) lesion. Requires urgent imaging (stroke, Pancoast tumor).

Describe the swinging flashlight test, interpret results in a patient with suspected optic nerve disease, and differentiate between RAPD and normal pupils. (5 marks)
Technique: Alternate light between eyes (2 sec each); Normal: both constrict equally. RAPD: affected eye's pupil dilates when light swings to it. Indicates CN II/retinal disease.

Pupil Pathways

  • Light reflex arc: Retina (CN II) → Pretectal nucleus → EW (CN III) → Ciliary ganglion → Sphincter
  • Accommodation: Near object → Ciliary muscle contracts → Pupil constricts + lens thickens + eyes converge
  • RAPD (Marcus Gunn): Afferent defect → affected pupil dilates to light
  • A-R pupil: Small, irregular; near YES, light NO; neurosyphilis
  • Adie's pupil: Large; near YES (slow), light NO initially; supersensitive to dilute pilocarpine

Optic Nerve Disease

  • NAION: Painless, disc swelling, altitudinal VF loss, >50 yr
  • AION (arteritic): Temporal arteritis; ESR ↑↑↑; urgent high-dose steroids
  • Retrobulbar neuritis: Pain on EOMs; "patient sees nothing, doctor sees nothing"; MS risk 50%
  • LHON: Mitochondrial; males; optic pseudoedema; subacute vision loss
  • Papilloedema: Raised ICP; bilateral; venous pulsation absent (earliest sign)

CN Palsies (Quick Recall)

  • CN III: Down-and-Out + Ptosis + Dilated pupil = aneurysm risk
  • CN IV: Hypertropia; Bielschowsky head tilt test POSITIVE (opposite side worsens)
  • CN VI: Esotropia; can't look out; false localizing sign of raised ICP

Visual Field Defects

  • Monocular: Optic nerve/retina (CRAO, glaucoma)
  • Bitemporal: Chiasm (pituitary tumor)
  • Homonymous: Post-chiasm (tract, LGN, radiation, cortex)
  • "Pie in the sky": Meyer's loop (temporal lobe) = superior quadrantanopia
  • "Pie on floor": Baum's loop (parietal) = inferior quadrantanopia
