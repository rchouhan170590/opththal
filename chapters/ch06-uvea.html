Key Uveitis Entities

PG
▼

Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH)

  • Type: Granulomatous panuveitis (bilateral)
  • Features: Exudative retinal detachment, disc swelling, meningeal symptoms (headache, meningitis-like)
  • HLA: Not strictly HLA-linked but predisposition in certain populations
  • Treatment: Systemic steroids (prednisolone 60 mg/day, tapered) + immunosuppressants if needed

Sympathetic Ophthalmia (SO)

  • Type: Granulomatous panuveitis (bilateral); autoimmune reaction to eye injury
  • Trigger: Trauma, surgery to one eye → bilateral uveitis 2 weeks–years later
  • Features: Diffuse granulomatous inflammation; optic nerve involvement; exudative RD
  • Treatment: Aggressive systemic steroids + immunosuppressants to prevent phthisis

Fuchs Heterochromic Cyclitis (FHC)

  • Type: Chronic anterior uveitis (usually unilateral)
  • Features: Fine, diffuse KPs; minimal photophobia/pain; iris heterochromia (lighter in affected eye); cataract common
  • Prognosis: Excellent vision; self-limited; rarely blindness
  • Etiology: Possibly rubella-associated (debated)

Posner-Schlossman Syndrome

  • Type: Acute anterior uveitis (self-resolving episodes)
  • Features: Recurrent, painless attacks; elevated IOP; fine KPs; minimal photophobia
  • Trigger: Often seasonal (spring/fall); possibly herpetic
  • Course: Episodes last 6–12 weeks, spontaneous resolution

Sarcoidosis-Associated Uveitis

  • Type: Can be anterior, intermediate, or posterior; often granulomatous
  • Systemic features: Hilar lymphadenopathy, pulmonary involvement, skin lesions (erythema nodosum)
  • Ophthalmic: Lacrimal gland involvement (keratoconjunctivitis sicca); optic nerve papillitis
  • Diagnosis: Elevated ACE, hypercalciuria, chest X-ray; biopsy if needed

Complications of Uveitis

UGPG
▼
  • Posterior synechiae: Adhesions between iris and lens; iris bombe if complete; secondary glaucoma
  • Iris bombe: Forward bulging of iris due to complete synechiae; angle-closure glaucoma
  • Secondary glaucoma: From inflammation, posterior synechiae, trabeculitis, or membrane over TM
  • Band keratopathy: Calcium deposition in cornea; common in chronic uveitis; causes hazy vision
  • Cataract: Anterior subcapsular; due to inflammation or chronic steroid use; nuclear sclerosis in older patients
  • Vitritis: Vitreous inflammation; floaters, haze; in pars planitis = "snowball" opacities
  • Cystoid macular edema (CME): Macular thickening from inflammation or chronic uveitis; causes vision loss
  • Optic nerve involvement: Papillitis, optic neuritis; disc swelling and colour changes
  • Exudative retinal detachment: Especially in VKH, SO; can threaten central vision
  • Phthisis bulbi: End-stage; blind, shrunken eye; from chronic uncontrolled uveitis

Uveal Melanoma

PGHigh-Yield
▼
Key Fact: Uveal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults (NOT retinoblastoma, which is the most common in children).

Epidemiology

  • Incidence: ~5 cases per million per year; peak age 50–70 years
  • Risk factors: Fair skin, light eyes, dysplastic nevi, ocular melanocytosis
  • Not associated: Sun exposure (unlike cutaneous melanoma)

Location

  • Choroid (80%): Most common; posterior aspect
  • Ciliary body (10%): Often diagnosed late
  • Iris (10%): Better prognosis (diagnosed earlier)

Clinical Features

  • Often asymptomatic (found on screening or routine exam)
  • Dark pigmented lesion on fundus (brown/black)
  • Subretinal exudation; lipofuscin (orange pigment) at apex
  • Visual symptoms: floaters, photopsia, or visual field loss if central

Callender Classification (Histopathology)

TypeCharacteristicsPrognosis
Spindle AUniform, fusiform nucleiBest (10-year survival ~90%)
Spindle BSpindle cells with coarse chromatinIntermediate
MixedMixture of spindle and epithelioid cellsIntermediate
EpithelioidEpithelioid cells; high mitotic activityWorst (10-year survival ~35%)

Treatment

  • Plaque brachytherapy: Ruthenium-106 plaque; first-line for medium-sized tumours; preserves globe
  • Enucleation: For large tumours or those threatening vision; curative but permanent vision loss
  • External beam radiotherapy: Proton/photon therapy; preserves globe in some cases
  • Chemotherapy: Systemic for metastatic disease (liver is common site)
  • Laser/thermal therapy: For small, peripheral lesions

Surveillance

  • Regular dilated fundal exams (every 3–6 months)
  • Ultrasound B-scan for monitoring tumour size
  • Imaging (MRI, CT) and systemic workup for metastases

MCQ Bank — Uveal Tract & Uveitis

12 questions · ~24 min
Q1 UG
Which of the following is the most common type of uveitis?
AIntermediate uveitis
BPosterior uveitis
CAnterior uveitis
DPanuveitis
Correct Answer: C — Anterior uveitis
Anterior uveitis accounts for ~80% of all uveitis cases. It involves inflammation of the iris, ciliary body, and AC angle. Posterior and intermediate uveitis are less common.
Q2 PG
Mutton fat keratic precipitates (KPs) are characteristic of which type of uveitis?
AGranulomatous uveitis
BNon-granulomatous uveitis
CAcute bacterial uveitis
DViral uveitis
Correct Answer: A — Granulomatous uveitis
Mutton fat KPs are large, greasy, and fluffy, located on the lower cornea. They indicate granulomatous inflammation (lymphocytes). Non-granulomatous uveitis has small, fine, dusty KPs. Granulomatous uveitis requires systemic workup.
Q3 PG
Which HLA antigen is most strongly associated with acute anterior uveitis?
AHLA-A29
BHLA-B27
CHLA-B51
DHLA-DR3
Correct Answer: B — HLA-B27
HLA-B27 is the most common HLA association with acute anterior uveitis. Associated diseases include AS, Reiter's syndrome, psoriatic arthritis, and IBD-associated uveitis. Occurs in young males; photophobia and pain on eye movement are characteristic.
Q4 PG
Birdshot chorioretinopathy is most commonly associated with which HLA antigen?
AHLA-B27
BHLA-B51
CHLA-DR4
DHLA-A29
Correct Answer: D — HLA-A29
HLA-A29 is specific to birdshot chorioretinopathy. The characteristic fundus finding is a pattern of birdshot lesions (small, round, orange spots) at the posterior pole. It causes posterior uveitis with photopsia and visual field loss.
Q5 PG
Behçet's disease-associated uveitis is characterised by all EXCEPT:
AHLA-B51 association
BInsidious onset with minimal pain
CPanuveitis with recurrent attacks
DSystemic features (oral ulcers, genital ulcers)
Correct Answer: B — Insidious onset with minimal pain
Behçet's uveitis is acute and painful, not insidious. It presents with severe ocular pain, redness, and photophobia. Recurrent attacks are common. Associated with HLA-B51 and systemic features (oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions, arthralgia).
Q6 UG
Posterior synechiae formation in uveitis can lead to:
AAnterior chamber shallowing
BIris bombe and secondary glaucoma
CCataract formation
DCorneal opacity
Correct Answer: B — Iris bombe and secondary glaucoma
Complete posterior synechiae (iris adheres to lens across full pupil) → aqueous trapped behind iris → forward bulging (iris bombe) → angle closure → secondary glaucoma. Partial synechiae cause irregular pupil and distorted vision.
Q7 PG
Which uveal melanoma histopathological type has the best prognosis?
ASpindle A
BSpindle B
CMixed
DEpithelioid
Correct Answer: A — Spindle A
Spindle A has uniform, fusiform nuclei with best 10-year survival (~90%). Epithelioid type has worst prognosis (10-year survival ~35%). Spindle B and mixed are intermediate. Callender classification is based on histopathology and helps guide treatment and surveillance.
Q8 UG
Uveal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in:
AInfants and young children
BAdolescents
CYoung adults
DMiddle-aged and older adults
Correct Answer: D — Middle-aged and older adults
Peak age for uveal melanoma is 50–70 years. In children, retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy. Fair skin, light eyes, and dysplastic nevi are risk factors for uveal melanoma (NOT sun exposure).
Q9 PG
Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) syndrome is characterised by all EXCEPT:
ABilateral granulomatous panuveitis
BHLA-B27 association
CExudative retinal detachment
DMeningeal symptoms (headache)
Correct Answer: B — HLA-B27 association
VKH is not strictly HLA-B27 associated. It presents as bilateral granulomatous panuveitis with exudative RD, disc swelling, and meningeal symptoms. Treated with systemic steroids. HLA-B27 is associated with acute anterior uveitis (AS, Reiter's), not VKH.
Q10 PG
Fuchs heterochromic cyclitis (FHC) is known for all EXCEPT:
ASevere, painful attacks of uveitis
BIris heterochromia (lighter iris in affected eye)
CFine, diffuse keratic precipitates
DExcellent visual prognosis
Correct Answer: A — Severe, painful attacks
FHC is a chronic but painless anterior uveitis. Patients often have minimal complaints. Characterized by iris heterochromia, fine KPs, and generally excellent visual outcome despite cataract formation. Prognosis is much better than other uveitis entities.
Q11 PG
Which is the most common site for uveal melanoma?
AIris (10%)
BCiliary body (10%)
CChoroid (80%)
DOptic nerve head
Correct Answer: C — Choroid (80%)
Choroidal melanomas comprise 80% of uveal melanomas. Ciliary body (10%) and iris (10%) are less common. Choroidal melanomas are often posterior and may be asymptomatic. Iris melanomas have better prognosis (diagnosed earlier).
Q12 PG
The first-line treatment for medium-sized uveal melanoma is:
APlaque brachytherapy (Ruthenium-106)
BEnucleation
CExternal beam radiotherapy
DLaser photocoagulation
Correct Answer: A — Plaque brachytherapy
Plaque brachytherapy with Ru-106 is the first-line treatment for medium-sized uveal melanomas. It preserves the globe and is curative. Enucleation is reserved for large tumours or those threatening vision. Laser therapy is for small, peripheral lesions.

Correct Answer: Posterior synechiae → iris bombe → glaucoma. Cataract, CME, band keratopathy, optic neuritis, exudative RD. Management: steroids, immunosuppressants, treatment of secondary glaucoma, cataract surgery if needed.

Uveitis Classification

  • Anterior (80%): Iritis, iridocyclitis; AC inflammation
  • Intermediate: Pars planitis; vitritis with snowball opacities
  • Posterior: Choroiditis, retinitis; optic involvement
  • Panuveitis: All three; most severe; VKH, SO, Behçet's

Granulomatous vs Non-Granulomatous

  • Granulomatous: Mutton fat KPs, Koeppe/Busacca nodules, systemic assocs (TB, sarcoidosis, VKH)
  • Non-granulomatous: Fine KPs, acute onset, HLA-B27 (AS, Reiter's), SLE

HLA in Uveitis

  • B27: AAU, AS, Reiter's, psoriasis, IBD
  • A29: Birdshot chorioretinopathy
  • B51: Behçet's (HLA-B51 = Behçet's)
  • ANA+: SLE, JIA

Key Entities

  • VKH: Bilateral panuveitis; exudative RD; systemic steroids
  • SO: Bilateral after eye trauma; aggressive steroids + ISS
  • FHC: Chronic painless; heterochromia; excellent prognosis
  • Posner-Schlossman: Recurrent painful episodes; seasonal

Uveal Melanoma Facts

  • Most common intraocular malignancy in adults (NOT retinoblastoma)
  • Peak age 50–70 years
  • Choroid (80%), ciliary body (10%), iris (10%)
  • Callenden classification: Spindle A (best) → Epithelioid (worst)
  • Treatment: Plaque brachytherapy (medium); enucleation (large)

Complications of Uveitis

  • Posterior synechiae → iris bombe → secondary glaucoma
  • Band keratopathy (calcium deposition)
  • Cataract (anterior subcapsular)
  • CME (macular oedema)
  • Optic neuritis, exudative RD
  • Phthisis bulbi (end-stage)
