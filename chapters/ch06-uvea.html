Chapter 6

Uveal Tract

Uveitis in all its forms — anterior, intermediate, posterior and panuveitis — with HLA associations, systemic connections, and management including specific conditions like VKH, Behcet's and toxoplasmosis.

UG PG

Uveitis is one of the most information-dense topics in ophthalmology. The system below distils the essential facts — HLA associations, clinical signs, cause patterns, and complications — into cards you can revise quickly the night before an exam.

Uveitis Classification

The SUN (Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature) working group provides the framework most exams use.

  • Anatomical — Anterior (iritis, iridocyclitis), Intermediate (pars planitis, cyclitis), Posterior (choroiditis, chorioretinitis), Panuveitis (all layers)
  • Morphological — Granulomatous (mutton-fat KPs, iris nodules, causes: TB/sarcoid/VKH/syphilis/leprosy) vs Non-granulomatous (fine stellate KPs, causes: HLA-B27 diseases)
  • Temporal — Acute (sudden onset, limited duration less than 3 months) vs Chronic (persistent beyond 3 months) vs Recurrent
  • Aetiology — Infectious vs Non-infectious (immune-mediated)
High-Yield

Anterior Uveitis — Clinical Features

The classic presentation is a unilateral red, painful, photophobic eye with reduced vision, typically in a young or middle-aged adult.

  • Circumcorneal injection — ciliary flush (pink ring at the limbus from deep episcleral/limbal vessels); does not extend to the fornix
  • KPs (keratic precipitates) — fine stellate (non-granulomatous, at Arlt's triangle) vs mutton-fat (large greasy, granulomatous)
  • Cells and flare — cells = white cells in AC; flare = protein leak (Tyndall effect); graded 0 to 4+ on SUN scale
  • Posterior synechiae — iris adherent to lens; pupil irregular, does not dilate uniformly; can lead to seclusio/occlusion pupillae
  • Iris nodules — Koeppe (at pupil margin, both types), Busacca (in iris stroma, granulomatous only)
High-Yield

HLA Associations in Uveitis

HLA associations are a perennial exam favourite. Know these cold.

  • HLA-B27 — Ankylosing spondylitis, Reactive arthritis (Reiter's), Psoriatic arthritis, IBD-related uveitis. Mnemonic: PAIR (Psoriasis, Ankylosing spondylitis, IBD, Reactive arthritis)
  • HLA-A29 — Birdshot retinochoroidopathy (bilateral posterior uveitis with discrete cream-coloured spots)
  • HLA-B51 — Behcet's disease (strongest in Turkish/Middle Eastern populations)
  • HLA-DR4 — Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) disease
High-Yield

Posterior Uveitis Causes

  • Toxoplasmosis — most common worldwide; "headlight in fog" (active white fluffy lesion adjacent to old pigmented chorioretinal scar); reactivation disease in adults; cat as primary host
  • Toxocara — children, single peripheral white granuloma, strabismus or leucocoria; dog as intermediate host; ELISA confirms
  • CMV retinitis — immunocompromised (HIV CD4 below 50); "pizza-pie fundus" with haemorrhages and white areas; requires systemic antiviral (ganciclovir/valganciclovir)
  • TB — choroidal granuloma (Dalen-Fuchs-like), periphlebitis, serpentine choroiditis; treat with ATT plus steroids
  • Syphilis — the great mimic; can cause any pattern of uveitis; FTA-ABS most sensitive; systemic penicillin
High-Yield

Complications of Uveitis

  • Band keratopathy — calcium deposits at Bowman's layer in the interpalpebral zone; from chronic anterior uveitis (especially in children with JIA-associated uveitis); treated with EDTA chelation
  • Complicated cataract — posterior subcapsular cataract (PSC); from chronic inflammation and steroid use
  • Secondary glaucoma — trabeculitis (open angle) or pupil block from iris bombe (posterior synechiae causing 360-degree posterior synechiae)
  • Cystoid macular oedema (CMO) — most common cause of visual loss in posterior uveitis; petaloid pattern on FFA; treated with intravitreal steroid or anti-VEGF
  • Phthisis bulbi — end-stage; shrunken, disorganised, non-functional eye

Key Mnemonics

PAIR for HLA-B27 diseases: Psoriatic arthritis, Ankylosing spondylitis, IBD-related uveitis, Reactive arthritis. All cause anterior non-granulomatous uveitis. All in the HLA-B27 spectrum.

ABCDE workup for posterior uveitis:

  • A — ANCA (Wegener's), ACE level (sarcoidosis)
  • B — Blood counts, VDRL/RPR (syphilis screen)
  • C — Chest X-ray (TB, sarcoidosis, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy)
  • D — Diabetes screen, Diagnostic vitreous tap (if infective cause suspected)
  • E — ELISA for toxoplasma (IgG/IgM), FTA-ABS for syphilis
PG Level — Detailed Notes

Uveal Tract Anatomy

UGPG
▼

The uveal tract is the middle vascular coat of the eye, sandwiched between the sclera externally and the retina internally. It comprises three anatomically continuous structures: the choroid, the ciliary body, and the iris. Together they constitute the major vascular supply and immune environment of the eye, which is why uveitis — inflammation of any part of this tract — has such profound consequences for vision.

The choroid extends from the optic disc margin posteriorly to the ora serrata anteriorly. It is essentially a bed of blood vessels arranged in layers — the outermost Haller's layer contains large vessels, the middle Sattler's layer contains medium vessels, and the innermost choriocapillaris is a dense capillary network immediately beneath Bruch's membrane. Bruch's membrane (the basement membrane of the RPE on its outer aspect) separates the choroid from the RPE and retina. The choroidal blood supply comes from the posterior ciliary arteries and drains through the vortex veins. The choroid has a very high blood flow rate — the highest of any tissue in the body per unit weight — which is relevant to its role in temperature regulation and oxygen delivery to the outer retina.

The ciliary body occupies the anterior third of the uveal tract, lying between the ora serrata posteriorly and the iris root anteriorly. It has two parts: the pars plana (flat, posterior, 4 mm wide — the surgeon's entry point for vitreoretinal procedures) and the pars plicata (corrugated, anterior, with ciliary processes that produce aqueous humour). The ciliary muscle controls accommodation. Sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation regulate both the ciliary muscle and the pupil dilator and sphincter.

The iris is the anterior extension of the uveal tract, lying in front of the lens and behind the cornea. It has two layers — the anterior stroma containing melanocytes (and the sphincter and dilator pupillae muscles) and the posterior pigmented epithelium. The colour of the iris depends on the density of melanocytes in the anterior stroma — blue eyes have few, brown eyes have many. Iris nodules in uveitis (Koeppe and Busacca) are collections of inflammatory cells within the iris stroma and at the pupil margin.

The uveal blood supply via the posterior and anterior ciliary arteries means that inflammation in one part of the uvea readily spreads to adjacent regions. The circle of Zinn-Haller (arterial anastomosis around the optic nerve) and the major arterial circle of the iris connect these systems. This explains why posterior scleritis can cause anterior segment changes, and why severe anterior uveitis can have posterior segment sequelae.

Anterior Uveitis

UGPG
▼

Anterior uveitis (iritis or iridocyclitis) is by far the most common form of uveitis in clinical practice. The typical patient presents acutely with unilateral redness, deep aching pain, photophobia, and blurred vision. These symptoms may develop over hours to a day. There is usually no discharge — this distinguishes it from conjunctivitis, where discharge is a prominent feature.

On examination, the circumcorneal injection (ciliary flush) is the key external sign. Unlike conjunctivitis, where the redness is maximal at the fornices and brightens toward the cul-de-sac, the ciliary flush is a pink-violet injection that is maximal at the limbus and fades peripherally toward the fornix. The cornea may show fine Descemet's folds from mild oedema. Corneal sensation is preserved, unlike in herpetic keratitis.

Slit lamp examination of the anterior chamber is the core of the diagnosis. Cells appear as white particles floating in the aqueous when you use a narrow beam at a 45-degree angle in a dark room. Flare is the haziness of the aqueous beam — like headlights in fog — caused by protein leaking from inflamed vessels into the AC. The SUN grading system uses 0 to 4+ for both cells (per field at 1mm x 1mm beam) and flare. Grade 2+ cells means 6 to 15 cells per field; Grade 4+ is more than 50. Knowing how to grade these properly is a clinical skills examination requirement.

Keratic precipitates (KPs) are inflammatory cells that deposit on the corneal endothelium. The location follows Arlt's triangle — the inferior third of the cornea, where aqueous convection currents deposit cells. Fine stellate or "dusted" KPs are seen in non-granulomatous uveitis (HLA-B27 diseases, Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis). Large, greasy "mutton-fat" KPs are the hallmark of granulomatous uveitis — TB, sarcoidosis, VKH, syphilis, and leprosy. The KP morphology is the fastest way at the slit lamp to decide whether you are dealing with granulomatous or non-granulomatous disease.

Posterior synechiae are adhesions between the posterior iris surface and the anterior lens capsule. They form when fibrin and inflammatory cells bridge the gap during active inflammation. When the pupil dilates unevenly — pulling away from some adherent points but not others — the irregular pupil that results is called "festooned." In severe cases, 360-degree posterior synechiae lead to complete pupillary block. The iris then bulges forward (iris bombe) because aqueous cannot flow from the posterior to the anterior chamber, causing secondary angle-closure glaucoma — an emergency.

Treatment of anterior uveitis follows a predictable pattern. Topical prednisolone acetate 1% is the first-line anti-inflammatory agent — given every 1 to 2 hours in acute severe disease, tapering as the eye quiets. Cycloplegics (atropine 1% for severe disease, cyclopentolate 1% for moderate disease) serve two purposes: they prevent posterior synechiae formation by keeping the pupil moving, and they relieve the ciliary spasm that causes the deep aching pain. Never give a mydriatic without a cycloplegic in uveitis — dilation without cycloplegia does not adequately break synechiae and may cause them to form at the dilated position. For bilateral, severe, or treatment-resistant disease, systemic prednisolone is added.

The most common cause of acute anterior uveitis in clinical practice in a young adult without other features is HLA-B27-associated disease. Ankylosing spondylitis accounts for the majority. Always ask about back pain, sacroiliac tenderness, morning stiffness, skin rashes (psoriasis), bowel symptoms (IBD), and recent urethritis or diarrhoea (reactive arthritis). The HLA-B27-associated uveitis tends to be acute, unilateral (though alternating between eyes), non-granulomatous, and responds well to topical steroids.

1
Cycloplegic (always first): Atropine 1% for severe disease, cyclopentolate 1% for moderate. Prevents posterior synechiae and relieves ciliary spasm pain. Never omit this step.
2
Topical steroid: Prednisolone acetate 1% every 1 to 2 hours initially. Taper as cells decrease — aim for less than 1+ cells before reducing frequency. Minimum 4 to 6 weeks of treatment for acute anterior uveitis.
3
Systemic steroid: Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day for bilateral, severe, or topical-resistant disease. Slow taper over weeks. Always cover with a proton pump inhibitor.
4
Treat underlying cause: Identify and treat the systemic disease (spondyloarthropathy, TB, sarcoid). This reduces recurrence frequency and severity.
5
Complications: Monitor IOP (steroid-induced glaucoma vs trabeculitis). Treat secondary glaucoma with aqueous suppressants. PSC cataract surgery once the eye is quiet for at least 3 months. Band keratopathy with EDTA chelation.

Steroid-induced glaucoma is a real risk in uveitis patients receiving intensive topical prednisolone acetate. Check IOP at every visit when on intensive steroids. If IOP rises above 24 mmHg, add an aqueous suppressant (timolol, dorzolamide) and consider switching to a lower-potency steroid (fluorometholone, loteprednol). Prostaglandin analogues should be avoided in active uveitis as they can exacerbate inflammation.

Granulomatous Uveitis Causes

UGPG
▼

When you see mutton-fat KPs, Busacca nodules in the iris stroma, or Koeppe nodules at the pupil margin, you are looking at granulomatous uveitis. The differential is narrower than non-granulomatous disease but each cause has specific features that can allow clinical diagnosis.

Tuberculosis uveitis is the most important infectious cause of granulomatous uveitis in high-prevalence countries. It can cause anterior granulomatous uveitis, but its most characteristic posterior manifestations include a solitary choroidal granuloma, occlusive periphlebitis (Eales' disease pattern — "candle wax drippings" is actually a sarcoid description; TB causes more florid periphlebitis), and the rare serpentine choroiditis (an aggressive creeping choroidal lesion). Diagnosis rests on Mantoux test, IGRA (interferon gamma release assay), chest X-ray, and CT thorax. Treatment is full anti-tuberculosis therapy for 6 to 9 months plus oral prednisolone to prevent paradoxical worsening from immune reconstitution.

Sarcoidosis causes bilateral, chronic, granulomatous uveitis in middle-aged adults, more common in black populations. The classic slit lamp finding is mutton-fat KPs plus "candle wax drippings" on the retinal venules (perivenular exudates). Iris nodules, trabecular meshwork nodules (causing secondary glaucoma), and optic disc granulomas are also seen. Systemic features include bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (most common), erythema nodosum, and parotid enlargement. Serum ACE is elevated in about 60% of sarcoid patients and supports the diagnosis but is not specific. Treatment is systemic corticosteroids with steroid-sparing immunosuppression for chronic disease.

Granulomatous Uveitis Causes — SALT

S — Sarcoidosis (bilateral, chronic, candle-wax drippings, ACE elevated). A — Autoimmune (VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia). L — Leprosy (iris pearls in lepromatous), also Lyme disease. T — Tuberculosis (choroidal granuloma, periphlebitis), plus Syphilis (the great mimic, added to T for Treponema). Remember: non-granulomatous causes are the HLA-B27 PAIR group and Behcet's — they produce fine KPs, not mutton-fat.

Syphilis deserves special mention as the "great mimic" — it can cause any pattern of uveitis (anterior, posterior, or pan), mimicking virtually every other condition. The fundoscopic appearances range from diffuse chorioretinitis to focal lesions, vasculitis, and even a picture resembling APMPPE or serpiginous choroiditis. In any atypical uveitis that does not fit a pattern, syphilis serology (FTA-ABS, the most sensitive test, and TPHA/RPR for screening) must be checked. Treatment is systemic benzylpenicillin (aqueous crystalline penicillin G for ocular syphilis, given intravenously for at least 10 days).

Leprosy (Hansen's disease) causes ocular disease through two mechanisms: direct bacillary infiltration in lepromatous leprosy, and immune reactions. The characteristic ocular finding is iris pearls (lepra pearls) — glistening, whitish nodules on the iris surface in lepromatous leprosy from accumulation of Mycobacterium leprae. Madarosis (loss of lateral eyebrows and eyelashes) and lagophthalmos from facial nerve involvement are the other important features.

VKH Disease and Sympathetic Ophthalmia

UGPG
▼

Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada disease and sympathetic ophthalmia share a common immunological theme — both are autoimmune conditions targeting melanocytes and melanin-containing tissues — but they differ in trigger, laterality at onset, and prognosis.

VKH is a bilateral, multisystem, granulomatous panuveitis with strong HLA-DR4 association, seen predominantly in Japanese, East Asian, Latin American, and Middle Eastern populations. The disease has four phases. The prodromal phase resembles viral meningitis: headache, meningism, cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis, tinnitus, and dysacusis. The acute uveitic phase follows within days: bilateral diffuse choroiditis, multiple serous retinal detachments (bilateral, often bullous), disc oedema, and anterior granulomatous uveitis with mutton-fat KPs. The convalescent phase brings the characteristic dermatological features — poliosis (whitening of hair, eyelashes, and eyebrows), vitiligo (patchy skin depigmentation), and alopecia. The recurrent chronic phase is marked by recurrent episodes of anterior uveitis with depigmented "sunset glow fundus."

Dalen-Fuchs nodules are the histological hallmark of VKH (and also of sympathetic ophthalmia). They are focal collections of epithelioid cells and pigmented macrophages between Bruch's membrane and the RPE — essentially granulomas at the level of the outer retina. On fundoscopy they appear as small, discrete, yellowish lesions scattered throughout the fundus. They are pathognomonic in the appropriate clinical context.

Treatment of VKH requires immediate high-dose systemic corticosteroids — oral prednisolone 1 to 1.5 mg/kg/day, with very slow tapering over 6 to 12 months. Rapid tapering causes relapse and the chronic recurrent form is more damaging than the initial acute episode. Steroid-sparing immunosuppressives (azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclosporin) are added in corticosteroid-dependent or refractory cases.

Sympathetic ophthalmia (SO) occurs after penetrating injury or intraocular surgery to one eye (the "exciting" eye), triggering bilateral granulomatous panuveitis. The uninjured eye is called the "sympathizing" eye. The latent period is classically never less than 2 weeks — the minimum period required for sensitisation — and can be as long as years or even decades after the original injury. The bilateral granulomatous uveitis that develops, with Dalen-Fuchs nodules, is the mechanism.

The critical management decision in sympathetic ophthalmia is whether to enucleate the exciting eye. If the exciting eye has no visual potential, enucleation within 2 weeks of injury prevents the development of SO — this is the prophylactic window. Once SO has established (once the sympathizing eye develops uveitis), enucleation of the exciting eye no longer prevents or reverses the disease in the sympathizing eye. At this point, systemic immunosuppression (high-dose prednisolone plus steroid-sparing agents) is the mainstay of treatment. This is a frequently examined principle: enucleation is prophylactic (within 2 weeks), not therapeutic (after SO develops).

Behcet's Disease

UGPG
▼

Behcet's disease is a systemic vasculitis with a predilection for young males of Turkish, Middle Eastern, and East Asian descent. It carries the HLA-B51 association, the strongest of any HLA-disease link in ophthalmology in terms of relative risk in certain populations. The classic triad of recurrent oral aphthous ulcers, genital ulcers, and ocular inflammation defines the syndrome, though systemic involvement can include the joints, skin, gut, and central nervous system.

The ocular involvement is bilateral and potentially devastating. It presents as recurrent, severe, occlusive vasculitis of the retinal vessels — both veins and arteries can be affected. What makes Behcet's uveitis distinctive is the hypopyon: a non-infectious, shifting hypopyon (it moves when the patient changes head position, unlike the adherent hypopyon of bacterial endophthalmitis or fungal infection). The hypopyon in Behcet's is a collection of inflammatory cells in the AC rather than purulent material. Vitreous haze is severe, retinal neovascularisation occurs after occlusive disease, and optic atrophy follows repeated episodes of disc involvement. Visual prognosis without treatment is poor — up to 25% of patients are legally blind within 5 years.

The pathergy test is a useful clinical feature of Behcet's: a positive response is the development of a pustule or erythematous nodule at a site of minor skin trauma (needle prick) within 24 to 48 hours. This hyperreactivity of the skin to minor injury reflects the exaggerated innate immune response that characterises Behcet's. The test is more positive in Turkish and Japanese patients than in Western populations.

Treatment of ocular Behcet's has improved substantially with biologics. The traditional approach uses oral prednisolone plus azathioprine (which reduces the frequency of attacks). Colchicine helps with mucocutaneous disease. For severe, vision-threatening ocular disease or refractory cases, anti-TNF agents (adalimumab and infliximab) have shown excellent results in controlling retinal vasculitis and preventing visual loss. Interferon alpha-2a has also been used, particularly in Europe, with good efficacy for ocular Behcet's.

Posterior Uveitis and Toxoplasmosis

UGPG
▼

Toxoplasma gondii is the most common cause of posterior uveitis worldwide. The organism is an obligate intracellular protozoan whose definitive host is the cat. Humans become infected by ingesting oocysts from cat faeces (via contaminated soil, water, or unwashed vegetables) or by eating tissue cysts in undercooked meat. Congenital infection occurs when a pregnant woman acquires a primary infection and the tachyzoites cross the placenta.

In congenital toxoplasmosis, the posterior pole is affected bilaterally — old chorioretinal scars with pigmented edges are seen at the macula, causing severe vision loss from birth. Intracranial calcification (seen on CT) and hydrocephalus may be present. This is distinct from the reactivation disease seen in adults.

The classic adult presentation of toxoplasma retinochoroiditis is reactivation at the edge of an old chorioretinal scar. The old scar appears as a well-defined area of retinal atrophy with hyperpigmented edges. The new active lesion is a fluffy, white, raised area of retinitis immediately adjacent to this old scar — this is the "headlight in fog" appearance. The "fog" is the overlying vitreous haze from the inflammatory response to active Toxoplasma replication. This visual description is the most exam-tested feature of toxoplasma retinitis.

Diagnosis is mainly clinical — the fundoscopic picture combined with positive toxoplasma IgG serology (which is present in most adults as evidence of past infection) is sufficient in the typical presentation. IgM positivity suggests recent or primary infection. Atypical presentations may require PCR of vitreous or aqueous humour for Toxoplasma DNA. The clinical scenario of a fluffy active white lesion next to a pigmented scar, with overlying vitreous haze, is essentially pathognomonic and does not need further testing before commencing treatment.

Treatment is indicated for lesions threatening the macula, optic disc, or major vessels, and for large or severe lesions. The classical regimen is pyrimethamine (loading dose then 25 mg daily) plus sulfadiazine (4 g daily in divided doses) plus folinic acid (to prevent pyrimethamine-induced bone marrow suppression). Oral prednisolone is added after 24 to 48 hours of antiparasitic therapy to control the inflammatory response, but never given alone as it would allow unchecked organism replication. Cotrimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) is a simpler, well-tolerated alternative regimen. Intravitreal clindamycin is used when systemic therapy is contraindicated or for focal lesions requiring targeted treatment.

Intermediate Uveitis and Cystoid Macular Oedema

UGPG
▼

Intermediate uveitis describes inflammation primarily affecting the vitreous, pars plana, and peripheral retina. The classic presentation is a young adult with floaters and mild blurred vision, often bilateral, with a relatively quiet anterior segment. The hallmark findings are vitreous cells and inflammatory debris, with specific patterns at the peripheral fundus.

Snowballs are round white aggregates of inflammatory cells floating in the vitreous, typically visible in the inferior vitreous. Snowbanking is a more advanced finding — a white fibrovascular exudate coating the inferior pars plana and peripheral retina like a bank of snow. Snowbanking is pathognomonic of pars planitis (the idiopathic form of intermediate uveitis) and represents progressive fibrovascular proliferation. It can lead to peripheral traction and retinal detachment.

Always screen intermediate uveitis patients for multiple sclerosis and sarcoidosis. MS-associated uveitis is intermediate in over 90% of cases, and some estimates suggest that 5 to 10% of intermediate uveitis patients have MS. MRI brain (looking for demyelinating plaques) and chest X-ray (for sarcoid hilar nodes) are part of the workup. Lyme disease is another association in endemic regions.

The most important complication of intermediate uveitis is cystoid macular oedema (CMO), which accounts for most of the visual loss in this condition. CMO develops from breakdown of the inner blood-retinal barrier (Muller cells, inner retinal capillaries) with accumulation of fluid in the outer plexiform and inner nuclear layers, forming cysts in a characteristic petaloid pattern around the fovea. On OCT it appears as intraretinal cyst-like spaces. On fluorescein angiography (FFA), the petaloid pattern of late leakage is classic.

Treatment of intermediate uveitis and CMO begins with posterior sub-Tenon's injections of triamcinolone acetonide — a depot steroid placed in the sub-Tenon's space that achieves high local concentrations in the vitreous and posterior segment without systemic side effects. Oral prednisolone is used for severe bilateral disease. Intravitreal triamcinolone or dexamethasone implants (Ozurdex) can be used for refractory CMO. Anti-VEGF injections (bevacizumab, ranibizumab) help control the vascular component of CMO. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide orally or brinzolamide topically) have a modest but real effect on reducing macular oedema from uveitis through a mechanism independent of anti-VEGF or steroid pathways. Vitrectomy is considered for dense vitreous debris limiting vision, or for snowbanking causing peripheral traction.

Three clinical skills that form the backbone of every uveitis consultation — anterior chamber examination with grading, fundus assessment, and the systematic workup. Mastering these at the bedside separates the good clinician from the average one.

Slit Lamp Anterior Chamber Examination in Uveitis Examination Technique
1
Setup for Cell and Flare Assessment

Dim the room lights completely — this is not optional. You need maximum contrast between the slit beam and the dark AC to see cells. Set the slit beam to a narrow, bright, square section approximately 1 mm wide by 1 mm tall (a 1 mm x 1 mm field). Angle the illumination column at 45 to 60 degrees from the observation axis. Focus the beam through the pupil into the anterior chamber so that it cuts through the aqueous between the posterior cornea and the anterior lens. Use medium-to-high magnification (16x to 25x).

Ask the patient to look straight ahead and blink normally. Patients who stop blinking develop corneal tear film changes that scatter the slit beam and mimic mild flare. Count cells in the illuminated column, not outside it.
2
Grading Cells — SUN Classification

Count the white cells visible in the 1 mm x 1 mm beam in the anterior chamber. The SUN grading is: Grade 0 (fewer than 1 cell per field), Trace (1 to 5), 1+ (6 to 15), 2+ (16 to 25), 3+ (26 to 50), 4+ (more than 50 cells). In practice, most examiners use trace, 1+, 2+, 3+, and 4+. Record what you see clearly. A hypopyon is a formal layering of cells at the inferior AC — always measure and record the height in millimetres.

3
Grading Flare

Flare is the Tyndall effect — the slit beam becomes visible as a luminous column (like a torch beam in fog) when protein leaks into the aqueous. SUN flare grading: Grade 0 (absent), 1+ (faint), 2+ (moderate, iris and lens details clear), 3+ (marked, iris and lens hazy), 4+ (intense, fibrinous exudate or plastic aqueous). High flare with relatively few cells suggests a chronic or treated uveitis with persistent blood-aqueous barrier breakdown but less active cellular response. High cells with low flare suggests an acute episode.

Flare and cells are graded independently and both should be recorded. "1+ cells, 2+ flare" tells the next clinician much more than "mild uveitis."
4
Examining KPs and Iris Nodules

After grading cells and flare, examine the corneal endothelium systematically from superior to inferior looking for KPs. Note their size, shape, distribution, and whether they are fresh (white, fluffy edges) or old (pigmented, fibrotic). Large mutton-fat KPs in the inferior endothelium immediately suggest granulomatous disease. Fine dusted KPs in Arlt's triangle suggest non-granulomatous. Then examine the iris surface for nodules. Koeppe nodules at the pupil margin are seen in both granulomatous and non-granulomatous uveitis. Busacca nodules in the iris stroma (not at the pupil margin) are granulomatous only. Document their size, number, and location.

5
Assessing Posterior Synechiae and Pupil

Dilate the pupil with cyclopentolate 1% (and phenylephrine 2.5% if safe). Observe how the pupil dilates under the microscope with the slit lamp. Posterior synechiae appear as areas where the pupil margin remains tethered to the lens, creating a festooned or irregular pupil margin. If synechiae are present over 360 degrees, the pupil may not dilate at all — this is seclusion of the pupil (seclusio pupillae). If fibrinous membrane also blocks the pupil, it is occlusion of the pupil (occlusio pupillae). Examine the angle with gonioscopy if raised IOP or peripheral anterior synechiae are suspected.

Always check the IOP before dilating — a secluded or occluded pupil can cause acute IOP spikes that need urgent treatment before you start dilating and potentially making things worse.
Fundus Examination for Posterior Uveitis Examination Technique
1
Indirect Ophthalmoscopy Technique

Dilate the pupil adequately with tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5%. Use the binocular indirect ophthalmoscope (BIO) at 20 D or 28 D lens for initial survey. Position the lens vertically in front of the patient's pupil with the concave side toward you. The image is inverted and reversed — superior retina appears at the bottom of the image. Scan methodically from disc to periphery in each quadrant. Dim the room to reduce reflections. Use the 20 D for better resolution and the 28 D for wider field and better peripheral access in undilated or poorly dilating pupils.

2
Identifying Active Uveitis Lesions

Active chorioretinal lesions appear as fluffy white or cream-coloured areas with indistinct borders, often with surrounding retinal oedema and overlying vitreous haze. Old scars have defined pigmented borders, a pale atrophic centre, and no surrounding oedema. The contrast between the active lesion and the adjacent old scar is the "headlight in fog" picture of toxoplasma reactivation — look for a white active satellite lesion immediately adjacent to an old pigmented scar. This combination, nowhere else in ophthalmology, is almost diagnostic of toxoplasma.

If vitreous haze is severe and you cannot see the fundus clearly, B-scan ultrasonography should be performed to assess for retinal detachment and to get some sense of posterior segment integrity before inflammation is treated.
3
Dalen-Fuchs Nodules and Fundus Findings of VKH

In acute VKH, indirect ophthalmoscopy shows multiple discrete yellowish subretinal deposits scattered throughout the posterior pole — these are Dalen-Fuchs nodules. Multiple serous retinal detachments (bullous in severe cases) are present bilaterally. The disc is hyperaemic and oedematous. In the convalescent phase, the fundus depigments — the "sunset glow fundus" appearance (brilliant orange-red choroidal glow without the usual pigmentation). Discrete small white subretinal lesions (Sugiura spots at the peripapillary region) and chorioretinal scars develop over time.

4
Retinal Vasculitis Findings in Behcet's and TB

Retinal vasculitis manifests as perivascular sheathing (white cuffs along vessel walls — inflammatory infiltrate around the vessel) and, in occlusive disease, haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots. In Behcet's, the vasculitis is occlusive and affects both arteries and veins — look for haemorrhages, disc swelling, and neovascularisation from ischaemia. Periphlebitis (venous sheathing with perivascular exudates) is the hallmark of TB retinitis — sometimes described as "candle wax drippings," though this term is more accurately applied to sarcoidosis. Fluorescein angiography (FFA) greatly assists in defining the extent of vascular leakage, ischaemia, and neovascularisation.

Systemic Workup for Uveitis Investigative Protocol
1
History-Directed Investigation

The workup should be guided by the clinical presentation rather than ordered as a blanket panel. A young man with acute unilateral anterior non-granulomatous uveitis and back pain needs HLA-B27 and sacroiliac joint X-ray, not a full immunology panel. A 40-year-old with bilateral granulomatous posterior uveitis and breathlessness needs ACE, chest X-ray, and possibly HRCT thorax for sarcoid. The targeted approach is more efficient and less costly. That said, there are reasonable baseline investigations for most cases of non-trivial uveitis.

2
Baseline Blood Tests

Full blood count (FBC) and differential (eosinophilia for Toxocara, Behcet's systemic disease monitoring). ESR and CRP (general inflammatory markers — elevated in active disease, useful for monitoring). ANA (systemic lupus, JIA-associated uveitis in children). ANCA — c-ANCA (PR3) for Wegener's, p-ANCA (MPO) for PAN. Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies. Serum ACE — elevated in sarcoidosis (60% sensitivity). HLA-B27 testing for suspected seronegative spondyloarthropathy. Random blood glucose (diabetic patients have higher infection risk and different differential).

Serum lysozyme has been used as an alternative to ACE for sarcoidosis screening, with similar sensitivity but slightly different specificity. In some laboratories it is ordered alongside ACE.
3
Infectious Disease Serology

VDRL or RPR as syphilis screening test — positive results should be confirmed with FTA-ABS (fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption test), which remains positive even after treatment and is the most sensitive treponemal test. TPHA is another confirmatory test. Toxoplasma IgG and IgM — IgG positive (past exposure) in most adults, particularly in high-prevalence regions; IgM suggests recent infection. Toxocara ELISA for children with white peripheral lesions or eosinophilia. CMV serology in immunocompromised patients or atypical retinitis. IGRA (interferon gamma release assay — QuantiFERON-TB Gold) for latent TB, more specific than Mantoux in BCG-vaccinated individuals.

4
Imaging and Ocular Investigations

Chest X-ray — bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (sarcoid), upper zone fibrosis or calcification (TB), Ghon's focus. HRCT thorax if chest X-ray is equivocal and sarcoid/TB is suspected. Sacroiliac joint X-ray (bamboo spine, sacroiliitis) for HLA-B27 disease. MRI brain and spine if MS or CNS vasculitis is suspected. Fluorescein angiography (FFA) — maps vascular leakage, defines CMO, identifies ischaemic zones and neovascularisation. OCT — quantifies macular oedema, monitors CMO response to treatment. B-scan ultrasonography — when media is too hazy for fundal assessment, and for posterior scleritis. Diagnostic vitreous tap with PCR panel for infectious uveitis where clinical diagnosis is uncertain.

HLA typing (B27, B51, A29, DR4) should be ordered when a specific disease is suspected and the result would change management. HLA-B51 testing in a Middle Eastern young male with bilateral recurrent hypopyon uveitis and oral ulcers strongly supports Behcet's and would guide aggressive systemic treatment.

1
Describe the clinical features and management of anterior uveitis.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition and Classification

Anterior uveitis is inflammation of the iris (iritis), ciliary body (iridocyclitis), or both. It is the most common form of uveitis, classified as granulomatous or non-granulomatous, and acute or chronic.

Clinical Features — Symptoms

Acute: sudden onset pain (deep aching, photophobia from ciliary spasm), redness (circumcorneal injection/ciliary flush), blurred vision, lacrimation. Chronic: may be relatively asymptomatic with the eye appearing quiet while complications develop insidiously (cataract, band keratopathy, glaucoma, CMO).

Clinical Features — Signs

Circumcorneal injection (ciliary flush — maximal at limbus, fades peripherally). Corneal: fine Descemet's folds; KPs in Arlt's triangle (stellate = non-granulomatous; mutton-fat = granulomatous). Anterior chamber: cells (SUN grading 0 to 4+) and flare (Tyndall effect). Pupil: miosis from sphincter spasm; irregular from posterior synechiae; festooned appearance on dilation. Iris: Koeppe nodules at pupil margin (both types), Busacca nodules in stroma (granulomatous only), rubeosis in severe disease. Lens: fibrinous exudate on anterior capsule; PSC cataract in chronic disease. IOP: reduced early (ciliary shutdown) or raised (trabeculitis, steroid, pupil block).

Aetiology

Non-granulomatous: HLA-B27 diseases (PAIR), Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis, herpes simplex iridocyclitis. Granulomatous: TB, sarcoidosis, VKH, syphilis, leprosy. Idiopathic (30 to 40%).

Investigations

Single episode mild: no workup. Recurrent/bilateral/granulomatous: FBC, ESR, ANA, HLA-B27, chest X-ray, Mantoux/IGRA, ACE, VDRL/FTA-ABS, ANCA.

Management

Cycloplegics: Atropine 1% (severe) or cyclopentolate 1% (moderate) — prevents posterior synechiae and relieves ciliary spasm. Topical steroids: Prednisolone acetate 1% hourly initially, tapering to twice daily as inflammation resolves. Systemic steroids: For bilateral, severe, or treatment-resistant disease — prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day. Treat underlying systemic disease. Complications management: Band keratopathy (EDTA chelation), raised IOP (aqueous suppressants — avoid prostaglandins in active uveitis), cataract (plan surgery in a quiet eye), CMO (sub-Tenon's steroid, intravitreal agents).

Describe Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada disease — its aetiology, clinical features, and management.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition and Epidemiology

VKH is a bilateral granulomatous panuveitis with systemic involvement of melanocyte-containing tissues. More common in East Asian, Japanese, South Asian, Latin American, and Middle Eastern populations. HLA-DR4 association. Autoimmune T-cell attack against melanocytes.

Clinical Phases

Prodromal phase: Headache, meningism (neck stiffness, photophobia), CSF pleocytosis, dysacusis (tinnitus, hearing loss), vertigo — resembles viral meningitis. Acute uveitic phase: Bilateral diffuse choroiditis, multiple serous retinal detachments (bullous), anterior granulomatous uveitis with mutton-fat KPs, disc oedema, Dalen-Fuchs nodules (RPE granulomas between Bruch's membrane and RPE). Convalescent phase: Dermatological features — poliosis (whitening of hair, eyelashes, eyebrows), vitiligo (skin depigmentation), alopecia; "sunset glow fundus" (fundus depigmentation). Chronic recurrent phase: Recurrent anterior uveitis; chorioretinal atrophy; Sugiura sign (peripapillary depigmentation).

Diagnosis

Clinical — Bilateral acute uveitis with systemic features (meningeal + dermatological). FFA: early multiple punctate hyperfluorescent spots from choroidal inflammation; late subretinal pooling in bullous serous RD. OCT: subretinal fluid, choroidal thickening (enhanced depth imaging). B-scan: bilateral serous RD. ICGA: choroidal hypofluorescence from choroidal vessel occlusion. CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis (prodromal phase).

Management

Immediate high-dose systemic corticosteroids: Prednisolone 1 to 1.5 mg/kg/day (oral) or IV methylprednisolone pulse (1 g/day for 3 days) for severe disease. Taper very slowly over 6 to 12 months — rapid taper causes relapse. Steroid-sparing immunosuppressives: Azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclosporin A for corticosteroid-dependent or refractory cases. Monitor dermatological and auditory manifestations. Regular follow-up OCT for subclinical fluid. Prognosis good if treated early and aggressively; poor if treatment delayed or inadequate, leading to subretinal fibrosis and permanent vision loss.

Describe toxoplasma retinochoroiditis — its pathogenesis, clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Organism and Life Cycle

Toxoplasma gondii — obligate intracellular protozoan. Definitive host: cat (sexual reproduction, oocysts shed in faeces). Intermediate hosts: warm-blooded animals including humans (asexual tissue cysts in muscle, brain, retina). Human infection: ingestion of oocysts from soil/water/unwashed vegetables, or tissue cysts from undercooked meat. Congenital: transplacental transmission during primary maternal infection.

Pathogenesis

In humans, primary infection results in tissue cysts (bradyzoites) forming in the retina and other tissues. These cysts remain dormant for years. Reactivation occurs when immunity wanes — the cyst wall ruptures, releasing rapidly dividing tachyzoites that invade adjacent retinal cells, causing focal necrotic retinochoroiditis. The inflammatory response (vitreous haze) is driven by the immune reaction to tachyzoite antigens.

Clinical Features

Congenital: Bilateral posterior pole chorioretinal scars (punched-out, hyperpigmented edges) — often at the macula causing congenital vision loss. Associated intracranial calcification, hydrocephalus, seizures. Acquired (adult reactivation): Unilateral; active fluffy white retinitis adjacent to old pigmented scar — "headlight in fog" (active satellite lesion); moderate-to-severe vitreous haze; anterior AC reaction (satellite cells). Active lesion is well demarcated from old scar in typical cases. Immune-compromised patients: Atypical large multifocal bilateral lesions without the typical adjacent scar pattern.

Diagnosis

Clinical (usually sufficient): Characteristic fundoscopic picture. Serology: Toxoplasma IgG (positive in most adults = past exposure, supports diagnosis); IgM (positive = recent/primary). PCR on aqueous or vitreous for toxoplasma DNA in atypical cases. FFA: Early hypofluorescence (necrosis blocks), late staining of active lesion edges. OCT: Retinal thickening, hyperreflectivity of active lesion.

Treatment

Indications: Lesions threatening macula or optic disc, large or severe lesions, immunocompromised patients. Regimen: Pyrimethamine 200 mg loading dose then 25 mg daily + sulfadiazine 1 g four times daily + folinic acid 15 mg three times weekly. Add oral prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg/day after 24 to 48 hours. Duration: 4 to 6 weeks. Alternatives: Cotrimoxazole (trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole) — simpler, fewer side effects. Prophylaxis: Cotrimoxazole in immunocompromised for secondary prophylaxis.

4
Describe sympathetic ophthalmia — its aetiology, clinical features, pathology, and management.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition

Sympathetic ophthalmia (SO) is a bilateral diffuse granulomatous panuveitis that develops after penetrating injury or surgery to one eye (the exciting eye). The uninjured fellow eye is called the sympathizing eye. It is immune-mediated, rare but potentially blinding, and carries a minimum latent period of 2 weeks.

Aetiology and Pathogenesis

Penetrating injury disrupts the blood-ocular barrier, exposing previously sequestered uveal melanocyte antigens (particularly retinal S-antigen and interphotoreceptor retinoid-binding protein) to the systemic immune system. T-cell sensitisation occurs in regional lymph nodes. These sensitised T-cells then traffic to the sympathizing eye and attack uveal melanocytes bilaterally, causing granulomatous panuveitis. HLA-DRB1 and HLA-A11 associations have been identified.

Incidence and Latent Period

Rare — approximately 0.2 to 0.5% after penetrating trauma, lower after surgery. Latent period: Never less than 2 weeks (minimum); peak at 4 to 8 weeks (80% within 3 months); can occur years to decades later. The 2-week minimum is the critical exam fact.

Clinical Features

Exciting eye: Signs of penetrating injury, often poor vision. Sympathizing eye: Bilateral granulomatous panuveitis — circumcorneal injection, mutton-fat KPs, cells and flare in AC, posterior synechiae, vitreous cells and haze, disc oedema, choroidal thickening, serous retinal detachment, Dalen-Fuchs nodules (RPE granulomas — pathognomonic). The sympathizing eye may develop uveitis insidiously or acutely. Without treatment, progressive and repeated inflammation leads to phthisis bulbi.

Pathology

Diffuse granulomatous uveal infiltration with lymphocytes, epithelioid cells, and giant cells. Dalen-Fuchs nodules between Bruch's membrane and RPE. RPE hyperplasia. Choroidal thickening. No retinal involvement initially (choriocapillaris spared early in most cases).

Management

Prophylaxis: Enucleation of the exciting eye within 2 weeks of injury if no visual potential — this is the only effective prevention. After 2 weeks, enucleation does not prevent SO. Treatment of established SO: High-dose systemic prednisolone (1 to 1.5 mg/kg/day, slow taper over 6 to 12 months minimum). Steroid-sparing immunosuppressives (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporin) for long-term management. The exciting eye should be preserved if it retains vision, as the sympathizing eye may eventually do worse than the exciting eye. Topical steroids and cycloplegics for anterior segment control alongside systemic treatment.

What is the minimum latent period for development of sympathetic ophthalmia after penetrating eye injury?
NEET PG
Show Answer
Answer: 2 weeks (14 days). Sympathetic ophthalmia never occurs in less than 2 weeks after a penetrating injury. This minimum period reflects the time required for T-cell sensitisation to uveal antigens, clonal expansion, and trafficking to the sympathizing eye. Enucleation of the exciting eye within this 2-week window can prevent SO from developing. After 2 weeks, if the exciting eye has no visual potential, the decision to enucleate must weigh the risk of developing SO against retaining the eye for psychological reasons. Describe the "headlight in fog" appearance in ocular toxoplasmosis. What are the treatment options?

Answer: The "headlight in fog" describes the fundoscopic appearance of reactivated toxoplasma retinochoroiditis. The "headlight" is the active, fluffy, white retinal lesion (reactivated toxoplasma replication causing focal necrotic retinochoroiditis). It characteristically occurs adjacent to, or at the edge of, an old well-demarcated chorioretinal scar with hyperpigmented borders (from a previous episode). Treatment is indicated when the macula or disc is threatened, or for large lesions.

A patient with VKH disease in the convalescent phase is likely to show which fundoscopic findings?

Answer: Sunset glow fundus and Dalen-Fuchs nodules. In the convalescent phase of VKH, the acute bilateral serous retinal detachments have resolved, and the fundus develops characteristic depigmentation. What is the ABCDE rule for systemic workup in posterior uveitis?

Answer: The ABCDE mnemonic guides the systemic investigation of posterior uveitis: A = ANCA (Wegener's/GPA — c-ANCA; vasculitis), ACE level and serum lysozyme (sarcoidosis); B = Blood counts (FBC, eosinophilia for Toxocara), VDRL/RPR (syphilis screening); C = Chest X-ray (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy in sarcoid, upper zone TB, Ghon's focus), HRCT thorax if equivocal; D = Diabetes (glucose, HbA1c), Diagnostic vitreous tap + PCR when infectious cause suspected and clinical diagnosis uncertain; E = ELISA for Toxoplasma IgG/IgM, FTA-ABS for syphilis (more sensitive than VDRL). Describe the clinical features of Behcet's disease with special reference to ocular involvement and management.
DNB
Show Answer
Answer: Behcet's disease is a systemic vasculitis with HLA-B51 association, predominantly affecting young males of Turkish, Middle Eastern, and East Asian descent. The diagnostic triad comprises: (1) Recurrent oral aphthous ulcers (major criterion); (2) Genital ulcers; (3) Ocular inflammation. Additional features: Skin lesions (erythema nodosum, pseudofolliculitis, acneiform lesions), arthritis, CNS involvement (meningoencephalitis, venous sinus thrombosis), and gut involvement. Pathergy test positive (pustule at needle prick site within 24 to 48 hours). Ocular involvement: Bilateral, recurrent, severe. Anterior: Non-granulomatous or minimal KPs, shifting hypopyon (moves with head position, non-infectious). Posterior: Occlusive retinal vasculitis (veins and arteries), retinal haemorrhages, vitreous haze, neovascularisation from ischaemia, disc oedema and eventual atrophy. Prognosis: Without treatment, 25% legally blind within 5 years. Management: Oral prednisolone for acute attacks; azathioprine for frequency reduction (maintenance); colchicine for mucocutaneous disease; anti-TNF agents (adalimumab, infliximab) for severe refractory ocular Behcet's — these have significantly improved visual prognosis; interferon alpha-2a is an alternative biologic used in some centres.
