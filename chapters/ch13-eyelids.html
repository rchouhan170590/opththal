Chapter 13

Diseases of the Eyelids

Ptosis classification and surgical management, entropion, ectropion, trichiasis, blepharitis, chalazion, hordeolum, and eyelid tumours including the malignant ones that matter most.

UG PG

The eyelid chapter covers a lot of ground — ptosis alone has enough depth to fill a viva by itself. Use these cards to nail the high-frequency exam facts before you go into the detailed notes.

Ptosis Classification

Ptosis is drooping of the upper lid below its normal position covering the upper 1 to 2 mm of the cornea. The classification is aetiological and directly drives management.

  • Neurogenic — CN III palsy (complete ptosis with ophthalmoplegia and mydriasis); Horner's syndrome (partial 2 mm ptosis with miosis and anhidrosis from sympathetic disruption of Müller's muscle)
  • Myogenic — Congenital (dystropic levator muscle with fibrofatty replacement, poor levator function); Myasthenia gravis (variable ptosis worse with fatigue, Cogan's lid twitch); Myotonic dystrophy (bilateral ptosis with frontalis overaction)
  • Aponeurotic — Senile (most common in elderly; levator aponeurosis disinsertion; normal levator function; deep superior sulcus; thin upper lid)
  • Mechanical — Lid tumour, haemangioma, or swelling weighing down the lid
  • Traumatic — Direct damage to levator, aponeurosis, or third nerve
High-Yield

Ptosis Assessment Keys

Before any ptosis surgery, three measurements determine both severity and surgical approach. Get these right and the operation plans itself.

  • MRD1 (Margin Reflex Distance) — distance from corneal light reflex to upper lid margin in primary gaze; normal 4 to 5 mm; below 2 mm indicates significant ptosis
  • Levator Function (LF) — excursion of upper lid from full downgaze to full upgaze with frontalis blocked by thumb; normal more than 10 mm; good 8 to 10 mm; fair 4 to 7 mm; poor below 4 mm (frontalis sling required)
  • Bell's Phenomenon — upward and outward rolling of the eye on attempted lid closure; protective reflex that shields the cornea; absent Bell's is a contraindication to aggressive ptosis surgery as exposure keratopathy risk is very high
High-Yield

Entropion vs Ectropion

  • Entropion — lid margin turns inward; lashes contact the cornea causing trichiasis and eventually keratitis; involutional (most common in elderly — horizontal laxity and overriding pretarsal orbicularis), spastic (blepharospasm), cicatricial (trachoma, burns, OCP — tarsal scarring pulls lid in)
  • Ectropion — lid margin turns outward; exposure keratopathy and epiphora result; involutional (most common — medial ectropion from medial lid laxity), paralytic (CN VII palsy), cicatricial (vertical skin shortage from burns or surgery), mechanical (tumour)

In both conditions the lower lid is affected far more often than the upper. The direction of the turn determines the symptoms: inward means corneal irritation; outward means exposure and watering.

Chalazion vs Hordeolum

  • Chalazion — chronic lipogranulomatous inflammation of a Meibomian gland; painless firm lump within the tarsal plate; no pointing; no lash loss; eversion shows red congested area posteriorly; treatment is warm compresses then incision and curettage from the conjunctival side
  • External hordeolum (stye) — acute infection of a Zeis or Moll gland at the lash margin; tender, red, points toward skin; warm compresses and topical antibiotic; epilation of central lash helps drainage
  • Internal hordeolum — abscess of a Meibomian gland; points toward conjunctival surface; more painful than external; systemic antibiotics and incision from conjunctival side

A recurrent chalazion always deserves a biopsy — sebaceous gland carcinoma is a notorious masquerader.

High-Yield

Eyelid Tumour Rule

Three malignant lid tumours dominate the exam. Know them by their signature features — the distinctions between them are frequently tested.

  • BCC — most common (90 to 95%); lower lid and medial canthus; pearly nodule with telangiectasia and rolled edges; lashes are destroyed (madarosis); rodent ulcer if neglected; treatment is excision with clear margins or Mohs micrographic surgery
  • SCC — less common; hyperkeratotic plaque or ulcer; can metastasise to regional nodes; wide excision required
  • SGC (sebaceous gland carcinoma) — upper lid predominance (Meibomian glands); yellow waxy appearance; mimics chalazion or chronic unilateral blepharitis; pagetoid spread through conjunctival epithelium; must do map biopsies; poor prognosis if delayed
Mnemonic — BBC Rule for BCC
Basal cell from lower lid, Breaks lashes (madarosis), Canthus medial favoured site. SGC breaks the rules — upper lid, yellow, and behaves like a chameleon.

Ptosis Surgery — Algorithm by Levator Function

1
Poor levator function (below 4 mm) — The levator muscle is too weak to use. Frontalis sling procedure suspends the lid from the frontalis muscle using fascia lata (autologous, from thigh) or silicone rod. The patient lifts the lid by raising their brows. Used for congenital ptosis with poor LF and for severe myogenic ptosis.
2
Fair levator function (4 to 7 mm) — Enough muscle to work with but not ideal. Levator resection (shortening the muscle by excising a measured segment) raises the lid. The amount resected depends on the degree of ptosis and the LF: the worse the function, the more needs to be resected for the same lid elevation.
3
Good levator function (above 7 mm) — Minimal ptosis with good function. Müller's muscle resection (Fasanella-Servat procedure) is sufficient: a strip of conjunctiva, Müller's muscle, and a sliver of tarsus is excised from the posterior lid surface. Effective for 1 to 2 mm ptosis correction, especially in Horner's syndrome (phenylephrine test positive predicts good result).
4
Aponeurotic ptosis (senile) — Levator function is good (above 10 mm) but the aponeurosis has disinserted or thinned. Treatment is levator aponeurosis repair — the disinserted aponeurosis is reattached or plicated (folded and sutured) to the tarsal plate. Excellent results with the most physiological repair of any ptosis surgery.

Eyelid Anatomy

UGPG
▼

Layers of the Eyelid

The eyelid is a deceptively complex structure for something so thin. From anterior to posterior there are seven layers: skin, subcutaneous areolar tissue, orbicularis oculi muscle, orbital septum, pre-aponeurotic fat pads, retractors, and conjunctiva. The skin of the eyelid is the thinnest in the body, which is why oedema and haematomas spread so dramatically after trauma or surgery.

The orbicularis oculi is the protractor of the lids — it closes them. It is innervated by the facial nerve (CN VII) and has three functional zones: the orbital portion (forceful closure), the preseptal portion (gentle voluntary closure), and the pretarsal portion (involuntary blinking, lacrimal pump function). Loss of orbicularis function from CN VII palsy is the basis of lagophthalmos.

The Tarsal Plates

The tarsal plates are dense fibrous structures that give the lids their rigidity and shape. The upper tarsus measures approximately 10 mm in vertical height; the lower tarsus is 5 mm. Embedded within the tarsal substance are the Meibomian glands — modified sebaceous glands that secrete the lipid layer of the tear film. There are about 25 to 30 Meibomian glands in the upper tarsus and 20 to 25 in the lower. Their orifices open at the posterior lid margin just anterior to the mucocutaneous junction (the grey line).

Upper Lid Retractors

Two muscles elevate the upper lid. The levator palpebrae superioris is the main retractor, innervated by the superior division of CN III. It originates from the lesser wing of sphenoid above the annulus of Zinn, runs forward in the orbit above superior rectus, then becomes the levator aponeurosis which fans out to insert into the anterior surface of the tarsus and the skin of the upper lid crease. The aponeurosis also sends expansions (horns) medially and laterally to the orbital walls, and the lateral horn splits the lacrimal gland into orbital and palpebral lobes.

The second retractor is Müller's muscle (superior tarsal muscle), innervated by sympathetic fibres. It originates from the undersurface of the levator muscle and inserts into the superior border of the tarsus. It contributes about 2 mm of lid elevation, which is why Horner's syndrome (sympathetic disruption) causes exactly 2 mm of ptosis. The pharmacological test exploits this: topical phenylephrine (an alpha-1 agonist) stimulates Müller's muscle, elevating the lid — if this corrects the ptosis, Müller's muscle resection will work well.

Lower Lid Retractors and Cilia

The lower lid retractors are the inferior tarsal muscle (sympathetic, analogous to Müller's) and the capsulopalpebral fascia (analogous to the levator aponeurosis). Together they keep the lower lid retracted about 5 mm below the inferior corneal limbus. Weakness in these structures contributes to involutional entropion.

The cilia (eyelashes) number 100 to 150 in the upper lid and 50 to 75 in the lower. They are arranged in two to three rows and are replaced every 5 to 6 months. The Zeis glands (modified sebaceous) and Moll's glands (modified apocrine sweat glands) open into the lash follicles — these are the glands that become infected in external hordeolum.

Ptosis — Assessment and Management

UGPG
▼

Congenital Ptosis

Congenital ptosis is almost always myogenic, resulting from a dystropic levator muscle where normal muscle fibres are replaced by fibrofatty tissue. The levator cannot generate normal excursion and — equally telling — it cannot relax fully, so the upper lid rides higher on downgaze than normal (lid lag in downgaze is characteristic of myogenic ptosis, unlike aponeurotic ptosis where the lid follows downgaze normally). Levator function is typically poor, often below 4 mm, so frontalis sling is the procedure of choice.

The most pressing clinical concern in a child with congenital ptosis is amblyopia. If the ptotic lid covers the visual axis, the eye is deprived of patterned visual stimulation during the critical period of visual development, and dense amblyopia develops. These children need urgent surgery — not elective correction — and aggressive amblyopia therapy postoperatively.

Aponeurotic Ptosis

Aponeurotic ptosis is the most common form in adults, particularly in the elderly. The levator aponeurosis — the tendinous extension of the levator muscle — gradually disinserts or thins. Since the muscle itself is intact, levator function is normal or near-normal (above 10 mm), but the mechanical connection between muscle and tarsus is lost. This produces a characteristic appearance: the ptotic lid is thin with a high, deep-set superior sulcus, the lid crease is elevated or even absent, and you can see through the thin lid tissue (translucency of the thin aponeurosis is sometimes visible on slit lamp). The same disinsertion can be caused acutely by prolonged hard contact lens wear, previous intraocular surgery, or trauma to the superior fornix.

Neurogenic Ptosis — CN III and Horner's

A complete third nerve palsy produces complete ptosis (the levator is totally denervated) alongside ophthalmoplegia (SR, MR, IR, IO all palsy), mydriasis (from loss of parasympathetic pupillomotor fibres), and sometimes aberrant regeneration if the cause is compressive rather than ischaemic. The most feared cause of a CN III palsy with pupil involvement is a posterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing the nerve — this is a neurosurgical emergency.

Horner's syndrome produces a triad of partial ptosis (2 mm, from Müller's muscle), miosis (from loss of sympathetic dilator pupillae tone), and anhidrosis of the ipsilateral face. The ptosis in Horner's is often subtle and may be missed. The inverse ptosis of the lower lid (the lower lid rides slightly higher than normal because the inferior tarsal muscle is also paralysed) is a useful confirmatory sign. Pharmacological testing with 4% cocaine (or 0.5% apraclonidine) confirms Horner's; hydroxyamphetamine 1% localises the lesion to pre- or post-ganglionic level.

Myasthenia Gravis

Myasthenia gravis deserves special attention because it can mimic any pattern of ptosis or ophthalmoplegia, and it can change from hour to hour. The classic lid sign is Cogan's lid twitch: when the patient looks down for 10 to 15 seconds and then quickly returns to primary gaze, the ptotic lid momentarily overshoots (twitches upward) before settling. This represents temporary acetylcholine accumulation at the neuromuscular junction during the downgaze rest, which then briefly overcomes the block on return to primary gaze. The ice test (applying ice to the closed lid for 2 minutes) improves ptosis in myasthenia because cooling slows acetylcholinesterase activity. The Tensilon (edrophonium) test or neostigmine test gives dramatic but brief improvement by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. AChR (anti-acetylcholine receptor) antibodies are positive in 85 to 90% of generalised myasthenia; anti-MuSK antibodies cover most of the seronegative cases. Management: treat the systemic disease first (pyridostigmine, steroids, thymectomy); only consider ptosis surgery after systemic control is achieved, as the variability makes surgical planning unreliable.

Never operate on ptosis without assessing Bell's phenomenon. In a patient with absent Bell's, even modest undercorrection of the ptosis can leave the cornea dangerously exposed during sleep. If you must operate, deliberately undercorrect and prescribe lubricants at night.

Surgical Management

The surgical approach is entirely determined by levator function, as described in the snapshot algorithm. A few practical points worth emphasising: levator resection amounts are guided by tables (Beard's tables) that correlate the amount of resection with the degree of ptosis and levator function, but experienced surgeons adjust based on intraoperative lid height (under local anaesthesia, you can ask the patient to look straight ahead and titrate the correction in real time). Under general anaesthesia in children, you aim to overcorrect slightly, knowing that some lid drop occurs as anaesthetic wears off and oedema resolves. For frontalis sling, fascia lata harvested from the thigh is the gold standard material — it has the best long-term durability. Silicone rods are used in young children where fascia lata is not yet adequate in quantity, and they can be adjusted postoperatively.

Entropion and Ectropion

UGPG
▼

Involutional Entropion

Involutional (senile) entropion of the lower lid is the result of three concurrent age-related changes: horizontal lid laxity (the lid is loose from its medial and lateral canthal tendon attachments), dehiscence of the lower lid retractors, and overriding of the preseptal orbicularis over the pretarsal orbicularis (the pretarsal orbicularis flips the lid margin inward). The lashes then rub against the cornea, causing punctate epithelial erosions, pain, and if neglected, corneal ulceration.

Emergency management while awaiting definitive surgery is to tape the lower lid to the cheek skin with micropore tape, everting it. Botulinum toxin injection into the lower lid orbicularis (Quickert's method) can provide temporary relief for months. Definitive treatment is the Quickert-Rathbun procedure: two or three full-thickness everting sutures placed through the lower lid to evert the lid margin and tighten the retractors. For more severe or recurrent cases, a lateral tarsal strip procedure (tightening the horizontal lid laxity at the lateral canthus) is combined with the everting sutures.

Cicatricial Entropion

In cicatricial entropion, scarring of the posterior lamella (conjunctiva and tarsus) shortens the posterior surface and pulls the lid margin inward. The classic cause is trachoma, but Stevens-Johnson syndrome, OCP (ocular cicatricial pemphigoid), chemical burns, and radiation all produce the same picture. Because the problem is tissue shortage, everting sutures alone will not work — you need to add posterior lamellar tissue. The standard repair involves a hard palate mucosal graft (or buccal mucosa graft) sutured into the posterior lamella to lengthen it and restore normal lid position.

Trichiasis

Trichiasis is misdirection of lashes toward the cornea, but with the lid margin in normal position — this distinguishes it from entropion where the entire margin is turned in. Treatment depends on the number of lashes involved. Epilation (pulling out the offending lashes) provides instant relief but lashes regrow in 4 to 6 weeks. For a few lashes, electrolysis (passing a current through a fine needle to destroy the follicle) or argon laser ablation of the follicle gives more lasting results. For extensive trichiasis (as in trachoma), cryotherapy with a double freeze-thaw cycle destroys follicles but must be applied carefully to avoid scarring.

Ectropion Types and Management

Involutional ectropion predominantly affects the medial lower lid. The medial canthal tendon weakens, allowing the punctum to fall away from the globe and producing epiphora from punctal malposition — the punctum no longer sits in the tear lake. The horizontal lid laxity compounds this. Management for purely medial ectropion is medial canthoplasty (tightening and repositioning the medial canthal tendon). Lateral lid laxity is addressed with a lateral tarsal strip procedure.

Paralytic ectropion (from CN VII palsy) presents with exposure keratopathy, inability to close the lid, and watering. Management depends on severity and likely duration. Lubricants and a moisture chamber (sealing the socket at night) protect the cornea. Botulinum toxin to the levator can deliberately produce ptosis as a corneal cover. Lateral tarsorrhaphy (suturing the lateral canthi together) reduces the palpebral aperture and protects the cornea for longer-term paralysis. Gold weight implantation into the upper lid works with gravity to close the eye and is reversible.

Cicatricial ectropion results from vertical shortage of the anterior lamella (skin) — burns, trauma, and over-aggressive lid surgery are the common causes. The contracted skin pulls the lid away from the globe. The treatment is to release the scar and add skin, either as a full-thickness skin graft from the upper lid, postauricular region, or supraclavicular area.

Distinguishing entropion from trichiasis at the slit lamp: In entropion the entire lid margin including the grey line is rotated inward. In trichiasis the lid margin sits normally but individual lashes point posteriorly. The distinction matters because the surgical approach is completely different.

Blepharitis

UGPG
▼

Anterior Blepharitis

Anterior blepharitis involves the anterior lid margin, the lash follicles, and the glands of Zeis and Moll. Two forms exist. Staphylococcal blepharitis causes collarette crusts — hard, dry deposits that encircle the base of the lashes like a sleeve. These are produced by fibrin from the inflammatory exudate. On removal, the underlying lid margin may be ulcerated. Chronic staphylococcal blepharitis leads to lash loss, distortion, and trichiasis, and is a potent cause of recurrent chalazia and marginal keratitis (phlyctenular disease from hypersensitivity to staphylococcal exotoxins). Seborrhoeic blepharitis produces greasy, waxy scales at the lash base — described as cylindrical dandruff — and is often associated with seborrhoeic dermatitis of the scalp and face.

Posterior Blepharitis — Meibomian Gland Dysfunction

Posterior blepharitis (Meibomian gland dysfunction, MGD) affects the Meibomian glands and the posterior lid margin. The gland secretion becomes thickened and inspissated, blocking the orifices. On examination the posterior lid margin shows telangiectasia, the orifices are capped with congealed secretion, and gentle pressure expresses turbid, toothpaste-like material instead of clear oil. Froth at the lid margins (foamy tears from saponification of abnormal lipid) and a dilated oily tear meniscus are characteristic. MGD is strongly associated with rosacea — any patient with the chronic facial flushing and telangiectasia of rosacea should have their lids examined. The lipid layer of the tear film becomes unstable, leading to rapid tear breakup and evaporative dry eye.

Management

The cornerstone of blepharitis management is lid hygiene, which must be done daily and indefinitely. Warm compresses applied for 5 to 10 minutes soften the inspissated Meibomian secretions (the melting point of normal meibum is around 28 to 32 degrees Celsius; dysfunctional meibum is thicker and requires higher temperatures). This must be followed immediately by lid massage to express the softened material and then lid scrubs (dilute baby shampoo or commercial lid cleansing wipes) to remove the debris.

Topical antibiotics — chloramphenicol drops or azithromycin 1% gel — help manage the bacterial component. For moderate to severe MGD and rosacea-associated blepharitis, oral doxycycline 100 mg once daily for 3 to 6 months is highly effective. Its mechanism is not primarily antimicrobial but anti-inflammatory and lipid-modifying — doxycycline inhibits bacterial lipases that break down normal meibum, and it reduces matrix metalloproteinase activity in the tear film. Low-dose doxycycline 40 mg modified release (Oracea) reduces the gastrointestinal side effects and is increasingly used for long-term management.

Managing patient expectations: Blepharitis is chronic and incurable. The goal is control, not cure. Patients who understand this comply better with the daily hygiene routine, because they are not waiting to be told they can stop.

Chalazion and Hordeolum

UGPG
▼

Chalazion

A chalazion begins when a Meibomian gland duct becomes blocked. The retained lipid secretion leaks into the surrounding tarsal tissue, triggering a granulomatous foreign body inflammatory reaction with Langerhans giant cells, epithelioid cells, and lymphocytes. The result is a firm, painless, slowly enlarging lump in the substance of the tarsus. On eversion of the lid, you see a red, congested central area corresponding to the inflamed gland — this is a useful confirmatory finding.

Most chalazia resolve with warm compresses applied 10 to 15 minutes four times daily for 4 to 6 weeks. Those that do not resolve spontaneously require incision and curettage (I&C). The procedure is done under local anaesthesia with the lid everted in a chalazion clamp. A vertical incision is made on the conjunctival surface perpendicular to the lid margin (to avoid cutting across Meibomian gland ducts), and the lipogranulomatous contents are curetted out. A horizontal incision risks cutting through adjacent gland ducts and causing further chalazia.

A chalazion that recurs at the same site, or that looks atypical (yellowish, ulcerated, or associated with lash loss), must be biopsied. Sebaceous gland carcinoma presents exactly this way, and the consequences of missing it are serious.

External and Internal Hordeolum

External hordeolum (the stye) is an acute staphylococcal abscess of a Zeis or Moll gland at the lash margin. It presents as a tender, red, pointing swelling at the lid margin adjacent to a lash. The acute inflammation responds well to warm compresses. Removing the central lash (epilation) opens the follicle and allows the abscess to drain. Topical chloramphenicol drops are used to prevent secondary spread. Most resolve within a week without specific intervention.

Internal hordeolum is a Meibomian gland abscess — more painful than external hordeolum and pointing toward the conjunctival side. Systemic antibiotics are generally required (flucloxacillin or co-amoxiclav), and drainage is from the conjunctival surface once the abscess is fluctuant.

Recurrent chalazion = biopsy mandatory. Sebaceous gland carcinoma of the eyelid can present as a recurrent chalazion for months or even years before the diagnosis is made. It is one of the leading causes of delayed cancer diagnosis in oculoplastics. If you drain a chalazion and it returns at the same site, send the contents for histology.

Eyelid Tumours

PG
▼

Basal Cell Carcinoma

BCC accounts for 90 to 95% of all malignant eyelid tumours, making it the most common lid malignancy by a wide margin. The lower lid is affected in about 50% of cases, the medial canthus in 25 to 30%, the upper lid in 15%, and the lateral canthus in 5%. The typical early lesion is a pearly, skin-coloured nodule with fine surface telangiectasia and a rolled, raised edge. As it grows it may ulcerate centrally — hence the old name rodent ulcer. Importantly, BCC destroys lashes through direct invasion but does not tend to metastasise by haematogenous spread (though nodal spread is described in neglected cases).

Treatment is surgical excision with adequate margins. The recommended minimum margin is 3 to 5 mm of clinically normal tissue. Mohs micrographic surgery — where the tumour is excised in horizontal layers and each layer is immediately examined by frozen section for tumour at the edges — allows margin-controlled excision and maximum tissue conservation, which is particularly valuable at the medial canthus where there is little tissue to spare. Radiotherapy is an alternative for patients who cannot tolerate surgery. Reconstruction of the lid defect depends on its size and location — small defects close directly, larger ones require local flaps or grafts.

Squamous Cell Carcinoma

SCC is less common than BCC but more aggressive. It may arise de novo or from actinic keratosis (premalignant). The lesion tends to be hyperkeratotic, indurated, and may ulcerate with an everted (not rolled) edge. SCC can metastasise to regional lymph nodes (preauricular and submandibular) and requires wide excision with sentinel lymph node biopsy if there is any clinical concern about nodal involvement. Perineural invasion is a worrying histological finding that predicts poorer prognosis and local recurrence.

Sebaceous Gland Carcinoma

SGC is the malignant lid tumour that every ophthalmologist must know well, because it is the one most commonly missed. It arises from Meibomian glands (upper lid predominantly, as the upper tarsus has more glands) or less often from Zeis glands. It has earned the title "the great masquerader" because it mimics benign conditions: recurrent chalazion, chronic unilateral blepharitis, or unilateral conjunctivitis are the three classic presentations that delay diagnosis. The tumour cells are lipid-laden, giving a characteristic yellowish hue on gross examination, and they demonstrate pagetoid spread — intraepithelial migration through the conjunctival and corneal epithelium without crossing the basement membrane. This means the visible lump may represent only part of the disease extent.

Diagnosis requires full-thickness lid biopsy and careful histopathological examination with Oil Red O staining for lipid (the intracytoplasmic lipid vacuoles are characteristic). If pagetoid spread is suspected, map biopsies of the conjunctiva are taken from multiple sites around the eye to determine the extent of epithelial involvement. Treatment is wide surgical excision with frozen section margin control and sentinel lymph node biopsy. Prognosis is worse than BCC or SCC, with reported mortality of 5 to 10% even with treatment, and higher in cases where diagnosis has been delayed.

Other Eyelid Lesions

Keratoacanthoma is a rapidly growing lesion that can simulate SCC: it expands over 6 to 8 weeks into a dome-shaped nodule with a central keratin-filled crater, then spontaneously involutes over months. Despite the self-limiting natural history, excision is recommended because clinical and histological distinction from SCC can be impossible on biopsy alone.

Xanthelasma presents as bilateral yellowish soft plaques at the medial canthi. They are cholesterol deposits in histiocytes within the dermis. Half of patients have a lipid abnormality and the rest have normal lipids. They are benign and managed by excision, CO2 laser, or trichloroacetic acid application, but they recur in about 40% of cases. Always check a fasting lipid profile.

Epicanthus, Epiblepharon, and Lagophthalmos

UGPG
▼

Epicanthus

Epicanthus is a vertical skin fold covering the medial canthus. It is common in Asian populations and in young children of all ethnicities (it generally disappears as the nasal bridge develops). There are four types based on the fold's origin: tarsalis (from upper lid to lower), inversus (from lower lid to upper), palpebralis (from lid fold to lower lid, crossing the inner canthus), and supraciliaris (from brow to lower lid). The clinical importance is pseudoesotropia — the medial sclera is hidden, making the child appear convergent when the eyes are actually straight. This is diagnosed by the symmetrical corneal light reflex and normal cover test.

Epiblepharon

Epiblepharon is common in East Asian children and is distinct from entropion. An extra fold of skin and orbicularis below the lower lid presses the lashes against the cornea — but the lid margin position is normal. In young children the cornea often tolerates this without complaint because of its remarkable plasticity and rapid epithelial regeneration. Many cases resolve as the facial bones mature and the nasal bridge develops, raising the lid away from the globe. If corneal staining or symptoms persist beyond age 3 to 4 years, surgical correction (excision of the extra skin fold) is indicated.

Lagophthalmos

Lagophthalmos is incomplete lid closure and is a corneal emergency in the making if untreated. Causes include CN VII palsy (the most common — orbicularis is denervated), severe proptosis (the globe protrudes beyond the orbital rim and the lids cannot reach each other), severe cicatricial ectropion, and after over-aggressive ptosis surgery. The inferior cornea and exposed interpalpebral zone suffer from desiccation, leading to exposure keratopathy, punctate epithelial erosions, and if severe, corneal ulceration or perforation. Management is aggressive: frequent preservative-free lubricating drops during the day, lubricating ointment at night, moisture chamber goggles during sleep, and lateral tarsorrhaphy for severe or long-standing cases. Gold weight implantation in the upper lid is a useful reversible option for paralytic lagophthalmos.

These are the practical examination skills for eyelid disease that come up repeatedly in clinical and viva assessments. Each is described as you would actually perform it at the slit lamp or at the bedside.

Ptosis Assessment Examination Technique
1
MRD1 Measurement

Shine a penlight on the patient's face from arm's length. The corneal light reflex should appear symmetrically in both eyes. MRD1 is the distance from the upper lid margin to this reflex in primary gaze. Normal is 4 to 5 mm. Values below 2 mm represent significant ptosis that obstructs the visual axis. Measure both eyes and record the difference — the contralateral lid often has compensatory retraction from Hering's law (bilateral equal innervation of the levator), which will correct after ptosis repair.

Cover the ptotic eye and observe if the contralateral lid drops — this confirms Hering's law retraction and predicts the final contralateral lid height after surgery.
2
Levator Function Test

Place your thumb firmly on the patient's brow, above the superior orbital rim, to completely block frontalis action. Ask the patient to look down as far as possible, then up as far as possible. Measure the total excursion of the upper lid margin with a ruler. The thumb must remain firmly pressed throughout — any release will recruit frontalis and falsely inflate the measurement. Normal LF is above 10 mm. Good is 8 to 10 mm, fair 4 to 7 mm, and poor below 4 mm. This single measurement determines the surgical approach more than any other.

3
Bell's Phenomenon

Ask the patient to close both eyes, then gently prise the upper lid open against their effort at closure. Observe where the cornea goes. In normal Bell's phenomenon, the globe rotates upward and outward (up and out), so when the lid is forcibly opened, you see white sclera rather than cornea — this is protective. If the globe remains in primary position (absent Bell's), any lid surgery that leaves the lids open risks corneal exposure. Document this carefully before any ptosis surgery.

Test each eye separately. Bell's phenomenon can be asymmetric.
4
Cogan's Lid Twitch for Myasthenia

Ask the patient to look straight ahead, then sustain downgaze for 15 seconds, then return rapidly to primary gaze. Watch the upper lid carefully as gaze returns. In myasthenia, the lid twitches upward momentarily before settling back to its ptotic position. This is the Cogan's lid twitch sign and is specific for myasthenia when present. Also ask the patient to sustain upgaze for 60 seconds and watch for fatigable ptosis developing — the lid droops progressively as the patient maintains the position.

Lid Eversion for Tarsal Examination Examination Technique
1
Simple Eversion of the Lower Lid

Ask the patient to look upward. With the index finger of your non-dominant hand, gently pull the lower lid downward at its margin. The inferior tarsus, lower fornix, and inferior bulbar conjunctiva come into view. This exposes the lower Meibomian gland orifices, any conjunctival follicles or papillae in the inferior fornix, and the inferior punctum. No special equipment is needed. Never pull the lid down from the skin of the cheek — you want traction on the margin itself.

2
Single Eversion of the Upper Lid

Ask the patient to look down and keep looking down throughout. Grasp the upper eyelashes gently between the thumb and forefinger of one hand and pull them downward and outward. Place a cotton bud horizontally across the lid skin at the level of the superior tarsal border (about 8 to 10 mm above the lid margin). Now fold the lid upward over the cotton bud by pulling the lashes upward and backward. The tarsal conjunctiva now faces you. Remove the cotton bud. Ask the patient to continue looking down — this keeps the lid everted. Examine the tarsal conjunctiva for papillae, follicles, concretions, scarring, and the Meibomian gland orifices.

If the patient looks up while the lid is everted, the lid will un-evert. Tell them explicitly to keep looking at the floor throughout.
3
Double Eversion for the Superior Fornix

Single eversion only exposes the tarsal conjunctiva. To examine the superior fornix (where subtarsal foreign bodies, symblepharon, and trachomatous scarring hide), you need double eversion with a Desmarres retractor. After single eversion, slide the Desmarres retractor behind the everted lid and sweep the concave blade upward into the superior fornix. The upper fornix is now visible. This requires practice and a cooperative patient. A chalazion or retractor is an acceptable substitute for a Desmarres retractor.

Examination of Lashes for Trichiasis Diagnostic Assessment
1
Slit Lamp Examination

Examine the lid margin at medium magnification (10x to 16x) with a focal beam. Look at the direction of each lash row. Normal lashes sweep away from the globe at their tips. In trichiasis, individual lashes or whole rows point posteriorly toward the cornea. Note which quadrant and how many lashes are affected — this determines the treatment choice. Evert the lid to confirm the lid margin position is normal (ruling out entropion as the cause).

2
Assessing Corneal Damage

Instil fluorescein and examine with the cobalt blue filter. Trichiasis that is actively rubbing the cornea produces punctate epithelial erosions in the area of contact, usually the inferior cornea and interpalpebral zone. The pattern of staining often points directly to the offending lash. Document the extent of corneal staining to guide urgency of treatment.

3
Treatment Planning

For one or two stray lashes with no underlying lid maldirection, electrolysis or argon laser follicle ablation is the most appropriate definitive treatment. For widespread trichiasis involving multiple rows (as in trachoma or OCP), cryotherapy to the anterior lid margin — using a double freeze-thaw cycle to destroy follicles while preserving lid margin tissue — is preferred. For any significant number of misdirected lashes, never rely on repeated epilation as a long-term strategy, because repeated trauma damages the follicle in a way that makes subsequent laser or cryotherapy less effective.

Always look for the underlying cause: is there entropion? Cicatricial disease? Trachoma scarring on the superior tarsus? Treating the lashes without addressing the lid position will result in recurrence.

1. The most common cause of ptosis in the elderly is:
A. Myasthenia gravis
B. Aponeurotic (senile) ptosis
C. Horner's syndrome
D. Congenital ptosis
Answer: B. Aponeurotic ptosis from disinsertion of the levator aponeurosis is by far the most common cause in elderly adults. It is characterised by normal levator function, a deep superior sulcus, and a thin upper lid. Myasthenia is variable; Horner's gives only 2 mm of ptosis; congenital ptosis presents at birth.
2. A patient has a ptosis with MRD1 of 1 mm and levator function of 2 mm. The most appropriate surgical management is:
A. Müller's muscle resection
B. Levator resection
C. Frontalis sling
D. Aponeurosis plication
Answer: C. Levator function below 4 mm is classified as poor, meaning the levator muscle is too weak to use surgically. Frontalis sling — suspending the lid from the frontalis muscle using fascia lata or silicone rod — is required. Levator resection is for fair LF (4 to 7 mm); Müller's resection is for good LF with minimal ptosis; aponeurosis repair is for aponeurotic ptosis with normal LF.
3. Cogan's lid twitch sign is associated with:
A. Myasthenia gravis
B. Third nerve palsy
C. Horner's syndrome
D. Aponeurotic ptosis
Answer: A. Cogan's lid twitch is a specific sign of myasthenia gravis: the ptotic lid momentarily overshoots upward when gaze returns from sustained downgaze. It reflects transient accumulation of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction during the rest period in downgaze, which briefly overcomes the block on rapid return to primary position.
4. The most common type of entropion in elderly patients is:
A. Cicatricial
B. Involutional
C. Congenital
D. Spastic
Answer: B. Involutional (senile) entropion is by far the most common type, caused by a combination of horizontal lid laxity, lower lid retractor dehiscence, and overriding of the preseptal orbicularis. Cicatricial entropion follows conjunctival scarring (trachoma, OCP, burns) and is less common overall though common in trachoma-endemic regions.
5. A chalazion differs from a hordeolum in that it:
A. Is more painful
B. Points toward the skin surface
C. Involves the Zeis gland
D. Is a chronic lipogranulomatous lesion in the tarsal plate
Answer: D. A chalazion is a chronic, painless lipogranuloma of the Meibomian gland within the tarsal plate. It does not point. A hordeolum is an acute suppurative infection: external hordeolum involves the Zeis/Moll gland and points to skin; internal hordeolum is a Meibomian gland abscess that points to the conjunctival surface. Chalazia are painless; hordeola are painful.
6. The most common malignant eyelid tumour is:
A. Squamous cell carcinoma
B. Sebaceous gland carcinoma
C. Basal cell carcinoma
D. Melanoma
Answer: C. BCC accounts for 90 to 95% of all malignant lid tumours. It is most common at the lower lid and medial canthus, and characteristically has a pearly rolled edge with telangiectasia and madarosis. It rarely metastasises but is locally destructive if neglected.
7. Bell's phenomenon refers to:
A. Abnormal lid retraction on upgaze
B. Upward and outward rolling of the globe on attempted lid closure
C. Lid lag in thyroid eye disease
D. The Cogan's lid twitch sign
Answer: B. Bell's phenomenon is the physiological upward and outward rotation of the globe when the eyelid attempts to close. It protects the cornea from exposure when the lid cannot close fully. Its absence is a contraindication to aggressive ptosis surgery, because the cornea will be left exposed if the lid does not fully close postoperatively.
8. Incision and curettage of a chalazion should be made:
A. Perpendicular to the lid margin on the conjunctival surface
B. Parallel to the lid margin on the conjunctival surface
C. Through the skin surface
D. At the lash margin
Answer: A. I&C is performed from the conjunctival surface (posterior approach) to avoid a visible skin scar. The incision is made perpendicular to the lid margin — a horizontal incision parallel to the margin would cut across multiple Meibomian gland ducts, risking further blockage and chalazia.
9. Horner's syndrome causes ptosis because it affects:
A. Levator palpebrae superioris
B. Orbicularis oculi
C. Müller's (superior tarsal) muscle
D. Frontalis muscle
Answer: C. Müller's muscle (superior tarsal muscle) is innervated by sympathetic fibres. Horner's syndrome (sympathetic chain disruption) paralyses Müller's muscle, causing 1 to 2 mm of ptosis. The levator palpebrae is intact (CN III). This is why phenylephrine (a sympathomimetic) corrects Horner's ptosis.
10. The preferred surgical procedure for involutional entropion is:
A. Posterior lamellar graft
B. Cryotherapy to lashes
C. Medial canthoplasty
D. Quickert's procedure with lateral tarsal strip
Answer: D. Involutional entropion is caused by horizontal lid laxity and overriding pretarsal orbicularis. The Quickert-Rathbun procedure (everting sutures to correct the orbicularis override) combined with a lateral tarsal strip (to tighten horizontal laxity) addresses both components. Posterior lamellar graft is for cicatricial entropion; medial canthoplasty is for medial ectropion.
11. Epiblepharon is different from entropion because in epiblepharon:
A. The lid margin is inverted
B. The lid margin position is normal
C. Surgical correction is always required
D. It is caused by trachoma
Answer: B. Epiblepharon is a skin fold that pushes lashes against the cornea, but the lid margin position remains normal. In entropion, the entire lid margin — including the grey line — is turned inward. Epiblepharon is common in Asian children and often resolves spontaneously as the nasal bridge matures.
12. Oral doxycycline is used in blepharitis primarily because:
A. It modifies Meibomian gland lipid secretion and reduces inflammation
B. It eradicates Demodex mites permanently
C. It reduces intraocular pressure
D. It prevents corneal vascularisation
Answer: A. Doxycycline's benefit in blepharitis and MGD is largely non-antibiotic. It inhibits bacterial lipases that degrade normal meibum, reduces matrix metalloproteinase activity in the tear film, and has direct anti-inflammatory properties. This makes it effective at 100 mg OD for 3 to 6 months even at sub-antimicrobial doses (40 mg modified release).

1. A 70-year-old woman has ptosis with MRD1 of 2 mm, levator function of 12 mm, and a deep superior sulcus with a thin upper lid. The most appropriate surgical repair is:
A. Frontalis sling with silicone rod
B. Müller's muscle resection alone
C. Levator aponeurosis repair/plication
D. Levator resection of 16 mm
Answer: C. This is classic aponeurotic ptosis: good LF (12 mm), deep superior sulcus, thin lid, elderly patient. The levator muscle is intact but the aponeurosis has disinserted. Aponeurosis repair or plication is the physiological and correct procedure. Frontalis sling is for poor LF; Müller's resection is for minimal ptosis with good LF; large levator resection is unnecessary when the muscle is normal.
2. Sebaceous gland carcinoma of the eyelid most commonly arises from:
A. Lower lid Meibomian glands
B. Zeis glands
C. Moll's glands
D. Upper lid Meibomian glands
Answer: D. SGC predominantly arises from Meibomian glands of the upper tarsus, which contains more glands (25 to 30) than the lower tarsus (20 to 25). This is why SGC presents in the upper lid far more often than BCC (which favours the lower lid). The upper lid origin is a key distinguishing feature for examinations.
3. Pagetoid spread in sebaceous gland carcinoma refers to:
A. Lymphatic spread to regional nodes
B. Intraepithelial spread through conjunctival epithelium without crossing the basement membrane
C. Perineural invasion along the orbital nerves
D. Direct invasion into orbital fat
Answer: B. Pagetoid spread describes intraepithelial migration of SGC cells through the conjunctival (and sometimes corneal) epithelium while remaining above the basement membrane. This means the tumour can extend far beyond the visible lid lesion, which is why map biopsies of the conjunctiva and frozen section margins are required before reconstruction. Missing pagetoid extension causes recurrence.
4. In the phenylephrine test for ptosis, a positive result predicts a good outcome from:
A. Müller's muscle resection (Fasanella-Servat)
B. Frontalis sling
C. Levator resection
D. Brow suspension
Answer: A. Topical phenylephrine 2.5% stimulates Müller's muscle (alpha-1 agonist). If instillation raises the ptotic lid to a satisfactory level, it predicts that Müller's muscle resection will produce equivalent elevation surgically. The test is particularly useful before Fasanella-Servat procedure and for evaluating Horner's ptosis.
5. Cicatricial entropion is managed with posterior lamellar grafting because:
A. The anterior lamella is scarred and contracted
B. The orbicularis is overriding the pretarsal muscle
C. Posterior lamella scarring shortens the conjunctival surface pulling the lid margin in
D. Horizontal lid laxity is the primary problem
Answer: C. In cicatricial entropion (trachoma, OCP, Stevens-Johnson), the conjunctiva and tarsus are shortened by scarring, pulling the lid margin inward. The solution is to lengthen the posterior lamella by adding tissue — hard palate mucosal graft or buccal mucosa graft. Everting sutures alone fail because they do not address the fundamental posterior lamella shortage.
6. A patient with CN VII palsy and lagophthalmos requires long-term corneal protection. The most appropriate reversible surgical option for the upper lid is:
A. Permanent lateral tarsorrhaphy
B. Conjunctival flap
C. Botulinum toxin to the levator
D. Gold weight implantation in the upper lid
Answer: D. Gold weight implantation uses gravity to passively close the upper lid during blinking and sleep. It is reversible (the weight can be removed if facial function recovers), cosmetically acceptable, and well-tolerated. Botulinum toxin to the levator is a temporary measure (3 months). Permanent tarsorrhaphy, while effective, is cosmetically unacceptable and is used when other measures fail.
7. The Quickert-Rathbun procedure for involutional entropion works primarily by:
A. Shortening the horizontal lid length
B. Placing full-thickness everting sutures that correct pretarsal orbicularis override and tighten the retractors
C. Adding posterior lamellar tissue to lengthen the conjunctiva
D. Excising the marginal strip of conjunctiva and Müller's muscle
Answer: B. The Quickert-Rathbun procedure places 2 to 3 full-thickness sutures through the lower lid from the inferior fornix (picking up the retractors) to emerge through the skin below the lashes. When tied, these sutures evert the lid margin and tighten the lower lid retractors, correcting both the orbicularis override and the retractor laxity that cause involutional entropion.
8. Congenital ptosis with poor levator function causes amblyopia primarily by:
A. Visual axis deprivation causing stimulus deprivation amblyopia
B. Refractive error from the lid pressing on the cornea
C. Strabismus from ptosis-related head posture
D. Anisocoria from associated Horner's syndrome
Answer: A. When the ptotic lid covers the visual axis during the critical period of visual development, the affected eye receives no patterned visual input, resulting in stimulus deprivation amblyopia — the most severe and treatment-resistant form of amblyopia. There may also be an anisometropic component (the lid pressure on the cornea induces astigmatism) but the primary mechanism in lid-occluding ptosis is visual deprivation.
9. Mohs micrographic surgery is preferred over standard excision for eyelid BCC at the medial canthus because:
A. It is faster and less expensive
B. It avoids the need for reconstruction
C. It provides real-time margin control and maximum tissue conservation
D. It is better for morphoeic BCC only
Answer: C. Mohs surgery excises tissue in mapped horizontal layers, with each layer immediately examined under frozen section by the surgeon-pathologist. If tumour is present at any margin, only that area is re-excised. This maximises tissue conservation (critical at the medial canthus where tissue is limited and vital structures are nearby) while providing the highest cure rates of any technique.
10. Lid lag in downgaze is characteristic of which type of ptosis:
A. Aponeurotic ptosis
B. Horner's syndrome
C. Neurogenic ptosis from CN III palsy
D. Congenital myogenic ptosis
Answer: D. In congenital myogenic ptosis, the fibrofatty dystropic levator cannot lengthen on downgaze as a normal muscle would. The lid stays up in downgaze, producing lid lag — the lid rides higher than expected when the patient looks down. Aponeurotic ptosis follows downgaze normally because the muscle is intact. This lid lag is a hallmark of myogenic ptosis and helps distinguish it from aponeurotic causes.
11. The nerve supply to orbicularis oculi is:
A. Nasociliary branch of V1
B. Facial nerve (CN VII)
C. Oculomotor nerve (CN III)
D. Trochlear nerve (CN IV)
Answer: B. Orbicularis oculi is the protractor of the lid and is entirely innervated by CN VII (facial nerve), via its temporal and zygomatic branches. This is why CN VII palsy causes lagophthalmos — inability to close the lid. CN III innervates the levator (lid opener). Sensory innervation of the lid skin is via branches of CN V1 and V2.
12. A recurrent chalazion at the same site in a 60-year-old man should prompt:
A. Full-thickness lid biopsy to exclude sebaceous gland carcinoma
B. Repeat incision and curettage without histology
C. Topical steroid injection
D. Referral for radiotherapy
Answer: A. Recurrent chalazion at the same site in a middle-aged or older patient is SGC until proven otherwise. SGC is notorious for presenting as a recurrent chalazion. A full-thickness lid biopsy with histopathological examination (including Oil Red O stain for lipid) is mandatory. Simply repeating I&C without histology misses the diagnosis and allows the cancer to progress.
Long answer questions for MS/DNB written examinations. Plan your answer to cover classification, clinical features, examination, investigations, and management.
1. Classify ptosis. Describe the clinical assessment of a patient with ptosis and outline the surgical management based on levator function. (15 marks)
Classification of Ptosis

Ptosis is classified by aetiology into five groups. Neurogenic ptosis arises from CN III palsy (complete ptosis with ophthalmoplegia and mydriasis) or Horner's syndrome (partial 2 mm ptosis from Müller's muscle denervation, with miosis and anhidrosis). Myogenic ptosis includes congenital (dystropic levator with fibrofatty replacement), myasthenia gravis (variable, fatigue-related, Cogan's lid twitch), and myotonic dystrophy. Aponeurotic ptosis (senile) — the most common in adults — results from disinsertion of the levator aponeurosis. Mechanical ptosis is caused by tumour, swelling, or haematoma weighing down the lid. Traumatic ptosis follows direct injury to the levator or aponeurosis.

Clinical Assessment

Assessment begins with a careful history: onset (congenital vs acquired), variability (worse in evening suggests myasthenia), associated symptoms (diplopia, facial weakness, ptosis elsewhere), medications, and family history. General inspection notes the degree of ptosis, the superior sulcus depth (deep = aponeurotic), lid crease position, and compensatory frontalis overaction or chin-up head posture. MRD1 (margin reflex distance) is measured from the corneal light reflex to the upper lid margin — normal 4 to 5 mm; below 2 mm is significant. Levator function is measured with frontalis blocked: excursion from full downgaze to full upgaze measured with a ruler. Bell's phenomenon is assessed by prising the closed lid open — upward globe rotation is protective. Corneal sensation, extraocular movements, and pupil examination complete the assessment. Myasthenia is evaluated with the ice test and Cogan's twitch sign; Tensilon test and AChR antibodies confirm the diagnosis if suspected.

Surgical Management by Levator Function

Poor LF (below 4 mm): frontalis sling using autologous fascia lata or silicone rod. Fair LF (4 to 7 mm): levator resection — the muscle is shortened by a measured amount using Beard's tables as a guide. Good LF (above 7 mm) with minimal ptosis: Müller's muscle resection (Fasanella-Servat), particularly if phenylephrine test is positive. Aponeurotic ptosis with normal LF: levator aponeurosis repair or plication — the disinserted aponeurosis is reattached to the tarsal plate. Before any surgery, absent Bell's phenomenon is a contraindication to aggressive correction; myasthenia must be treated medically before any surgical intervention.

2. Classify entropion. Describe the management of each type. (10 marks)
Classification

Entropion is turning in of the lid margin so that lashes contact the cornea. It is classified as congenital (rare, due to anterior lamella deficiency), involutional (most common — elderly, horizontal lid laxity, overriding orbicularis, retractor dehiscence), spastic (from blepharospasm overriding the pretarsal orbicularis), and cicatricial (from posterior lamella scarring — trachoma, OCP, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, burns).

Management

Involutional entropion: temporary measures include lid taping to the cheek and botulinum toxin into the lower lid orbicularis. Definitive surgery is the Quickert-Rathbun procedure (full-thickness everting sutures) combined with a lateral tarsal strip to address horizontal laxity. Spastic entropion: treat the underlying blepharospasm (botulinum toxin to the orbicularis); if persistent, manage as involutional. Cicatricial entropion: the fundamental problem is posterior lamella shortage. Everting sutures alone fail. Treatment requires posterior lamellar augmentation using a hard palate mucosal graft or buccal mucosa graft sutured into the posterior lamella to lengthen the scarred surface. Congenital entropion: surgical correction with anterior lamella augmentation is required once the diagnosis is confirmed and spontaneous resolution has not occurred.

Emergency Measures

Regardless of type, the cornea must be protected immediately with lubricating ointment and taping of the lid to the cheek skin until definitive surgery can be arranged. Corneal staining should be documented and corneal ulceration treated aggressively if present.

3. Compare and contrast BCC and SGC of the eyelid. How would you diagnose and manage each? (10 marks)
Comparison

BCC is the most common malignant lid tumour (90 to 95% of cases), typically affecting the lower lid and medial canthus. It presents as a pearly, skin-coloured nodule with surface telangiectasia, a rolled edge, and madarosis. It is locally destructive — the rodent ulcer of neglected cases — but metastasis is rare. SGC is far less common but significantly more dangerous. It arises predominantly from upper lid Meibomian glands (upper lid in 60 to 75% of cases) and presents insidiously as a recurrent chalazion, unilateral chronic blepharitis, or unexplained unilateral conjunctivitis. The tumour cells are lipid-laden and the lesion may appear yellowish. SGC metastasises to regional lymph nodes and has a reported mortality of 5 to 10%. Its most dangerous property is pagetoid spread — intraepithelial migration through conjunctival epithelium that extends far beyond the visible lid lesion.

Diagnosis

BCC is diagnosed clinically and confirmed by excisional biopsy with histopathology. SGC requires full-thickness lid biopsy with Oil Red O staining for intracytoplasmic lipid. If pagetoid spread is suspected, map biopsies (8 to 10 sites around the conjunctiva) are taken to map the extent of intraepithelial spread before planning surgery.

Management

BCC: excision with 3 to 5 mm margins or Mohs micrographic surgery (preferred at medial canthus). Radiotherapy for inoperable cases or patients unfit for surgery. Lid reconstruction after excision depends on defect size. SGC: wide surgical excision with frozen section margin control, map biopsy confirmation of clear conjunctival margins, and sentinel lymph node biopsy. If pagetoid spread is confirmed, cryotherapy to involved conjunctival areas or topical mitomycin C may be used. Orbital exenteration is reserved for orbital invasion. Prognosis of SGC is substantially worse than BCC.

4. Classify blepharitis and outline its management. (10 marks)
Classification

Blepharitis is classified anatomically into anterior and posterior forms. Anterior blepharitis affects the anterior lid margin, lash follicles, and glands of Zeis and Moll. It has two subtypes: seborrhoeic (greasy scales at lash bases, associated with seborrhoeic dermatitis of scalp and face) and staphylococcal (hard collarette crusts encircling lash bases, marginal lid ulceration, chalazia, phlyctenules from hypersensitivity to exotoxins). Posterior blepharitis (Meibomian gland dysfunction, MGD) affects the posterior lid margin and Meibomian glands. Inspissated meibum blocks gland orifices; the abnormal lipid destabilises the tear film causing evaporative dry eye. MGD is strongly associated with rosacea.

Clinical Features

Symptoms include lid margin irritation, grittiness, crusting, and intermittent blurring from unstable tear film. Signs: collarette crusts (staphylococcal), seborrhoeic scales (seborrhoeic), Meibomian gland orifice capping and turbid secretion on expression (MGD), posterior lid margin telangiectasia, froth at lid margins (MGD), reduced TBUT.

Management

Lid hygiene is the foundation of all blepharitis management. Warm compresses (5 to 10 minutes) are applied to soften inspissated meibum, followed immediately by lid massage to express the secretions, followed by lid scrubs (dilute baby shampoo or commercial wipes) to remove debris. This must be performed daily and indefinitely. Topical antibiotics (chloramphenicol drops or azithromycin 1% gel) address the bacterial component of anterior blepharitis. Oral doxycycline 100 mg once daily for 3 to 6 months is indicated for moderate to severe MGD and rosacea — it acts by inhibiting bacterial lipases, reducing matrix metalloproteinases, and direct anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Preservative-free artificial tears address the secondary evaporative dry eye. Patients must understand that blepharitis is a chronic condition requiring ongoing management rather than a cure.

What is the significance of Bell's phenomenon in ptosis surgery? When is its absence a contraindication to surgery?
Bell's phenomenon is the upward and outward rolling of the globe on attempted lid closure, serving as a protective mechanism. In ptosis surgery, if the lid is elevated more than the cornea can tolerate when the lids are open, exposure keratopathy results. Absent Bell's means the cornea has no protective covering during sleep or incomplete blink — any lid elevation that leaves a gap will directly expose the bare cornea. Absent Bell's is a relative contraindication to aggressive ptosis surgery; if surgery must be performed, deliberate undercorrection is made and intensive lubricant therapy prescribed postoperatively. It is not an absolute contraindication to frontalis sling (which can be adjusted) but it demands extreme caution.

Describe the features that distinguish aponeurotic ptosis from congenital ptosis.
Aponeurotic ptosis (senile): elderly patient; acquired onset; thin upper lid; deep superior sulcus from loss of aponeurotic adhesion to lid skin; good or excellent levator function (above 10 mm); lid crease elevated or absent; lid follows downgaze normally (no lid lag); aponeurosis can be visualised through the thin lid as a pale band. Congenital ptosis: present from birth; levator function typically poor (below 4 mm) due to dystropic muscle with fibrofatty replacement; lid lag in downgaze (muscle cannot lengthen); no deep superior sulcus (the sulcus is poorly formed); risk of amblyopia if covering the visual axis; commonly unilateral; requires frontalis sling for poor LF.

Sebaceous gland carcinoma is called "the great masquerader." Explain this statement and describe how you would diagnose it.
SGC earns this title because it mimics three common benign conditions: recurrent chalazion (most common presentation), chronic unilateral blepharitis (diffuse upper lid thickening and lash loss), and unilateral conjunctivitis that fails to respond to topical treatment. The tumour grows slowly and its lipid-laden cells do not produce the dramatic surface changes of BCC or SCC, allowing months or years to pass before the diagnosis is made. Classify eyelid tumours. What are the features of BCC of the eyelid and how is it treated?
Eyelid tumours are classified as benign (papilloma, seborrhoeic keratosis, keratoacanthoma, haemangioma, xanthelasma, naevus) and malignant (BCC, SCC, SGC, melanoma). BCC features: most common malignant lid tumour (90 to 95%); lower lid and medial canthus preferred sites; pearly, skin-coloured nodule with surface telangiectasia and a raised, rolled edge; madarosis (lash loss from direct invasion); may ulcerate centrally (rodent ulcer); rarely metastasises. What is the surgical algorithm for ptosis based on levator function?
The levator function (LF) measurement — excursion of the lid from full downgaze to upgaze with frontalis blocked — determines the surgical approach. Poor LF (below 4 mm): the levator is too weak to use. Frontalis sling using autologous fascia lata (from the thigh, gold standard) or silicone rod is performed, suspending the lid from the frontalis muscle. Fair LF (4 to 7 mm): enough muscle for levator resection — the muscle is shortened by excising a calculated length from its origin. Good LF (above 7 mm) with minimal ptosis: Müller's muscle resection (Fasanella-Servat) is sufficient, particularly if phenylephrine test is positive. Normal LF with aponeurotic ptosis: levator aponeurosis repair or plication — the disinserted aponeurosis is reattached to the tarsal plate. The amount of resection for each procedure is guided by standard tables (e.g., Beard's tables) but is adjusted intraoperatively when surgery is performed under local anaesthesia in adults.

Describe the management of acute dacryocystitis. (Note: though this is a lacrimal question it frequently appears in eyelid/adnexa sections of examinations.)
Acute dacryocystitis presents as a sudden painful, red, tender swelling below the medial palpebral ligament (important — this is slightly below the medial canthus, at the lacrimal sac fossa). Regurgitation of mucopus on pressure over the sac confirms the diagnosis. Management: systemic antibiotics are the first priority — amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily or a first-generation cephalosporin is standard; intravenous antibiotics if cellulitis is spreading. Warm compresses help localise and soften the abscess. Topical antibiotic drops are added. Syringing and probing are contraindicated in the acute phase as they can spread infection. If the abscess becomes fluctuant and points to the skin, incision and drainage (I&D) from the skin surface relieves acute symptoms. DCR (dacryocystorhinostomy) is the definitive treatment but is performed only after the acute infection has completely resolved — typically 4 to 6 weeks later. Operating in the acute phase risks spreading infection and significantly worsens outcomes.
