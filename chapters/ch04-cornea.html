Chapter 4

Diseases of the Cornea

Corneal ulcers (bacterial, viral, fungal), keratoconus, dystrophies, and transplantation — critical for UG/PG exams and clinical practice.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Bacterial Corneal Ulcer

UGPG
▼

Common Organisms

  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Most common cause of microbial keratitis in soft contact lens wearers; aggressive; can perforate in <7 days
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Slower progression; associated with chronic suppuration
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae: Hypopyon frequent; mucopurulent exudate
  • Risk factors: Contact lens overuse, corneal trauma, compromised epithelium, herpetic eye disease

Clinical Features

  • Symptoms: Pain (severe), photophobia, lacrimation, discharge (purulent)
  • Signs: Anterior chamber reaction (AC cells/flare), hypopyon (sterile or infected pus; 0.5–2 mm level), stromal infiltration/opacity
  • Pseudomonas hallmark: Yellow-green mucopurulent discharge, rapid stromal necrosis, ring infiltrate (less common)

Management

  • Diagnostic culture: Scrapings for Gram, Giemsa, culture (blood agar, chocolate, Sabouraud)
  • Intensive topical antibiotics: Fluoroquinolone (moxifloxacin, gatifloxacin) or aminoglycoside (tobramycin) fortified + cephalosporin (cefazolin 5%) every 1–2 h initially
  • Oral antibiotics: Consider fluoroquinolone (if risk of perforation)
  • Supportive:** Lubricants, cycloplegia, NSAIDs cautiously (may slow epithelialization); avoid patching (↑ risk)
  • Surgery: If perforation imminent → tissue adhesive (cyanoacrylate), bandage contact lens, or amniotic membrane graft
️ Pseudomonas Risk: Contact lens wearers (extended wear, sleeping in lenses, improper cleaning) at highest risk. 1 mm penetration per day possible. Requires aggressive hourly topical therapy.

Viral Keratitis — HSV & VZV

UGPG
▼

HSV-1 Keratitis

  • Epidemiology: Most common infectious keratitis in developed nations; 5–10% recurrence rate within 1 year
  • Pathogenesis: Primary infection (blepharoconjunctivitis) → latency in trigeminal ganglion → reactivation (stress, UV, fever)

Morphology of HSV Keratitis

  • Epithelial keratitis: Dendritic ulcer (pathognomonic; branching epithelial defect with terminal bulbs); stains with fluorescein; rose-bengal/lissamine green shows infected epithelium
  • Stromal keratitis: Granulomatous or necrotizing; dense infiltration; immune-mediated; may progress despite antivirals
  • Disciform keratitis: Central disc-shaped stromal edema + epithelial dome; anterior chamber inflammation; may have Wessely immune ring (rare)
  • Endotheliitis: Anterior uveitis pattern, endothelial cell loss; may lead to corneal decompensation

Geographic Ulcer

  • Expansion of dendritic ulcer when treated with topical corticosteroids alone (steroids suppress local immunity)
  • Presentation: Large, amorphous stromal infiltration; sloughed necrotic epithelium
  • Management: STOP steroids; use high-dose topical acyclovir (5%) q2h + oral acyclovir/valacyclovir; may need conjunctival flap or amniotic membrane

Treatment

  • Epithelial: Topical acyclovir 5% q2h (or vidarabine, trifluridine) until healed (7–10 d) + oral acyclovir 400–800 mg 4–5×/day
  • Stromal/Disciform: Topical corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1% q4h, taper over 4–6 weeks) + oral antivirals; careful balance (immunosuppression vs inflammation control)
  • Endotheliitis: Aggressive topical steroids + oral acyclovir; IV acyclovir if severe
  • Prophylaxis (recurrence risk): Long-term oral acyclovir 400 mg bd or valacyclovir 500 mg daily

VZV Keratitis

  • Follows herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO); pseudodendritic (blunt borders; no terminal bulbs), stromal infiltrates, anterior uveitis
  • Treatment: Topical acyclovir 5% q2h + oral/IV acyclovir (higher doses than HSV); oral antivirals critical for VZV

Fungal Keratitis

PG
▼
  • Epidemiology: 5–15% of microbial keratitis in tropical regions (India, Southeast Asia); plant trauma major risk
  • Common agents: Aspergillus (fumigatus, flavus), Fusarium, Candida (immunocompromised)

Clinical Features

  • Presentation: Insidious onset; slow progression; history of trauma (twig, leaf, corneal abrasion)
  • Appearance: Dense, dry, wrinkled stromal infiltrate; satellite lesions (hallmark); raised borders; feathery margins
  • Hypopyon: Usually absent or minimal (unlike bacterial)
  • Minimal ocular pain: Relative to size of ulcer

Diagnosis

  • Culture: Sabouraud dextrose agar (slow; 2–3 weeks); KOH prep shows hyphae/spores
  • Giemsa/PAS stain: Shows fungal elements (hyphae, pseudohyphae, spores)
  • Confocal microscopy: Can visualize hyphae in vivo (non-contact)

Management

  • Antifungal drops: Natamycin 5% (polyene; fungistatic; preferred for Aspergillus/Fusarium) q1–2h; alternative: amphotericin B 0.15% or miconazole 1%
  • Oral systemic agents (adjunct): Fluconazole 400 mg bd (Candida, some Aspergillus); voriconazole (Aspergillus/Fusarium)
  • Avoid corticosteroids: Worsen fungal infection; promote deeper invasion
  • Surgery: Corneal scraping/debridement for thick membrane; therapeutic keratoplasty if large or threatening perforation
Natamycin advantage: Polyene; does not penetrate well (stays superficial; ideal for anterior keratitis); minimal toxicity; less resistance development.

Acanthamoeba Keratitis

PG
▼
  • Epidemiology: Contact lens wearers (especially soft lenses); water source (tap, poorly disinfected solutions); rare but sight-threatening
  • Pathogenesis: Free-living amoeba; creates collagenase → stromal melting; severe inflammation

Clinical Features

  • Symptoms: Pain out of proportion to findings (classic); photophobia, watery discharge
  • Early: Epithelial defect, anterior stromal infiltration (may be multifocal)
  • Advanced: Ring infiltrate (pathognomonic; concentric stromal density without ulceration; 50% of cases), perineural infiltration
  • Chronic stage: Stromal opacity, vascularization, possible scleritis

Diagnosis

  • Culture: Slow; non-nutrient agar plate seeded with E. coli; incubated 25–37°C; difficult isolation
  • Wet mount: Motile trophozoites (amoeboid movement) or cysts
  • Confocal microscopy: Reveals characteristic morphology; non-invasive
  • Serology: Acanthamoeba antibodies (research, not routine)

Management

  • Topical: Polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) 0.02% q1h initially or chlorhexidine 0.02% (cyst & trophozoite lethal); add propamidine 0.1% (mechanism unclear; synergistic)
  • Systemic: Oral itraconazole 200 mg bd or miltefosine (experimental)
  • NSAIDs: Cyclosporine drops for severe inflammation
  • Avoid:**Steroids (enhance infection); aminoglycosides (ineffective)
  • Surgery:**Therapeutic keratoplasty if severe stromal involvement or refractory to medical therapy; high failure rate (organism persists in sclera)
️ Contact Lens Contamination: Tap water (chlorine-resistant cysts), homemade saline, non-sterile solution bottles. Prevention: sterile saline, chemical disinfection (not just rinsing), avoid sleeping in lenses.

Keratoconus

UGPG
▼

Overview

  • Definition: Progressive corneal ectasia; cone-shaped protrusion; collagen cross-linking abnormality
  • Onset: Puberty, progresses to 3rd–4th decade, then stabilizes
  • Associations: Down syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, connective tissue disorders; eye rubbing (risk factor)

Clinical Signs

  • Munson's sign: V-shaped deformation of lower eyelid on downward gaze (lower lid pushed by cone apex)
  • Fleischer ring: Golden-brown iron deposition at cone base (along Descemet's); may fade after CXL
  • Vogt's striae: Vertical stress lines in stroma (resolve partially with pressure relief)
  • Rizzuti's sign: Conical reflex at slit lamp (light reflex follows cone shape)

Progression & Staging

  • Amsler-Krumeich stages (I–IV): Based on keratometry, pachymetry, visual acuity
  • Acute hydrops: Descemet's rupture → stromal edema, transient vision loss; resolves in weeks to months; scar forms

Management (Stepwise)

  • Early (I–II): Spectacle correction (irregular astigmatism, myopia)
  • Moderate (II–III): RGP contact lenses (vault over apex, uniform optical surface); Intacs (intrastromal corneal rings; insert at 80% depth) for stabilization
  • Advanced (III–IV, thin cornea <400 µm): Corneal cross-linking (CXL; riboflavin + UVA); halts progression; preferred modern approach
  • Severe/End-stage: Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP); high success (90–95%); may regraft if rejects or if KC recurs in graft

Corneal Cross-Linking (CXL)

  • Mechanism: Riboflavin (vitamin B2) photoactivated by UVA (370 nm); creates covalent cross-links in collagen → increases corneal rigidity
  • Results: Halts progression in 90%+; may improve vision in some; minimal regression after 2 years
  • Technique: Epithelium removed (epi-off; standard) or intact (epi-on; faster recovery, less efficacy); riboflavin soaked, UVA exposure 3 W/cm² for 30 min
  • Contraindication: Corneal thickness <400 µm (risk of endothelial damage); epithelial dystrophies

Interstitial Keratitis & Corneal Dystrophies

PG
▼

Interstitial Keratitis (IK)

  • Definition: Inflammation of corneal stroma without primary epithelial involvement; immune-mediated
  • Causes: Congenital syphilis (Hutchinson's keratitis; 20% of congenitally syphilitic), TB, leprosy, Lyme disease, HSV (disciform variant)
  • Clinical features: Gradual onset; photophobia, watery; anterior chamber reaction (cells/flare); bilateral in syphilis (can be asymmetric)
  • Pathognomonic (syphilitic): "Salmon patch" (diffuse stromal hyperemia); "ghost vessels" (resolved neovascularization forming empty ghost-like outlines)
  • Outcome: Ghost vessels + salmon patch typically fade; residual scarring; vision may recover

Corneal Dystrophies

Post-transplant Management

  • Immunosuppression (PKP): Topical steroids (prednisolone 0.5% or dexamethasone, tapered over 6–12 months); topical calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine) for chronic rejection prevention
  • Rejection episodes (5–10% PKP, 1–2% DMEK): Sudden onset; Krachmer triad (keratic precipitates, anterior chamber cells, endothelial line). Rx: intensive steroids (topical q2h, systemic/IV if severe)
  • Complications: Infection, astigmatism (PKP), graft failure (endothelial cells decline ~0.6%/year, faster initially), ocular hypertension

MCQ Bank — Diseases of Cornea

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
Most common cause of microbial keratitis in soft contact lens wearers:
APseudomonas aeruginosa
BStaphylococcus aureus
CAspergillus fumigatus
DAcanthamoeba
Correct Answer: A — Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Pseudomonas: most common in contact lens wearers (especially extended wear, poor cleaning). Rapid progression (1 mm/day); yellow-green discharge; can perforate <7 days if untreated.
Q2 UG
Dendritic ulcer is pathognomonic of:
ABacterial keratitis
BFungal keratitis
CHSV-1 epithelial keratitis
DVZV keratitis
Correct Answer: C — HSV-1 epithelial keratitis
Dendritic ulcer: branching epithelial defect with terminal bulbs. Stains fluorescein (ulcer center), rose-bengal/lissamine green (infected epithelium borders). Treated with topical acyclovir 5% q2h.
Q3 PG
Geographic ulcer (expansion of HSV dendritic ulcer) occurs due to:
ASecondary bacterial superinfection
BTopical corticosteroid monotherapy suppressing local immunity
CInsufficient topical antivirals
DOral acyclovir resistance
Correct Answer: B — Topical corticosteroid monotherapy suppressing local immunity
Geographic ulcer: large amorphous ulcer from dendritic expansion when treated with steroids alone. ALWAYS combine antivirals (acyclovir 5% q2h) with any steroid use in HSV keratitis.
Q4 UG
Satellite lesions in fungal keratitis are caused by:
ASecondary bacterial invasion
BImmune reaction to fungal antigens
CToxins released by main colony
DSpread of fungal hyphae via corneal nerves or fluid streams
Correct Answer: D — Spread of fungal hyphae via corneal nerves or fluid streams
Satellite lesions (hallmark of fungal keratitis): scattered infiltrates adjacent to main ulcer. Indicate fungal spread. Associated with Aspergillus/Fusarium. Minimal pain (unlike bacterial).
Q5 PG
Best topical antifungal for Aspergillus keratitis:
ANatamycin 5% (polyene)
BFluconazole drops (azole)
CGriseofulvin ointment
DKetoconazole 1%
Correct Answer: A — Natamycin 5%
Natamycin: polyene; does not penetrate stromal (stays superficial; ideal for anterior keratitis). Preferred for Aspergillus/Fusarium. Amphotericin B 0.15% or miconazole 1% alternatives. Oral fluconazole/voriconazole adjunctive only.
Q6 UG
Munson's sign is a characteristic finding in:
AFuchs' dystrophy
BHSV keratitis
CKeratoconus
DBacterial corneal ulcer
Correct Answer: C — Keratoconus
Munson's sign: V-shaped deformation of lower eyelid on downward gaze (lower lid pushed by cone apex). Other signs: Fleischer ring (iron at cone base), Vogt's striae (vertical stress lines), Rizzuti's sign (conical reflex).
Q7 PG
Corneal cross-linking (CXL) for keratoconus is contraindicated if:
AKeratoconus stage III
BCorneal thickness <400 µm
CPrevious RGP lens wear
DHigh myopia (>−6 D)
Correct Answer: B — Corneal thickness <400 µm
CXL contraindication: thin cornea (<400 µm) risks endothelial damage from UVA penetration. Also contraindicated in epithelial dystrophies. Epithelium usually removed (epi-off standard); epi-on faster recovery but less effective.
Q8 UG
Ring infiltrate is a characteristic sign of:
ABacterial keratitis (any organism)
BHSV keratitis
CVZV keratitis
DAcanthamoeba keratitis
Correct Answer: D — Acanthamoeba keratitis
Ring infiltrate: concentric stromal density without ulceration; found in 50% of Acanthamoeba cases. Pathognomonic if present. Accompanied by perineural infiltration and pain out of proportion.
Q9 PG
DMEK (Descemet Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty) compared to DSAEK offers:
AFaster visual recovery
BBetter endothelial cell preservation
CBest visual acuity outcomes; minimal astigmatism; thinnest graft
DEasier surgical insertion
Correct Answer: C — Best visual acuity outcomes; minimal astigmatism; thinnest graft
DMEK: Descemet + endothelium only; ultrathin; best visual outcomes. Disadvantage: most technically difficult insertion; highest cost. DSAEK: larger graft (includes partial stroma); easier insertion but endothelial cell loss faster.
Q10 UG
Interstitial keratitis with anterior uveitis, "salmon patch" stromal hyperemia, and "ghost vessels" suggests:
ACongenital syphilis (Hutchinson's keratitis)
BTuberculosis
CLeprosy
DHSV disciform keratitis
Correct Answer: A — Congenital syphilis (Hutchinson's keratitis)
Salmon patch + ghost vessels are classic for congenital syphilitic IK. Bilateral (can be asymmetric). Often accompanied by Hutchinson's teeth, saddle nose, frontal bossing. May fade with residual scarring.

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Discuss bacterial corneal ulcer. Cover: common organisms, risk factors, clinical features, management, and complications. (12 marks)
Organisms: Pseudomonas (CL users; most aggressive), S. aureus (slow), S. pneumoniae (hypopyon). Risk: CL overuse, trauma, corneal disease. Features: severe pain, purulent discharge, hypopyon, stromal infiltration. Rx: culture, hourly topical antibiotics (fluoroquinolone/fortified), oral antivirals if needed. Complications: perforation, scarring, opacity, secondary glaucoma. Surgery if perforation risk (tissue adhesive, AMG).
University Clinical Exam
A patient presents with branching epithelial defect, photophobia, and anterior uveitis. Examine and classify the HSV keratitis. Discuss management at each stage. (8 marks)
Diagnosis: HSV epithelial keratitis (dendritic ulcer). Stages: 1) Epithelial — acyclovir 5% q2h + oral acyclovir 400 mg 4–5×/d (7–10 days); 2) Stromal/immune — topical steroids (prednisolone 0.5–1% q4h, taper 4–6 weeks) + antivirals (balance suppression vs inflammation); 3) Endotheliitis — aggressive steroids + antivirals; 4) Recurrence prophylaxis — oral acyclovir 400 mg bd long-term. Watch for geographic ulcer (steroid monotherapy risk).

Corneal Ulcer Organisms

  • Pseudomonas: CL (esp extended wear); aggressive (1 mm/day); yellow-green discharge; ring infiltrate possible
  • S. aureus: Slower; mucopurulent; chronic suppuration
  • S. pneumoniae: Hypopyon frequent; mucopurulent
  • Fungal: Satellite lesions (hallmark); dry, wrinkled; plant trauma history; slow progression

HSV Keratitis Forms

  • Epithelial: Dendritic (branching, terminal bulbs); fluorescein (ulcer), rose-bengal (borders)
  • Geographic: Large amorphous ulcer from dendritic expansion (steroids alone!); STOP steroids, add acyclovir
  • Stromal: Dense infiltration; immune-mediated; may progress despite antivirals
  • Disciform: Central disc stromal edema + epithelial dome; anterior uveitis
  • Endotheliitis: Anterior chamber pattern; endothelial cell loss risk

Keratoconus Management Progression

  • Stage I–II (mild): Spectacles (irregular astigmatism correction)
  • Stage II–III (moderate): RGP lenses (vault over apex) or Intacs (stromal rings)
  • Stage III–IV (advanced, thin <400 µm): Corneal cross-linking (CXL; halts progression 90%+)
  • End-stage: Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP; 90–95% success)
  • Signs: Munson's (V-lid), Fleischer (iron ring), Vogt's (striae), Rizzuti (cone reflex)

Corneal Transplant Types

  • PKP: Full-thickness; large incision; astigmatism, slow recovery (months); versatile
  • DALK: Anterior only; preserves host endothelium; preserves corneal sensation; technical difficulty
  • DSAEK: Posterior layers; small incision; quick recovery; endothelial loss over time
  • DMEK: Descemet + endothelium only; best VA; minimal astigmatism; most difficult insertion
