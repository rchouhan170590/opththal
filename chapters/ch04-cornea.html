Corneal Anatomy and Properties

The Five Layers

The cornea is a transparent, avascular, densely innervated structure forming the anterior one-sixth of the fibrous coat of the eye. Its thickness is approximately 540 microns centrally and 700 microns peripherally. Understanding each layer is not just anatomy for examination purposes — it directly determines how diseases behave and how they are treated.

The epithelium consists of five to six layers of stratified squamous non-keratinised cells resting on a basement membrane. The basal cells divide continuously, with daughter cells migrating superficially and eventually shedding into the tear film over a cycle of about seven days. This rapid turnover means pure epithelial defects heal quickly and without permanent scarring — which is why a corneal abrasion heals overnight but a deeper injury does not.

Bowman's layer is an acellular condensation of randomly arranged type I and type V collagen fibrils. It is approximately 8 to 12 microns thick. The critical clinical point is that Bowman's layer cannot regenerate once destroyed. Damage to it, whether from infection, trauma, or dystrophy, leaves a permanent opacity. This is why certain infections — even after cure — leave anterior stromal haze.

The stroma constitutes 90% of the total corneal thickness. It is composed of approximately 200 to 250 lamellae of type I collagen arranged in a highly regular orthogonal pattern. This precise geometric arrangement is the primary basis of corneal transparency. Stromal keratocytes (modified fibroblasts) lie between the lamellae and are responsible for maintaining the extracellular matrix. The stroma has very limited regenerative capacity — healed stroma forms disorganised collagen that scatters light, i.e., a scar.

Descemet's membrane is the basement membrane secreted by the endothelium. Unlike Bowman's layer it is elastic and surprisingly strong — it can withstand considerable intraocular pressure even when the overlying stroma has thinned dramatically (as in descemetocele formation). It thickens progressively throughout life, from about 3 microns at birth to 10 to 12 microns in adults. The guttata of Fuchs dystrophy represent abnormal focal thickenings of Descemet's membrane produced by diseased endothelial cells.

The endothelium is a single layer of hexagonal cells approximately 5 microns thick. The cell count at birth is around 3500 cells/mm2, falling to about 2500 cells/mm2 in adulthood through normal attrition. The critical threshold below which corneal decompensation occurs is generally considered to be around 500 to 700 cells/mm2. Endothelial cells cannot regenerate; when a cell is lost, neighbouring cells enlarge and migrate to cover the gap, a process called polymegethism and pleomorphism. The endothelium maintains corneal clarity by actively pumping water out of the stroma against an osmotic gradient, preventing it from swelling. When the pump fails — from disease, trauma, or surgery — the cornea swells, light scatters, and vision is lost.

Transparency Mechanisms

Four factors maintain corneal transparency. First, the precise uniform spacing of stromal collagen fibrils (18 to 30 nm diameter, spaced 60 nm apart) causes destructive interference of scattered light. Second, the relative dehydration of the stroma — maintained at 78% water content by the endothelial pump — preserves the lattice geometry. Third, the absence of blood vessels eliminates light-scattering cells and haemoglobin. Fourth, the absence of myelin in corneal nerve fibres prevents scatter at the nerve level.

Nerve Supply and Corneal Sensitivity

The cornea is the most densely innervated tissue in the body, supplied by the long ciliary nerves from the nasociliary branch of the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve. Approximately 70 to 80 nerve trunks enter the peripheral cornea at the mid-stromal level, losing their myelin sheaths at the limbus. They branch extensively within the stroma, then penetrate Bowman's layer to form the sub-basal plexus, from which free nerve endings ascend into the epithelium.

This dense innervation explains why corneal disease is often exquisitely painful. It also explains a clinically important diagnostic sign: conditions that damage corneal nerves — most notably HSV keratitis and Acanthamoeba keratitis — reduce corneal sensitivity, which you can test with a wisp of cotton or the Cochet-Bonnet aesthesiometer. A painless-appearing but severe-looking corneal ulcer with reduced sensation should immediately raise the suspicion of herpetic disease.

Corneal vascularisation: The normal cornea has no blood vessels — this is called an "immune-privileged site." Neovascularisation (pannus formation) can occur in chronic disease such as trachoma, contact lens hypoxia, or chemical injury. New vessels carry immune cells into what was previously a sanctuary site, which dramatically increases the risk of graft rejection if keratoplasty is subsequently needed.

Bacterial Keratitis

Common Organisms and Risk Factors

Bacterial keratitis is a sight-threatening emergency. The causative organism varies predictably with the risk factor, which is worth memorising for examination purposes. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the classic organism in contact lens wearers — it has particular affinity for the hydrogel material, produces proteases that cause rapid stromal melting, and can perforate a cornea within 48 hours if untreated. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism in non-contact-lens-related bacterial keratitis, particularly in patients with blepharitis. Streptococcus pneumoniae causes a characteristically hypopyon ulcer that tends to spread centripetally. Moraxella lacunata is strongly associated with alcoholism and debilitated states — an indolent ulcer in a malnourished patient should prompt this consideration. Nocardia should be considered after trauma with soil contamination; it produces a slow-growing ulcer with a wreath-like infiltrate.

Clinical Features

Bacterial keratitis presents acutely with pain, photophobia, lacrimation, and reduced vision. On examination you will see a stromal infiltrate — a white or grey-white opacity within the cornea — which may be surrounded by a zone of epithelial loss. The infiltrate is typically dense, with poorly defined edges in rapidly progressive infections like Pseudomonas. A hypopyon (sterile layering of inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber) is present in about 30% of cases and indicates a severe immune response, not direct bacterial invasion of the anterior chamber.

Pseudomonas ulcers deserve special mention because of their speed and severity. The organism secretes alkaline protease and elastase, causing rapid stromal dissolution (keratolysis). The ulcer base is characteristically necrotic, with a surrounding ring infiltrate and profuse mucopurulent discharge. This is one of the few corneal conditions where you can lose significant vision within a day or two.

Investigation — Corneal Scraping

Before starting treatment in any non-trivial bacterial ulcer, scraping is mandatory. Under slit lamp magnification, the advancing edge and base of the ulcer are scraped with a sterile Kimura spatula or the bent tip of a 26-gauge needle. Material is directly inoculated onto blood agar, chocolate agar, and Sabouraud's dextrose agar plates, and smears are made on glass slides for Gram stain (bacteria), KOH mount (fungal hyphae), Giemsa stain (Acanthamoeba, chlamydia), and acid-fast stain (Nocardia, Mycobacterium). The Gram stain result guides empirical therapy adjustment while cultures are pending.

Management

Topical fluoroquinolones are the first-line agents. Ciprofloxacin 0.3% or moxifloxacin 0.5% is started hourly (even through the night for the first 48 hours in severe cases). For sight-threatening ulcers near the visual axis, or when resistance is suspected, fortified antibiotics are used: fortified cefazolin 5% (for Gram-positive cover) combined with fortified tobramycin 1.3% or gentamicin 1.5% (for Gram-negative cover). The combination provides broad coverage while culture results are awaited.

Cycloplegic drops (atropine 1% or homatropine 2%) are used to reduce ciliary spasm and prevent synechiae formation. Topical steroids are generally avoided until the infection is controlled, though in certain circumstances — particularly to limit immune-mediated stromal melting after bacterial load is reduced — they may be introduced cautiously after 48 to 72 hours of antibiotic therapy.

Never patch a bacterial ulcer. Patching creates the warm, humid environment ideal for bacterial proliferation. Always keep the eye open to air exposure and maintain frequent antibiotic instillation.

Herpes Simplex Keratitis — Types and Clinical Patterns

HSV-1 is responsible for nearly all cases of herpetic eye disease in adults; HSV-2 occasionally causes neonatal keratitis. The virus establishes latency in the trigeminal ganglion after primary infection and reactivates under triggers such as UV exposure, fever, stress, or immunosuppression. Each reactivation episode causes further corneal damage, so recurrence prevention is as important as treating the acute episode.

Epithelial (dendritic) keratitis is the classic presentation. The dendrite is a linear branching epithelial ulcer with terminal bulbs at its ends — these bulbs are pathognomonic of HSV and distinguish it from the pseudodendrite of HZO. The ulcer stains vividly with fluorescein (the ulcer base itself) and with Rose Bengal (the surrounding devitalised epithelial cells). Corneal sensation is reduced even at this early stage, which is a valuable diagnostic clue. The dendrite can enlarge into a geographic ulcer — a large amoeboid epithelial defect with undermined, virally infected edges.

Stromal keratitis occurs in two forms. Immune stromal (disciform) keratitis is characterised by a round central or paracentral stromal disc of oedema with associated Descemet's folds and fine keratic precipitates on the endothelium beneath the disc — the Wessely immune ring may be visible. This is an immune-mediated reaction to viral antigen deposited in the stroma and is treated with topical corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5% to 1%) combined with prophylactic antiviral cover. Necrotising stromal keratitis is less common but more destructive; it involves direct viral replication within the stroma with intense inflammation, vascularisation, and potential for perforation.

Endotheliitis is a third pattern in which the endothelium is directly affected, leading to sectoral or diffuse corneal oedema with keratic precipitates in a linear or disciform pattern. It can closely mimic acute angle-closure glaucoma with corneal oedema, so IOP measurement is important.

Treatment of HSV Keratitis

For epithelial keratitis, topical acyclovir 3% ointment five times daily for two weeks is the standard. Systemic acyclovir 400 mg twice daily (prophylactic) or 400 mg five times daily (therapeutic) is used for stromal and endothelial disease. The HEDS (Herpetic Eye Disease Study) trials established that oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily significantly reduces recurrences when given long-term — current practice is to consider prophylaxis for at least one year in patients with two or more episodes per year. Debridement of the dendrite used to be practiced but is no longer recommended with effective antiviral agents available.

Topical steroids are contraindicated in active epithelial HSV keratitis. They allow viral replication to proceed unchecked, rapidly enlarging the ulcer. They are appropriate in immune stromal disease but only under antiviral cover.

Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO)

HZO results from reactivation of varicella zoster virus in the trigeminal ganglion, affecting the ophthalmic division (V1). Ocular involvement occurs in about 50% of cases and is predicted by Hutchinson's sign — vesicles on the tip of the nose (nasociliary nerve territory). The external appearance of the rash does not predict the severity of ocular disease.

Corneal manifestations of HZO are varied and differ importantly from HSV. The pseudodendrite of HZO has a mucus plaque overlying intact (or minimally disrupted) epithelium, without true epithelial ulceration and without terminal bulbs — it stains better with Rose Bengal than with fluorescein. Stromal keratitis, disciform oedema, and neurotrophic keratopathy (from cranial nerve V damage) can all occur. Neurotrophic keratopathy is perhaps the most debilitating long-term complication: the denervated cornea heals poorly, develops persistent epithelial defects, and can progress to frank corneal melting.

Treatment is with oral acyclovir 800 mg five times daily, valacyclovir, or famciclovir, started within 72 hours of rash onset. Earlier treatment reduces the severity and duration of ocular disease and the risk of post-herpetic neuralgia.

Fungal Keratitis

Fungal keratitis tends to be indolent but ultimately devastating if the diagnosis is delayed. In India and other tropical countries, it is actually more common than bacterial keratitis in some regions, largely because of agricultural trauma — paddy farmers sustaining eye injuries with vegetative matter are at particular risk. Filamentary fungi (Aspergillus, Fusarium) predominate after trauma with plant material. Candida species tend to affect immunosuppressed patients or those on prolonged topical steroids.

The clinical appearance differs enough from bacterial keratitis to allow a clinical diagnosis in experienced hands, though laboratory confirmation is essential. The infiltrate has a distinctive dry, rough texture and feathery, irregular borders rather than the smoother edges of bacterial infiltrates. Satellite lesions — small separate infiltrates at a distance from the main lesion — are a hallmark of fungal keratitis and reflect the ability of hyphae to spread through stromal lamellae. An immune ring (Wessely ring) may be present. The hypopyon in fungal keratitis tends to be thick, immobile, and difficult to shift — partly because fungal elements physically enter the anterior chamber through Descemet's membrane in severe cases.

Investigation requires corneal scraping sent for 10% KOH preparation (which dissolves epithelial cells and reveals the hyphae), Gram stain, and culture on Sabouraud's dextrose agar at both 25°C and 37°C. Confocal microscopy has emerged as a valuable non-invasive tool, allowing visualisation of fungal hyphae in vivo.

Treatment centres on natamycin 5% (the only topical antifungal approved specifically for keratitis, effective against filamentary fungi), with voriconazole 1% used for Aspergillus and Candida, and for cases not responding to natamycin. Oral voriconazole penetrates the cornea well and is added in severe cases. Treatment duration is prolonged — typically six to eight weeks — and the temptation to add steroids must be firmly resisted, as they dramatically worsen fungal keratitis.

Acanthamoeba Keratitis

Acanthamoeba is a free-living amoeba found ubiquitously in soil, fresh water, tap water, swimming pools, and hot tubs. Keratitis occurs almost exclusively in contact lens wearers, especially those who use tap water for lens cleaning or who swim with lenses in. The organism exists in two forms: the active trophozoite and the highly resistant double-walled cyst — it is the cyst form that makes treatment so difficult and prolonged.

The classic presentation is a contact lens wearer with severe ocular pain that seems out of proportion to the slit lamp findings early in the disease. This disparity is because the organism preferentially infects corneal nerves before producing a visible infiltrate — radial keratoneuritis (radial perineurial infiltrates following nerve paths) is an early, pathognomonic sign when present. As the disease progresses, a ring-shaped stromal infiltrate develops, which is the most characteristic slit lamp finding. The ring infiltrate represents the immune response to Acanthamoeba antigens and when present is virtually diagnostic.

Diagnosis is confirmed by corneal scraping with culture on non-nutrient agar seeded with E. coli (the amoeba feeds on the bacteria) or by confocal microscopy identifying the characteristic double-walled cysts in vivo. PCR of corneal scraping material is increasingly available and highly sensitive.

Treatment consists of two biguanide agents used in combination: polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) 0.02% and chlorhexidine 0.02%, given initially every hour around the clock and tapered over many months. The total treatment duration is typically six to twelve months because of the cyst form. Propamidine isethionate (Brolene) 0.1% may be added as a third agent. Topical steroids are generally avoided in early disease but may have a role in controlling severe inflammation in later stages.

The diagnostic trap: Acanthamoeba keratitis is frequently misdiagnosed as HSV keratitis because of the reduced corneal sensation and the superficial irregular epithelial changes. The key differentiating features are: contact lens wear history, the severity of pain relative to signs, radial keratoneuritis, and the lack of response to antiviral treatment.

Corneal Ulcer — General Management and Complications

Grading Severity

Several grading systems exist; the Mondino grading for corneal ulcers based on size, depth, and location is used clinically. A small, peripheral, superficial ulcer in a healthy patient carries a very different prognosis from a large, central, deep ulcer approaching Descemet's membrane. Any ulcer within 2 mm of the visual axis threatens vision directly; any ulcer involving more than one-third of the stromal depth is at risk of perforation.

The Descemetocele and Perforation

When infection destroys all overlying stroma, Descemet's membrane is left as the only barrier to perforation. This produces a descemetocele — a transparent, clear bulge at the base of the ulcer that has the appearance of a dark hole under slit lamp illumination (because you are seeing into the anterior chamber). Descemet's membrane, being elastic, can hold the intraocular pressure for a surprising time. However, any Valsalva manoeuvre — coughing, sneezing, straining — can cause it to rupture.

A frank corneal perforation is a surgical emergency. The immediate goal is to reform the anterior chamber and maintain globe integrity. Small perforations may be managed with tissue adhesive (cyanoacrylate glue) and a bandage contact lens as a temporising measure. Larger perforations require emergency therapeutic keratoplasty — a keratoplasty done not to restore vision but to save the eye. In resource-limited settings or where donor tissue is unavailable, amniotic membrane transplantation or conjunctival flap (Gundersen flap) can be used as bridging measures.

Amniotic Membrane Transplantation

Preserved amniotic membrane has anti-inflammatory, anti-scarring, and epitheliotrophic properties. It is used in corneal ulcers to promote healing, reduce inflammation, and decrease vascularisation. It can be sutured as an inlay (within the ulcer bed) or as an onlay (over the surface). The PROKERA device combines amniotic membrane with a rigid ring for self-retention.

Keratoconus

▼

Epidemiology and Pathogenesis

Keratoconus is a bilateral (though often asymmetric) progressive non-inflammatory ectasia of the cornea. The onset is typically in the second decade of life, with progression through the 20s and 30s, often stabilising in the 40s. The prevalence is roughly 1 in 2000 in the general population, though this may be an underestimate. Associated conditions include atopic disease (hay fever, eczema, asthma), Down syndrome, Leber's congenital amaurosis, Marfan syndrome, and — critically — chronic eye rubbing, which is now believed to be a major modifiable risk factor and is thought to mechanically degrade stromal collagen.

The pathological substrate is progressive thinning and weakening of the central and paracentral stroma. Stromal lamellae slip and rearrange, causing the cone-shaped protrusion. Iron deposition occurs at the epithelial level at the base of the cone — this is the Fleischer ring, best seen on cobalt blue illumination. Stress lines within the deep stroma appear as Vogt's striae, which characteristically disappear temporarily when you apply gentle pressure to the globe.

Clinical Signs

The signs of keratoconus span the full spectrum of clinical examination tools. On unaided inspection, advanced cases show Munson's sign — the V-shaped deformation of the lower eyelid caused by the protruding cone when the patient looks downward. Retinoscopy shows a characteristic scissors reflex, and the oil-drop sign (Charleaux sign) is visible as a dark oily shadow within the reflex. On slit lamp, Vogt's striae, Fleischer ring, apical corneal thinning, and subepithelial scarring at the cone apex are seen. Rizzuti's sign is the conical reflection seen on the nasal limbus when a penlight is shone from the temporal side.

Mnemonic — Signs of Keratoconus
FMVR: Fleischer ring, Munson's sign, Vogt's striae, Rizzuti's sign. To these add the Charleaux oil-drop on retinoscopy and the scissors reflex.

Investigations and Classification

Corneal topography is the most important investigation. It maps the corneal curvature and produces colour-coded maps where warmer colours (red, orange) represent steeper (more curved) areas. The classic keratoconus pattern is an inferocentral or inferior steepening producing an asymmetric bowtie or skewed bowtie pattern. The Amsler-Krumeich classification divides keratoconus into four grades based on keratometric values, best-corrected visual acuity, and pachymetry (corneal thickness at the thinnest point), with Grade IV representing K values above 55 D, BCVA worse than 6/60, and central scarring.

Corneal pachymetry (measuring thickness, ideally with Pentacam or OCT) documents progressive thinning. A thinnest point below 400 microns indicates advanced disease. Slit lamp biomicroscopy with retroillumination is valuable for visualising the striae and Fleischer ring.

Management Progression

In early keratoconus, spectacle correction or soft contact lenses compensate for the irregular astigmatism. As the cone progresses, rigid gas-permeable (RGP) contact lenses become necessary — they vault over the irregular surface and create a smooth refracting interface with the tear film.

Corneal collagen crosslinking (CXL) is the only treatment that halts progression. It involves application of riboflavin (vitamin B2) drops to the de-epithelialised cornea followed by ultraviolet A (UVA) irradiation at 365 nm. The photochemical reaction generates reactive oxygen species that form covalent bonds between collagen fibrils, stiffening the stroma and preventing further ectasia. The Dresden protocol (3 mW/cm2 for 30 minutes) is standard; accelerated protocols (9 or 18 mW/cm2 for shorter times) are increasingly used. CXL works best when there is still adequate corneal thickness (minimum 400 microns after de-epithelialisation) and in eyes with documented progression.

INTACS (intrastromal corneal ring segments) are polymethylmethacrylate ring segments inserted into the mid-peripheral stroma via femtosecond laser-created channels. They flatten the cone by creating a bending effect, improving BCVA and contact lens tolerance, but they do not halt progression.

When contact lenses are no longer tolerated and vision is unacceptably reduced, keratoplasty is indicated. DALK is preferred because it replaces the diseased stroma while preserving the healthy host endothelium, eliminating the risk of endothelial rejection and generally producing excellent visual outcomes.

Acute hydrops: In advanced keratoconus, a sudden rupture of Descemet's membrane allows aqueous to flood the stroma, causing acute massive corneal oedema — this is acute hydrops. The eye becomes suddenly very painful with a dramatic increase in corneal opacification. Treatment is conservative (cycloplegics, hypertonic saline, contact lens); the oedema slowly resolves over three to four months as Descemet's membrane heals, often leaving a dense scar. Paradoxically, the scar may actually flatten the cone slightly.

Classification by Layer

Corneal dystrophies are inherited, bilateral, symmetric, non-inflammatory corneal opacities that are not related to systemic disease or environmental factors. The IC3D (International Committee for Classification of Corneal Dystrophies) classification organises them by the corneal layer primarily affected.

Epithelial and Subepithelial Dystrophies

Epithelial basement membrane dystrophy (map-dot-fingerprint; Cogan's microcystic dystrophy; anterior basement membrane dystrophy, ABMD) is the most common corneal dystrophy overall. It results from abnormal epithelial basement membrane that extends intraepithelially, trapping cells and creating cysts (dots), geographic maps, and fingerprint lines visible with retroillumination. Most patients are asymptomatic but recurrent corneal erosions — typically causing sudden sharp pain on waking as the eyelid sticks to the loose epithelium — are the main clinical problem. Treatment of erosions involves hypertonic saline drops and ointment (dehydrating the oedematous epithelium), followed by diamond burr polishing or phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK) for refractory cases.

Bowman's Layer Dystrophies

Reis-Bucklers dystrophy affects Bowman's layer and the superficial stroma. It presents in the first decade with recurrent painful erosions, followed by progressive anterior stromal haze that reduces vision. The opacity has a geographical or honeycomb pattern. It is caused by mutations in the TGFBI gene (the same gene responsible for granular, lattice, and Avellino dystrophies). Treatment is PTK or superficial keratectomy, though recurrence in the graft or residual host cornea is common.

Stromal Dystrophies

The three classic stromal dystrophies are granular, macular, and lattice — these are the ones most heavily tested in examinations.

Granular dystrophy (Groenouw type I) shows discrete white crumb-like or bread-crumb deposits in the central anterior stroma, with clear intervening stroma. The deposits are composed of hyaline (a non-specific protein). Staining: Masson trichrome stains deposits red. It is autosomal dominant (TGFBI mutation). Vision is relatively well-preserved for many years because the clear intervals allow functional vision.

Macular dystrophy (Groenouw type II) is the most severe of the three. The deposits are diffuse, grey-white, with indistinct edges and no clear intervals — the entire stroma from limbus to limbus is eventually involved. It is the only stromal dystrophy that is autosomal recessive and results from a defect in the CHST6 gene encoding the enzyme that sulfates keratan. The accumulated material is abnormal keratan sulfate proteoglycan. Staining: Alcian blue and colloidal iron stain the deposits blue. Despite being the most severe, it tends to present earlier, leading to keratoplasty in the 30s or 40s.

Lattice dystrophy shows branching eosinophilic lines in the anterior to mid-stroma, with a ground-glass haze between them. The deposits are amyloid, staining with Congo red and showing apple-green birefringence under polarised light. It is autosomal dominant (TGFBI). Recurrent erosions are common because of subepithelial amyloid.

Fuchs Endothelial Dystrophy

Fuchs is the most clinically important endothelial dystrophy and the most common indication for keratoplasty in Western countries. It is more common in women (2:1) and tends to become symptomatic in the fifth to sixth decade, though the underlying endothelial pathology begins decades earlier.

The endothelium secretes abnormal thickened Descemet's membrane and develops focal excrescences called guttata (singular: guttate). On slit lamp with specular illumination, these appear as beaten-metal or dew-drop pattern on the endothelial surface. As endothelial cell density falls below the threshold for adequate pump function, the stroma swells — first at night (because reduced evaporation in sleep allows fluid accumulation, producing worse vision in the morning that clears as the day progresses) and eventually permanently. In the final stage, bullae form in the epithelium (bullous keratopathy), rupture painfully, and the resulting epithelial erosions are exquisitely uncomfortable.

Medical management includes hypertonic saline drops and ointment (5% sodium chloride) to dehydrate the cornea osmotically. A hair dryer held at arm's length directed at the open eye for a few minutes in the morning can improve vision by evaporating excess epithelial fluid. Definitive treatment is endothelial keratoplasty — either DSEK/DSAEK or DMEK — both of which replace the diseased endothelium while leaving the anterior cornea intact.

GML: Granular (hyaline, Groenouw I, AD, bread crumbs, Masson trichrome red), Macular (keratan sulfate, Groenouw II, AR, diffuse, Alcian blue), Lattice (amyloid, AD, lines, Congo red apple-green). Severity in increasing order for keratoplasty timing: Granular last, Macular first.

Penetrating Keratoplasty (PK)

Penetrating keratoplasty involves replacing the full thickness of the recipient cornea with a donor button. The donor corneal button (typically 7.5 to 8.5 mm diameter) is cut with a trephine, and the recipient is trephined to a slightly smaller size (0.25 to 0.5 mm) to create a tighter fit. The donor button is sutured with 10-0 nylon using interrupted, continuous (16-bite), or combined sutures. The surgery is performed under general anaesthesia or peribulbar block.

Indications for PK include corneal scarring involving all layers, descemetocele, failed previous grafts, and conditions where lamellar techniques are not feasible. The optical outcome can be excellent (20/20 vision is achievable) but the rehabilitation is slow — visual acuity is often limited by high irregular astigmatism for 12 to 18 months, and sutures may need selective removal over 2 to 3 years. The major long-term risks are graft rejection and graft failure.

Lamellar Keratoplasty

The principle of lamellar surgery is elegant: replace only the diseased layer and leave the healthy ones. This reduces rejection risk (as less foreign tissue is transplanted) and preserves host tissue for future procedures if needed.

DALK replaces the entire stroma down to Descemet's membrane, leaving the recipient's own endothelium in place. The Anwar big-bubble technique is the most elegant approach: air is injected into the deep stroma, creating a uniform space between the stroma and Descemet's membrane, allowing the overlying tissue to be excised cleanly. Because the host endothelium is preserved, endothelial rejection is impossible, and the long-term graft survival is superior to PK. DALK is the procedure of choice for keratoconus, stromal dystrophies, and anterior corneal scars with intact endothelium.

DSEK/DSAEK replaces Descemet's membrane and endothelium along with a thin disc of posterior stroma. The donor lenticule is folded or rolled, inserted through a small incision, and unfolded in the anterior chamber. An air bubble holds it against the recipient stroma while it adheres. Visual recovery is faster than PK, but the stromal interface between donor and host causes some light scatter, limiting final visual acuity to around 20/30 to 20/40 in many cases.

DMEK transplants Descemet's membrane and endothelium alone, with no stromal component. The interface is theoretically perfect, and visual outcomes are the best of any keratoplasty technique — many patients achieve 20/20 or better. The tradeoff is technical difficulty: the bare Descemet's scroll is fragile and difficult to manipulate, and graft dislocation rates are higher than DSEK, requiring a return to theatre for re-bubbling in about 10 to 15% of cases.

Graft Rejection — Recognition and Treatment

Graft rejection is an immune-mediated attack on the donor tissue. It can affect the epithelium, stroma, or endothelium — endothelial rejection is the most sight-threatening because endothelial cells, once destroyed, cannot regenerate. Risk factors for rejection include vascularised recipient beds, large grafts, inferior-quadrant grafts, previous rejection episodes, and non-compliance with topical steroids.

The KASH mnemonic captures the cardinal signs: Keratic precipitates on the graft endothelium, Anterior chamber flare and cells, Stromal oedema of the graft, Host-graft junction Khodadoust line (a advancing line of keratic precipitates at the host-graft margin representing the endothelial rejection front). The patient typically notices sudden worsening of vision, redness, and discomfort.

Treatment must be immediate — every hour counts. Intensive topical steroids (prednisolone 1% every hour during waking hours, with ointment at night) are started. Subconjunctival dexamethasone injection provides a depot. Systemic prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day is used for severe or endothelial rejection. If treated promptly, many episodes can be reversed; delayed treatment means irreversible endothelial loss and graft failure.

Graft rejection is a medical emergency. Patients must be counselled to attend immediately if they notice redness, photophobia, or any reduction in graft clarity. They should be given written instructions and a contact number. Long-term topical steroid use (at least two years post-PK, indefinitely post-DMEK) significantly reduces rejection risk.

Slit Lamp Examination of the Cornea Examination Technique
1
Setup and Patient Positioning

Seat the patient at the slit lamp with the chin firmly in the chin rest and the forehead touching the bar. The outer canthus of the eye should be level with the reference mark on the upright. Ask the patient to look straight ahead and to blink normally — many patients stop blinking, which dries the cornea and makes findings harder to interpret. Dim the ambient room lighting.

2
Diffuse Illumination — The Starting Point

Start with a wide, diffuse beam at low magnification (6x or 10x). This gives an overview of the anterior segment and allows you to identify obvious pathology — corneal opacity, hypopyon, ciliary injection, and anterior chamber depth — before you narrow in. Use medium intensity; high intensity with a wide beam causes glare and discomfort.

Always examine the cornea in a systematic fashion: superior to inferior, centre to periphery. Begin with diffuse illumination before using focal techniques.
3
Focal (Direct) Illumination — Optical Section

Narrow the beam to a thin slit (1 to 2 mm wide) and angle the illumination column approximately 30 to 45 degrees from the observation axis. The slit cuts through the cornea as an optical section, allowing you to localise pathology to a specific layer. Epithelial defects and opacities appear in the anterior bright zone; stromal haze is in the middle third; Descemet's folds, KPs, and guttata are at the posterior face. Increase magnification to 16x or 25x for detail. Moving the slit beam across the cornea systematically (parallelepiped illumination — a wider slit section) allows better appreciation of the extent of disease.

4
Retroillumination

Decouple the illumination column and observation axis. Direct the light at the iris or the fundus reflex (set the slit lamp for indirect illumination) and observe the cornea against this reflected background light. Epithelial changes (maps, dots, fingerprints in ABMD), stromal vacuoles, and Vogt's striae in keratoconus are much more visible with retroillumination than with direct focal illumination. This technique is essential for subtle epithelial dystrophies.

5
Specular Reflection

Position the illumination and observation arms at equal and opposite angles from the corneal surface normal. In the specular reflection zone you see the mirror-like reflection of the endothelial cell mosaic. Normal endothelium has a uniform hexagonal cell pattern; Fuchs dystrophy shows dark areas (guttata) disrupting the mosaic. This is only a qualitative assessment — formal endothelial cell count requires specular microscopy.

Check for corneal clarity, presence of oedema (ground-glass appearance of stroma), Descemet's folds (indicating raised IOP or endothelial failure), and keratic precipitates on the endothelial surface.
6
Cobalt Blue Filter — For Staining

Insert the cobalt blue filter to examine fluorescein-stained areas. Fluorescein glows bright green under blue light, making epithelial defects and dendritic ulcers immediately visible. The Fleischer ring in keratoconus (iron deposits) is best seen under cobalt blue without fluorescein — it appears as a brownish ring at the cone base. Always examine with cobalt blue after staining the cornea.

Corneal Staining — Fluorescein and Rose Bengal Diagnostic Technique
1
Fluorescein Staining

Touch a sterile fluorescein strip moistened with normal saline to the inferior fornix, or instil a drop of fluorescein sodium 2% (without preservative if possible — preservatives quench fluorescence). Ask the patient to blink twice. Examine under cobalt blue illumination. Fluorescein pools in areas where the epithelial barrier is disrupted — ulcer bases, dendritic ulcers, and epithelial erosions glow green. The dendrite of HSV keratitis stains vividly. After the instillation rinse out, residual staining of devitalised cells or mucus can be seen but is less brilliant.

Fluorescein also detects a corneal perforation: aqueous leaking through the wound dilutes the fluorescein at the site, producing a dark stream in the bright fluorescent pool — this is Seidel's sign (positive Seidel).
2
Rose Bengal Staining

Rose Bengal 1% stains devitalised and dead cells as well as mucus. It stains areas that fluorescein does not because it marks cells that are alive but have lost their mucin coat. This is particularly useful in HZO (pseudodendrite stains bright red with Rose Bengal), keratoconjunctivitis sicca (interpalpebral staining in the 3 and 9 o'clock positions), and superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis. Warn the patient that Rose Bengal stings significantly on instillation — topical anaesthetic beforehand makes the examination more acceptable.

3
Lissamine Green

Lissamine green is increasingly replacing Rose Bengal in clinical practice because it stains the same structures without causing significant stinging. It is preferred for dry eye evaluation and is the stain of choice when patient comfort is a concern. It does not require as bright illumination and can be viewed without a filter.

Corneal Sensitivity Testing Neurological Assessment
1
Cotton Wisp Method (Bedside)

Twist a thin wisp of sterile cotton wool to a fine point. Approach the cornea from the side (so the patient cannot see the cotton coming and blink in anticipation). Touch the central cornea with the tip of the wisp. A normal response is an immediate blink reflex. Grading is simply: present, diminished, or absent. Compare each eye and test different corneal zones (central, superior, inferior, nasal, temporal). This is a crude but useful bedside test.

Always approach from the side, never from directly in front — a patient who sees the object approaching will blink voluntarily, giving a false positive.
2
Cochet-Bonnet Aesthesiometer

This instrument consists of a fine nylon monofilament of 0.12 mm diameter that can be extended from 5 mm to 60 mm length. The longer the filament, the lower the pressure it exerts on the cornea (the filament bends more easily). Testing begins with the filament at full length (60 mm) and is progressively shortened until the patient reports feeling the touch. The length at which sensation is first perceived is recorded as the threshold. Normal central corneal sensitivity is at 60 mm; values below 40 mm suggest significant hypoaesthesia. HSV keratitis, HZO, and contact lens wear all reduce corneal sensitivity in characteristic patterns.

Corneal Scraping for Microbiology Diagnostic Procedure
1
Indications and Preparation

Scraping is indicated for any corneal ulcer that is not obviously viral — bacterial, fungal, and Acanthamoeba keratitis all require microbiological confirmation before definitive treatment. The eye is anaesthetised with topical proxymetacaine 0.5% or oxybuprocaine 0.4%. Preservative-free anaesthetic should be used if possible, as preservatives have antimicrobial properties that can reduce culture yield. Prepare labelled culture plates (blood agar, chocolate agar, Sabouraud's) and clean glass slides in advance, and ensure the slit lamp is set up with a microscope light.

2
Technique

Using a sterile Kimura spatula or a bent 26-gauge needle mounted on a syringe, scrape the active edge and base of the ulcer firmly under slit lamp visualisation. Multiple scrapes are needed. Directly inoculate culture plates by making C-shaped streaks across the agar surface. Prepare smears by spreading the material evenly across the glass slide in one direction. Avoid going back and forth, which damages cells and organisms. For Acanthamoeba, additional material can be inoculated onto non-nutrient agar plates seeded with heat-killed E. coli.

Make smears first before plate inoculation, as cells adhere better to glass when fresh. Label plates clearly with the patient's name, date, and the type of culture requested.
3
Stains and Their Interpretation

Gram stain identifies bacteria (gram-positive cocci — Staphylococcus, Streptococcus; gram-negative rods — Pseudomonas, Moraxella). KOH 10% mount reveals fungal hyphae or yeast cells (look for septate hyphae in Aspergillus/Fusarium, or budding yeast with pseudohyphae in Candida). Giemsa stain shows Acanthamoeba trophozoites and cysts (and is also used for chlamydial inclusions). Ziehl-Neelsen or modified ZN stain detects Mycobacterium and Nocardia. Calcofluor white fluorescent stain is highly sensitive for both fungal hyphae and Acanthamoeba cysts when a fluorescent microscope is available.

Keratometry and Corneal Topography Imaging and Measurement
1
Keratometry (K Readings)

The keratometer (Javal-Schiotz or Bausch and Lomb type) measures the anterior corneal curvature in two principal meridians. It projects two mires onto the cornea and measures their separation, which reflects the radius of curvature. The results are expressed either as radius of curvature in millimetres or as dioptric power. Normal K readings are 41 to 45 D (about 7.5 to 8.2 mm radius). Steeper K values (above 47 D) suggest ectasia or keratoconus. Flat K values (below 40 D) may indicate corneal flattening after refractive surgery or contact lens wear. In keratoconus, the two K readings are significantly different (irregular astigmatism) and the axes may be oblique.

2
Placido Disc Topography

Placido disc systems project concentric rings onto the corneal surface and analyse their reflection. A normal cornea reflects perfect concentric circles (Placido rings). Distortion of these rings reveals surface irregularity. The computerised topographers (Orbscan, Humphrey) generate colour-coded maps — warm colours (red, orange) for steeper areas, cool colours (blue, green) for flatter areas. The most useful maps are the axial (sagittal) curvature map and the elevation maps. In keratoconus, the characteristic inferior steepening produces a hot-spot of orange or red inferocentrally.

The I-S (inferior-superior) value — the difference between inferior and superior corneal power — is a useful screening index. An I-S value above 1.4 D is considered suspicious for keratoconus.
3
Scheimpflug Tomography (Pentacam)

Scheimpflug-based systems (Pentacam, Galilei) rotate a slit-beam camera around the optical axis to capture thousands of elevation data points, generating both anterior and posterior corneal elevation maps as well as pachymetry (thickness) maps. In keratoconus, the posterior elevation map shows ectasia before the anterior surface changes become obvious, making this the most sensitive screening tool for early ectasia — critical before refractive surgery. The Belin-Ambrosio enhanced ectasia display integrates multiple parameters to give a summary risk score.

Definition and Epidemiology

Bacterial keratitis is a suppurative infection of the corneal stroma, constituting an ophthalmic emergency. Risk factors include contact lens wear (Pseudomonas), blepharitis (Staphylococcus aureus), trauma, immunosuppression, and alcoholism (Moraxella).

Clinical Features

Acute onset of pain, photophobia, lacrimation, and reduced vision. Slit lamp shows a stromal infiltrate (white/grey-white opacity) with or without overlying epithelial defect, surrounding stromal haze, and oedema. Hypopyon (sterile layered cells in anterior chamber) in 30% of cases. Conjunctival injection and chemosis are present. Pseudomonas ulcers: rapid melting, ring infiltrate, mucopurulent discharge. Moraxella: indolent, inferior paracentral. Streptococcus: central, spreads centripetally, large hypopyon.

Investigations

Corneal scraping (Kimura spatula or 26G needle): Gram stain, KOH mount, Giemsa stain; culture on blood agar, chocolate agar, Sabouraud's dextrose agar; antibiotic sensitivity. Confocal microscopy for Acanthamoeba if suspected. Anterior segment photography for documentation.

Management

Mild-moderate: topical fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin 0.3% or moxifloxacin 0.5%) hourly. Severe or sight-threatening: fortified antibiotics (cefazolin 5% + tobramycin 1.3% alternating hourly). Cycloplegia (atropine 1%) for ciliary spasm and synechiae prevention. Never patch. Adjust based on culture results. Steroids only after 48 to 72 hours of controlled infection if immune-mediated stromal melting is occurring. Therapeutic keratoplasty for perforation or descemetocele with impending perforation.

Complications

Perforation, descemetocele, endophthalmitis, corneal scarring, vascularisation, secondary glaucoma from synechiae.

Classification by Layer (Enumerate)

Epithelial/Subepithelial: ABMD (map-dot-fingerprint), Meesmann, Lisch. Bowman's: Reis-Bucklers, Thiel-Behnke. Stromal: Granular (Groenouw I), Macular (Groenouw II), Lattice (Types 1-4), Schnyder, Fleck, Posterior amorphous. Descemet's/Endothelial: Fuchs, PPMD (posterior polymorphous), CHED (Types 1 and 2).

Fuchs Endothelial Dystrophy — Details

Most common corneal dystrophy requiring keratoplasty. More common in females (2:1). Autosomal dominant with variable expressivity (SLC4A11, ZEB1, TCF4 gene mutations). Bilateral but often asymmetric.

Pathology

Endothelial cells secrete abnormal Descemet's membrane (thickened, multilayered) and focal excrescences called corneal guttata. As endothelial cell density falls below pump threshold (approximately 500-700 cells/mm2), the stroma swells. Bullae form in advanced disease (bullous keratopathy).

Clinical Features

Early: asymptomatic guttata on specular reflection (beaten-metal, dew-drop pattern). Symptomatic: morning blur improving through day (nocturnal corneal oedema). Advanced: persistent corneal oedema, Descemet's folds, bullae formation, painful blisters rupturing causing recurrent erosions, secondary bacterial keratitis, corneal scarring and vascularisation.

Investigations

Specular microscopy: reduced cell count, polymegethism, pleomorphism, dark guttata. Pachymetry: increased central thickness. OCT anterior segment: subepithelial bullae. Confocal microscopy: guttata morphology.

Management

Medical: hypertonic NaCl 5% drops/ointment, hair dryer technique (morning), bandage contact lens for bullous keratopathy pain relief. Surgical: DSEK/DSAEK (Descemet's stripping endothelial keratoplasty) or DMEK (Descemet's membrane endothelial keratoplasty — best visual outcomes). DMEK achieves 20/20 in many patients; higher graft dislocation rate requiring re-bubbling.

Discuss keratoconus — etiology, clinical features, investigations, classification, and management.
15 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition and Epidemiology

Bilateral, progressive, non-inflammatory axial ectasia characterised by thinning and anterior protrusion of the cornea. Prevalence 1 in 2000. Onset in 2nd decade; progressive until 30s-40s when it usually stabilises.

Etiology and Associations

Multifactorial: genetic predisposition (VSX1, SOD1 mutations reported), eye rubbing (major modifiable risk), atopic disease (eczema, asthma, hay fever), Down syndrome, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos), Leber's congenital amaurosis, retinitis pigmentosa.

Pathology

Stromal thinning, disruption of Bowman's layer, slippage of stromal lamellae, iron deposition at Fleischer ring, stress lines (Vogt's striae) in deep stroma. Progressive biomechanical instability leads to ectatic deformation.

Clinical Features

Symptoms: progressive myopia and irregular astigmatism, monocular diplopia, glare. Signs: Munson's sign (V-shaped lower lid deformation on downgaze), Fleischer ring (cobalt blue), Vogt's striae (pressure-relievable), Rizzuti's sign (nasal limbal reflection), Charleaux oil-drop on retinoscopy, scissors reflex, apical thinning, subepithelial scarring. Complication: acute hydrops (Descemet's rupture, sudden oedema).

Investigations

Corneal topography (Placido disc): inferior steepening, asymmetric bowtie, I-S value above 1.4 D. Pentacam (Scheimpflug): posterior elevation changes earliest, pachymetry map, Belin-Ambrosio display. Specular microscopy. Ultrasound pachymetry. Keratometry: high K values, irregular astigmatism.

Classification — Amsler-Krumeich

Grade I: K below 48 D, BCVA above 6/7.5, no scarring. Grade II: K 48-53 D, BCVA above 6/24. Grade III: K 53-55 D, BCVA above 6/60, pachymetry 200-400 microns. Grade IV: K above 55 D, BCVA below 6/60, pachymetry below 200 microns, central scarring.

Management

Spectacles for early mild astigmatism. RGP contact lenses for moderate irregular astigmatism. Corneal collagen crosslinking (CXL) to halt progression (Dresden protocol: riboflavin + UVA 3 mW/cm2 for 30 minutes; minimum 400 microns post-de-epithelialisation). INTACS intrastromal ring segments (femtosecond laser) to improve CL tolerance and BCVA. DALK (preferred keratoplasty — big-bubble technique) for CL intolerance and poor BCVA with clear endothelium. PK for Grade IV with scarring. Treat acute hydrops conservatively.

What is keratoplasty? Classify and describe the indications, steps, and complications of penetrating keratoplasty.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition

Keratoplasty (corneal graft or corneal transplantation) is a surgical procedure in which diseased corneal tissue is removed and replaced with healthy donor corneal tissue.

Classification

Full thickness: Penetrating keratoplasty (PK). Anterior lamellar: DALK (stromal disease, endothelium intact), SALK (superficial anterior lamellar). Posterior lamellar: DSEK/DSAEK, DMEK (endothelial disease). Keratoprosthesis (artificial cornea): Boston KPro Types 1 and 2 (for failed multiple grafts, severe dry eye, chemical burns).

Indications for PK

Optical (to restore vision): keratoconus (when DALK not feasible), corneal scars, failed previous grafts, bullous keratopathy, corneal dystrophies. Tectonic (to restore integrity): descemetocele, perforation, severe ulcer. Therapeutic: uncontrolled microbial keratitis. Cosmetic: leucoma.

Steps of PK

Donor preparation: eye bank cornea with endothelial cell count assessed; donor button cut 7.5 to 8.5 mm with trephine (on endothelial side to protect endothelium). Recipient: general anaesthesia or peribulbar block; Flieringa ring to prevent globe collapse; recipient trephination 0.25 mm smaller than donor; removal of recipient button; four cardinal sutures placed; continuous 10-0 nylon suture (16-bite) or combined interrupted and continuous; reformation of anterior chamber; keratometry intraoperatively to adjust sutures and minimise astigmatism; topical antibiotics and steroid at end.

Complications

Intraoperative: expulsive haemorrhage, iris/lens damage, vitreous loss. Early postoperative: wound leak, flat anterior chamber, primary graft failure (dead endothelium), elevated IOP, infection. Late: graft rejection (KASH signs), irregular astigmatism, suture-related (loosening, vascularisation, infection), graft failure, glaucoma, cataract, recurrence of original disease.

Describe HSV keratitis — types, clinical features, diagnosis, and treatment.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Causative Agent and Pathogenesis

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) causes nearly all herpetic eye disease in adults. Primary infection (usually in childhood, often subclinical) establishes latency in the trigeminal ganglion. Reactivation by UV light, stress, fever, immunosuppression causes recurrent ocular disease. Corneal nerve involvement explains reduced corneal sensation.

Types and Clinical Features

Epithelial keratitis (dendritic ulcer): Linear branching epithelial ulcer with terminal bulbs (pathognomonic). Stains with fluorescein (ulcer base) and Rose Bengal (surrounding devitalised cells). Reduced corneal sensation. Can enlarge to geographic ulcer (large amoeboid, undermined edges). No stromal involvement initially.

Stromal keratitis (disciform): Immune-mediated, not direct viral invasion. Round central/paracentral disc of stromal oedema with Descemet's folds, fine KPs beneath disc, Wessely immune ring. Intact epithelium typically. Recurrent episodes cause progressive stromal scarring.

Necrotising stromal keratitis: Direct viral replication in stroma; intense inflammation, vascularisation, progressive thinning; risk of perforation; rare but destructive.

Endotheliitis: Sector or diffuse corneal oedema with KPs on endothelium; may raise IOP; mimics acute glaucoma.

Neurotrophic keratopathy: Long-term sequel of cranial nerve V damage; non-healing epithelial defect, poor healing, risk of stromal ulceration and perforation.

Diagnosis

Clinical slit lamp examination is usually sufficient. Reduced corneal sensitivity (Cochet-Bonnet). Fluorescein staining. In atypical cases: viral culture from conjunctival swab, PCR for HSV DNA (highly sensitive), Giemsa smear (multi-nucleated giant cells in epithelial scrapings). Corneal confocal microscopy may show denervation changes.

Treatment

Epithelial keratitis: topical acyclovir 3% ointment 5 times daily for 14 days. Oral acyclovir 400 mg 5 times daily for severe cases. Debridement no longer routinely recommended. Steroids contraindicated. Disciform keratitis: topical prednisolone 0.5% to 1% under antiviral cover (topical acyclovir 3% or oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily). Necrotising keratitis: oral acyclovir 400 mg 5 times daily; steroids with great caution; keratoplasty for perforation. Recurrence prophylaxis: oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily or valacyclovir 500 mg once daily for at least one year (HEDS trial). Neurotrophic keratopathy: preservative-free lubricants, bandage contact lens, cenegermin (nerve growth factor eye drops, Oxervate), tarsorrhaphy for severe non-healing defects.

AIIMS 2020
In Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, why is vision worse in the morning and improves through the day?
AIIMS
Show Answer
Answer: During sleep, the eyelids are closed, reducing tear evaporation from the corneal surface. The endothelium in Fuchs dystrophy has reduced pump function and cannot adequately maintain corneal dehydration. Without the additional dehydrating effect of evaporation during waking hours, fluid accumulates in the corneal stroma and epithelium overnight, worsening the oedema. On waking, evaporation from the open eye surface acts as a supplementary dehydrating mechanism, gradually reducing the oedema over 1 to 2 hours and improving visual acuity. This is the explanation for the characteristic morning blur that clears through the day, which is pathognomonic of early Fuchs dystrophy. The hair dryer test (directing warm air at the open eye) exploits the same principle — by accelerating evaporation, it temporarily improves vision and can be used diagnostically.
