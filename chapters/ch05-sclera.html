Chapter 5

Diseases of the Sclera

Structure and pathology of the sclera — episcleritis, scleritis, staphyloma — and their clinical management.

UG Level PG Level

Scleral Anatomy

UG
▼
Key Fact: The sclera comprises 85% of the ocular surface and is composed of Type I collagen fibres arranged in a lattice pattern.
  • Composition: Type I collagen (68%), Type III collagen, proteoglycans
  • Thickness: 1 mm at equator, 0.3 mm at muscle insertions, 0.4–0.6 mm at optic nerve entry
  • Avascular: Nutrition from ciliary vessels via emissary canals and anterior ciliary arteries
  • Layers: Episclera (outermost, vascular) → Sclera proper → Internal limiting lamina
  • Emissary canals: Allow passage of veins, nerves, and short ciliary arteries through the sclera

Episcleritis

UGPG
▼
Definition: Inflammation of the episclera (vascular layer between conjunctiva and sclera). Benign, self-limiting, minimal systemic associations.

Types

TypeClinical FeaturesDurationPrognosis
SimpleSectoral hyperaemia, mild pain, photophobia1–2 weeksExcellent; no scarring
NodularPalpable nodule of inflamed episclera, hyperaemia2–4 weeksExcellent; may recur

Associated Conditions

  • Usually idiopathic (majority)
  • Rare systemic associations: Gout, syphilis
  • NOT associated with: Rheumatoid arthritis (RA associates with scleritis)

Treatment

  • Observation (majority resolve spontaneously)
  • Topical NSAIDs: Indomethacin, ketorolac (first-line symptomatic treatment)
  • Topical steroids: If recurrent or severe (prednisolone acetate 1%)
  • Systemic NSAIDs: Rarely needed

Scleritis — Serious Inflammation

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼
Definition: Inflammation of the sclera proper. More serious than episcleritis. Strong systemic associations, risk of blindness.

Classification

TypeFeaturesRisk Profile
ANTERIOR SCLERITIS
DiffuseWidespread scleral inflammation, mild stromal necrosisLow; best prognosis
NodularElevated nodule of sclera, more granulomatousModerate
Necrotising (with inflammation)Scleral necrosis + inflammation; scleral melting riskHIGH; vision-threatening
POSTERIOR SCLERITIS
PosteriorInflammation behind equator; exudative retinal detachmentHIGH; retinal detachment, optic nerve compression

Systemic Associations (Critical)

  • RA: Most common association (~5% of scleritis patients have RA)
  • SLE, Sjögren's, scleroderma
  • Vasculitis: Wegener's granulomatosis (ANCA), polyarteritis nodosa
  • IBD: Crohn's, ulcerative colitis
  • Infections: Herpes zoster (VZV), syphilis, TB
  • Malignancy: Can be paraneoplastic

Scleromalacia Perforans

  • Definition: Necrotising scleritis without inflammation in advanced RA
  • Mechanism: Immune complex deposition → scleral softening → painless perforation
  • Prognosis: Poor; can lead to phthisis bulbi

Treatment

  • NSAIDs: Systemic (indomethacin 50–100 mg/day) — first-line
  • Corticosteroids: Topical, periocular, or systemic (prednisolone 30–60 mg/day) for severe/necrotising
  • Immunosuppressants: Methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate for severe/refractory cases and vasculitis
  • Biologics: TNF-α inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab) for RA-associated scleritis
  • Systemic workup mandatory: ANA, RF, ANCA, ESR, chest X-ray

Staphyloma

UG
▼
Definition: Outward bulging of the uvea (iris, ciliary body, choroid) through a thinned/weakened sclera.

Classification by Location

TypeLocationCausesClinical Features
AnteriorCornea/limbus areaCorneal scarring, perforation (alkali burn, keratitis)Dark brown bulge at limbus; vision loss if central
EquatorialAround equatorHigh myopia, posterior scleritis, chronic glaucomaLateral bulge; often asymptomatic
PosteriorPosterior pole (around optic nerve)High myopia, progressive myopia, glaucomaMyopic degeneration; may threaten central vision

Pathophysiology

  • Scleral weakening from inflammation, infection, or thinning (as in myopia)
  • Elevated intraocular pressure → outward bulging of uvea
  • Most common in myopic eyes (myopic staphyloma)

Management

  • Conservative: Serial imaging (fundus photography, ultrasound) for monitoring
  • Surgical: Scleral reinforcement (homograft, autograft) if progressive or threatening vision

Scleritis vs Episcleritis — Key Differences

▼
FeatureEpiscleritisScleritis
Depth of inflammationEpisclera only (superficial)Sclera proper (deep)

Clinical Pearl
Phenylephrine test: Episcleritis hyperaemia blanches; scleritis does not. This helps differentiate the two at the bedside.

MCQ Bank — Diseases of the Sclera

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
The thickness of the sclera at the equator is approximately:
A0.3 mm
B0.5 mm
C1 mm
D2 mm
Correct Answer: C — 1 mm
The sclera is thickest at the equator (~1 mm) and thinnest at the muscle insertions (~0.3 mm) and around the optic nerve (0.4–0.6 mm). This variable thickness affects the location of staphylomas.
Q2 UG
Episcleritis is characterised by all EXCEPT:
ABlanching of hyperaemia with topical phenylephrine
BStrong associations with rheumatoid arthritis
CMild pain and photophobia
DSelf-limiting course over 1–4 weeks
Correct Answer: B — Strong associations with RA
  • Episcleritis is usually idiopathic and has NO strong systemic associations.
  • RA is associated with scleritis, not episcleritis.
  • Episcleritis typically blanches with phenylephrine, causes mild pain, and resolves spontaneously in 1–4 weeks.
Q3 PG
A 65-year-old woman with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis presents with painless progressive scleral thinning and softening. Which complication should you most anticipate?
APosterior synechiae formation
BAnterior chamber inflammation
CKeratitis
DScleral perforation
Correct Answer: D — Scleral perforation
This describes scleromalacia perforans — necrotising scleritis without inflammation in RA. The painless nature (no inflammation) and scleral melting lead to perforation and ultimately phthisis bulbi if untreated. Requires urgent systemic immunosuppression.
Q4 UG
The first-line treatment for symptomatic episcleritis is:
ASystemic corticosteroids
BTopical NSAIDs
CImmunosuppressants
DPeriocular steroid injection
Correct Answer: B — Topical NSAIDs
Episcleritis is benign and self-limiting. Topical NSAIDs (ketorolac, indomethacin) are used for symptomatic relief. Most cases resolve spontaneously without treatment. Steroids are reserved for recurrent cases.
Q5 PG
Which finding would differentiate scleritis from episcleritis on clinical examination?
ASectoral hyperaemia
BBlanching with phenylephrine
CDeep scleral pain, worse at night
DMild photophobia
Correct Answer: C — Deep scleral pain, worse at night
Scleritis causes characteristic deep pain that wakes the patient at night. This is due to deep scleral involvement and associated inflammation. Episcleritis causes only mild discomfort. The pain is the key differentiator clinically.
Q6 PG
A patient with scleritis has hyperaemia that does NOT blanch with topical phenylephrine. This indicates:
AInflammation involves the deep sclera (not superficial episclera)
BPresence of bacterial infection
CImminent scleral perforation
DAssociated iritis
Correct Answer: A — Inflammation involves deep sclera
The phenylephrine blanch test differentiates depth: Episcleritis blanches (superficial vasculature); Scleritis does NOT blanch (deep scleral inflammation). This is a key clinical sign for differentiation.
Q7 UG
Which type of staphyloma is most common?
AAnterior staphyloma
BEquatorial staphyloma
CDiffuse staphyloma
DPosterior staphyloma
Correct Answer: D — Posterior staphyloma
Posterior staphyloma (myopic staphyloma) is the most common, occurring in high myopic eyes due to scleral weakening and progressive elongation. Associated with progressive myopia and myopic degeneration.
Q8 PG
Which systemic condition is MOST commonly associated with scleritis?
ADiabetes mellitus
BRheumatoid arthritis
CHypertension
DMyopia
Correct Answer: B — Rheumatoid arthritis
RA is the most common systemic association with scleritis. Scleritis occurs in ~5% of RA patients. Other associations include SLE, Wegener's, IBD, but RA remains the most frequent.
Q9 PG
Which immunosuppressant is commonly used as first-line for necrotising scleritis?
AMycophenolate mofetil
BCyclosporine
CMethotrexate
D6-Mercaptopurine
Correct Answer: C — Methotrexate
Methotrexate is commonly used as a steroid-sparing agent for severe/necrotising scleritis. Other options include azathioprine and mycophenolate. TNF-α inhibitors are used for RA-associated scleritis.
Q10 UG
Scleromalacia perforans is characterised by:
ANecrotising scleritis without inflammation in RA
BAcute necrotising scleritis with intense pain
CSuperficial episcleritis with nodule formation
DAnterior staphyloma in myopic eyes
Correct Answer: A — Necrotising scleritis without inflammation in RA
Scleromalacia perforans is a serious complication of advanced RA. It is painless necrotising scleritis due to immune complex deposition. The absence of pain is a key feature (no active inflammation). Can progress to perforation and phthisis bulbi.

University Practicals
What are emissary canals of the sclera? What structures pass through them?
Correct Answer: Emissary canals allow passage of short ciliary arteries, veins, and nerves through the sclera. Sclera is otherwise avascular; nutrition comes via these vessels.

Scleral Anatomy

  • Composition: Type I collagen (68%)
  • Thickness: 1 mm (equator), 0.3 mm (insertions)
  • Avascular; nutrition via emissary canals
  • 85% of ocular surface

Episcleritis vs Scleritis

  • Episcleritis: Superficial, painless, idiopathic, blanches with phenylephrine
  • Scleritis: Deep, painful, systemic assocs (RA), does NOT blanch
  • Scleritis types: diffuse, nodular, necrotising (anterior); posterior
  • Scleromalacia perforans = painless necrotising in RA

Scleritis Associations

  • Most common: RA (5% of RA)
  • SLE, Wegener's, Sjögren's, scleroderma
  • IBD (Crohn's, UC)
  • Infections: VZV, syphilis, TB

Staphyloma Types

  • Anterior: Corneal scarring (alkali burn)
  • Equatorial: Myopia, glaucoma, scleritis
  • Posterior (most common): High myopia, progressive myopia

Treatment Quick Reference

  • Episcleritis: Topical NSAIDs, supportive
  • Scleritis: Systemic NSAIDs, steroids, immunosuppressants
  • Severe/necrotising: MTX, azathioprine, TNF-α inhibitors for RA
  • Mandatory: Systemic workup (ANA, RF, ANCA, ESR, CXR)

Phenylephrine Blanch Test

  • Episcleritis: BLANCHES (superficial)
  • Scleritis: DOES NOT BLANCH (deep)
  • Key clinical differentiator at bedside
