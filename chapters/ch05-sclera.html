Chapter 5

Diseases of the Sclera

Episcleritis, scleritis, staphyloma — their differentiation, systemic associations and management.

UG

The sclera is deceptively simple at first glance, but episcleritis and scleritis are among the most commonly confused conditions in the exam hall. These cards reduce the confusion to something you can hold in your head in two minutes.

Episcleritis vs Scleritis

The two conditions look similar on first inspection but are fundamentally different in depth and danger.

  • Episcleritis — superficial episcleral vessels, sectoral or diffuse redness, mild discomfort at most, blanches with phenylephrine 2.5%, self-limiting over 2 to 3 weeks, usually no systemic disease
  • Scleritis — deep scleral involvement, blue-violet hue, boring severe pain that wakes the patient at night, does not blanch with phenylephrine, systemic association in over 50% (RA, Wegener's, SLE), can threaten vision

The key clinical test is phenylephrine 2.5%. Episcleritis blanches because the inflammation is in the superficial episcleral plexus. Scleritis does not blanch because the deep episcleral plexus is involved, and its vessels do not respond to vasoconstrictors.

High-Yield

Scleritis Classification

Watson and Hayreh's classification divides scleritis by location and severity.

  • Anterior diffuse — most common; generalised hyperaemia, blue-violet hue over anterior sclera
  • Anterior nodular — tender, immovable nodule fixed to underlying sclera (unlike episcleritis nodule which moves freely)
  • Necrotising with inflammation — most severe; avascular area, risk of perforation, scleral thinning exposing dark uvea
  • Scleromalacia perforans — necrotising without inflammation; seen in long-standing RA; painless yellow-grey avascular patches; sclera perforated by uveal tissue without preceding inflammatory signs
  • Posterior scleritis — headache, proptosis, limited motility, choroidal folds on fundoscopy, disc oedema; T-sign on B-scan ultrasonography (fluid in Tenon's capsule)
High-Yield

Systemic Associations

Scleritis demands a systemic workup because it can be the presenting feature of a serious vasculitic or connective tissue disease.

  • Rheumatoid arthritis — most common systemic association with scleritis overall; scleromalacia perforans is almost exclusively RA
  • Wegener's granulomatosis (GPA) — necrotising scleritis; ANCA-positive; check c-ANCA (PR3)
  • SLE — anterior scleritis; ANA positive, anti-dsDNA
  • Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) — rare but aggressive necrotising scleritis
  • Relapsing polychondritis — scleritis with ear and nasal cartilage involvement

Episcleritis is different. About 30% is idiopathic. When systemic disease is present it tends to be minor inflammatory conditions — rosacea, gout, IBD. Episcleritis does not demand the same exhaustive workup as scleritis.

Phenylephrine Test and T-Sign

Phenylephrine 2.5%: Episcleritis BLANCHES — the superficial episcleral vessels constrict. Scleritis DOES NOT BLANCH — the deep episcleral plexus is unresponsive. A simple way to remember it: Epi = outside = blanches; Sclera = deep = doesn't.

T-sign on B-scan: In posterior scleritis, fluid accumulates in Tenon's capsule around the optic nerve. On B-scan ultrasonography, this appears as a dark (echolucent) fluid shadow extending alongside the optic nerve shadow, creating the shape of a letter T. This is pathognomonic of posterior scleritis.

Scleromalacia perforans — the only form of necrotising scleritis that is painless. In RA, immune complex deposition causes avascular necrosis without acute inflammatory pain. The blue-grey uveal tissue peeks through the destroyed sclera.

Treatment Algorithm for Episcleritis and Scleritis

1
Episcleritis (mild): Most cases resolve without treatment. Lubricating drops for comfort, cold compresses. Topical NSAID (ketorolac 0.5%) for symptomatic relief. Mild topical steroid (fluorometholone 0.1%) for recurrent or prolonged cases. Systemic workup only if bilateral or recurrent.
2
Scleritis (first line): Systemic NSAIDs — indomethacin 25 to 50 mg three times daily with food, or ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily. These work in diffuse and nodular anterior scleritis in 50 to 70% of cases. Treat the underlying systemic condition simultaneously.
3
Scleritis (resistant to NSAIDs): Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day, tapering slowly. Periocular steroid injection is generally avoided in scleritis as it can cause scleral thinning and perforation. This is a key exam point — never inject subconjunctivally in scleritis.
4
Scleritis (steroid-dependent or refractory): Immunosuppressive agents — methotrexate (7.5 to 15 mg weekly), azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil. For Wegener's-associated necrotising scleritis, rituximab (anti-CD20) or cyclophosphamide are often needed.
5
Necrotising scleritis: Urgent aggressive systemic immunosuppression. Treat underlying disease. Scleral patch graft may be needed for structural support where uveal tissue is exposed. Involves rheumatology from the outset.

Detailed Notes

Scleral Anatomy

UG
▼

The sclera forms the posterior five-sixths of the fibrous outer coat of the eyeball, with the cornea making up the anterior sixth. It is roughly 1 mm thick at the equator but becomes thinner just posterior to the rectus muscle insertions — down to about 0.3 mm — which is why perforations from strabismus surgery or trauma tend to occur in this zone. The sclera thickens again around the optic nerve to about 1 mm.

Histologically, the sclera has four layers. The outermost is the episclera, a loose vascular connective tissue layer that carries the episcleral blood vessels responsible for nourishment and inflammation. Beneath this lies the scleral stroma proper, which makes up the bulk — collagen bundles here run in multiple interlacing directions, unlike the regular parallel arrangement in the corneal stroma. This random organisation is what makes the sclera opaque rather than transparent. The innermost layers are the lamina fusca (brownish pigmented layer) and then the uvea on the internal surface.

Emissary canals are passages in the sclera through which nerves and vessels enter and exit the eye. The vortex veins, ciliary arteries and nerves, and the optic nerve all pass through scleral canals. These weak points can be sites of tumour or infective spread, and they are also relevant in glaucoma where the lamina cribrosa at the optic nerve head is essentially scleral tissue modified into a sieve.

The sclera is avascular in its stroma. It relies on the episcleral circulation anteriorly and the choroidal circulation posteriorly for metabolic support. This avascularity means that healing after injury is slow and fibrosis is the predominant repair mechanism.

Blue sclera occurs when the sclera is abnormally thin, allowing the underlying dark uveal tissue (choroid) to show through. The most classic cause is osteogenesis imperfecta type I, where defective type I collagen leads to a thin, translucent sclera. Other causes include Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and severe iron deficiency anaemia. Always examine the scleral colour when seeing a patient with joint hypermobility or frequent fractures.

One point worth knowing for the exam: what is commonly called "scleral icterus" in clinical medicine is actually bilirubin deposition in the conjunctival connective tissue rather than in the true sclera itself. The sclera itself has very little elastin and does not accumulate bilirubin significantly. Clinically the distinction does not matter, but for MCQs the correct statement is that jaundice causes yellowing of the subconjunctival tissue.

Episcleritis

UG
▼

Episcleritis is inflammation confined to the episcleral tissue — the superficial vascular layer overlying the sclera. It is far more common than scleritis and generally much less serious. The typical patient is a young adult, more commonly female, who presents with a sector of redness in one eye, mild grittiness or discomfort, but no significant pain. Importantly, visual acuity is unaffected.

There are two clinical forms. Simple episcleritis shows diffuse or sectoral hyperaemia without a discrete nodule. The redness often has a salmon-pink colour because it is in the superficial plexus. Nodular episcleritis presents with a slightly elevated, tender, rounded nodule that, unlike the nodule of scleritis, moves freely when you push it with a cotton-tipped applicator — this is because it is attached to the mobile episcleral tissue rather than to the fixed sclera.

The phenylephrine 2.5% test is the bedside way to confirm episcleritis. Apply two drops to the eye and wait 10 minutes. In episcleritis, the superficial episcleral vessels constrict and the redness clears dramatically. In scleritis, there is no blanching. This test works because phenylephrine acts on the superficial plexus but not on the deep episcleral plexus.

Episcleritis is usually self-limiting, resolving over 2 to 3 weeks without treatment. About 70% of patients have recurrent episodes. Systemic associations exist but are generally minor — rosacea, gout, inflammatory bowel disease — and are found in perhaps 30% of cases. The investigation workup does not need to be exhaustive for a single episode of simple episcleritis. However, nodular, bilateral, or frequently recurrent episcleritis does warrant a basic screen.

Treatment is directed at comfort. Lubricating drops help with the grittiness. Topical NSAIDs such as ketorolac 0.5% reduce discomfort. For severe or prolonged cases, a mild topical steroid such as fluorometholone 0.1% can be used for a short course. Systemic NSAIDs are rarely needed. Subconjunctival injection has been used historically but is not generally recommended today.

Scleritis — Classification and Management

UGPG
▼

Scleritis is a serious inflammatory disease involving the deep scleral tissue. The pain is characteristically boring, deep, and severe — patients describe it as a pain behind the eye or in the orbit, often radiating to the temples and jaw, and it regularly wakes them from sleep. This nocturnal worsening is an important exam feature. The sclera appears blue-violet rather than the bright red of episcleritis, because the inflamed deep episcleral plexus causes this characteristic hue.

The Watson and Hayreh classification remains the standard. Anterior scleritis accounts for about 95% of cases and is subdivided into diffuse, nodular, and necrotising types. Posterior scleritis is often missed because it has no visible anterior segment signs in early cases.

Diffuse anterior scleritis is the most common form and the most benign of the scleritis types. Generalised deep scleral hyperaemia with blue-violet discolouration is present, typically sectoral but sometimes circumferential. It responds reasonably well to systemic NSAIDs.

Nodular anterior scleritis presents with one or more tender nodules that are immovable — this distinguishes it from nodular episcleritis where the nodule can be moved with a cotton swab. The immobility reflects that the inflammation is anchored in the deep scleral stroma.

Necrotising scleritis with inflammation is the most dangerous form. An avascular area develops within the inflamed region, appearing pale or yellow-grey, surrounded by severe active inflammation. The sclera progressively thins, uveal tissue becomes visible through the thinned wall, and perforation can occur. This form is strongly associated with systemic vasculitis, particularly Wegener's granulomatosis and PAN. Aggressive systemic immunosuppression is mandatory.

Scleromalacia perforans is unique. It is necrotising scleritis without inflammation — the patient has no pain, and the eye is quiet. Yellow-grey avascular patches appear in the sclera, typically in patients with severe, long-standing rheumatoid arthritis. Immune complex deposition causes ischaemic necrosis. The dark uveal tissue becomes visible and may eventually protrude. Despite the dramatic appearance, there is often little discomfort. There is no effective treatment to reverse the process; systemic RA management is the only intervention.

Posterior scleritis is one of the great mimics in ophthalmology. It presents with ocular or periorbital pain, proptosis, restricted eye movements, and reduced vision. On fundoscopy, choroidal folds, annular choroidal detachment, disc oedema, and exudative retinal detachment may be seen. B-scan ultrasonography is the defining investigation — it shows thickening of the posterior sclera and fluid in Tenon's capsule around the optic nerve, producing the classic T-sign where the optic nerve shadow and the fluid collection form a T-shape.

Treatment of scleritis follows a stepwise approach. Systemic NSAIDs are first line for diffuse and nodular anterior scleritis — indomethacin 25 to 50 mg three times daily is the traditional choice, though flurbiprofen and ibuprofen are alternatives. When NSAIDs fail or disease is severe, oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day is added and tapered slowly. For refractory or steroid-dependent cases, methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil are used. Necrotising scleritis in the context of systemic vasculitis often requires cyclophosphamide or rituximab, and the involvement of a rheumatologist is not optional — it is necessary.

Periocular steroid injection — subconjunctival or sub-Tenon's injection of steroid — is contraindicated in scleritis. The scleral tissue is already weakened and inflamed, and depot steroid injection can precipitate scleral melt or perforation. This is a classic exam question and a common real-world error.

Staphyloma

UG
▼

A staphyloma is an ectatic bulge of the outer coat of the eye lined by uveal tissue. The term comes from the Greek word for grape, reflecting the appearance of uveal tissue bulging through weakened sclera or cornea. The defining feature is that uveal tissue lines the wall of the ectasia, distinguishing it from a simple dehiscence or hernia.

Anterior staphylomas are classified by location. A corneal staphyloma follows severe corneal ulceration where the cornea perforates, the iris plugs the gap, and subsequent ectasia occurs lined by iris tissue. The ectatic cornea becomes opaque and vascularised. A limbal (or intercalary) staphyloma occurs at the corneoscleral junction and is classically associated with absolute glaucoma or severe anterior uveitis that has caused circumferential scleral weakness. A ciliary staphyloma occurs just behind the limbus at the level of the ciliary body, most commonly after trauma or severe inflammatory disease.

Intercalary staphyloma is the one to know for the exam. It sits at the corneoscleral junction (at the level of the filtration angle, between the cornea and the iris attachment), and the classic association is absolute glaucoma with buphthalmos or advanced anterior uveitis. When you see this term in an MCQ, think of it as the staphyloma of the limbus.

Posterior staphyloma occurs in high myopia where progressive axial elongation and scleral weakness leads to an outpouching at or near the posterior pole. Curtin classified posterior staphylomas into ten types based on their location relative to the disc and fovea. The clinically relevant consequence of posterior staphyloma is that it distorts the shape of the posterior sclera and creates a tilted optic disc, myopic degeneration, posterior pole atrophy, and potentially lacquer cracks and choroidal neovascular membranes. On fundoscopy, the edge of the staphyloma appears as a crescent of atrophy or a visible boundary at the disc margin.

Treatment of staphyloma is largely supportive. Optical correction for the myopic error, management of associated complications (anti-VEGF for choroidal neovascularisation), and in some centres posterior segment reinforcement surgery (scleral buckle-type procedures) have been attempted for progressive posterior staphyloma, though the evidence remains limited.

Blue Sclera, Scleral Icterus and Other Scleral Conditions

UG
▼

Blue sclera results from abnormal thinning of the scleral stroma, which allows the underlying dark choroidal pigment to be visible through the translucent wall. In normal eyes, the scleral stroma is thick and opaque enough to mask the choroid completely. When collagen is deficient or structurally abnormal, as in osteogenesis imperfecta, the sclera thins and takes on a blue hue.

Osteogenesis imperfecta type I is the textbook cause of blue sclera. These patients have defective type I collagen synthesis, leading to brittle bones, short stature, hearing loss, and blue sclera. The blue colouration is actually due to the choroid being visible through the thin scleral wall. Other systemic conditions that can cause blue sclera include Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (connective tissue disorders broadly), and severe iron deficiency anaemia where scleral thinning occurs from nutritional deficiency.

Blue Sclera Causes

Think OME: Osteogenesis imperfecta (most classic), Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Add iron deficiency anaemia to complete the list. All share a common mechanism of deficient or structurally weak collagen leading to scleral thinning.

What clinical medicine calls "scleral icterus" deserves a specific note. The yellowing of the eye in jaundice is actually bilirubin deposition in the conjunctival and sub-Tenon's connective tissue, not in the sclera itself. The scleral stroma, being avascular, does not accumulate significant bilirubin. Clinically the distinction makes no difference — the yellowing is visible in the white of the eye — but for MCQs, be aware that the correct anatomical statement is that bilirubin deposits in the subconjunctival tissue.

When a patient presents with bilateral red eyes, the distinction between scleritis and uveitis can trip up junior residents. Scleritis has predominantly external signs (scleral injection, tenderness to palpation of the globe), whereas anterior uveitis has primarily internal anterior segment signs (KPs on endothelium, flare and cells in the anterior chamber, posterior synechiae, miosed pupil). The pain in scleritis is primarily external and periorbital; in uveitis it is a deep ache with photophobia. They can coexist — sclerouveitis — and in that context systemic disease is even more likely.

Two practical tests that every ophthalmologist should be able to perform and interpret at the bedside or in the outpatient clinic. The phenylephrine test is simple but frequently misused. B-scan ultrasonography for posterior scleritis is a skill that prevents this diagnosis from being missed for months.

Phenylephrine 2.5% Test for Episcleritis vs Scleritis Diagnostic Technique
1
Preparation and Rationale

Phenylephrine 2.5% is a selective alpha-1 adrenergic agonist that causes vasoconstriction of the superficial episcleral and conjunctival vessels. The principle of the test is straightforward: the superficial episcleral plexus (involved in episcleritis) is responsive to topical vasoconstrictors, while the deep episcleral plexus (involved in scleritis) is not. You need phenylephrine 2.5% specifically — 10% is too strong and causes excessive pupil dilation and can precipitate angle closure in a predisposed eye. Have a pen torch and ideally a slit lamp available for the assessment.

2
Technique

Instil 2 drops of phenylephrine 2.5% into the affected eye. Ask the patient to keep the eye open and gently closed for 30 seconds to allow distribution. Wait 10 minutes. Do not touch the eye during this time. Have the patient sit comfortably, and repeat your observation at the slit lamp using the same angle and beam width as your baseline assessment.

Always compare the fellow eye throughout. The fellow eye acts as your baseline — you are looking for a change in the test eye, not an absolute colour value. Note the hue of the redness before the drops go in; deep blue-violet engorgement of scleritis looks different from the bright salmon-pink of episcleritis even before the test.
3
Interpretation

After 10 minutes, assess the degree of blanching. In episcleritis, the superficial vessels constrict and the eye becomes markedly less red — you may see only a faint pink blush remaining over the area. In scleritis, the redness does not change appreciably. The deep episcleral vessels remain engorged and the blue-violet hue persists. The test is not perfectly binary; partial blanching can occur, but complete blanching strongly supports episcleritis and no blanching supports scleritis.

Remember that even after blanching in episcleritis, the deep scleral colour is normal. In scleritis, the blue-violet appearance persists because this colour reflects the deep vascular plexus, not the conjunctival vessels.
4
Pitfalls and Limitations

The test is not useful for posterior scleritis, which has no visible anterior redness. It should not be used if the patient has a history of narrow-angle glaucoma or if the anterior chamber appears shallow, as phenylephrine causes pupil dilation that can precipitate an acute angle-closure attack. In patients on monoamine oxidase inhibitors, systemic absorption of phenylephrine can rarely cause a hypertensive episode; use with caution. The test complements rather than replaces careful slit lamp examination — the pattern of vessels (radial episcleral vessels in episcleritis, plexiform deep engorgement in scleritis) is also diagnostically useful.

B-scan Ultrasonography for Posterior Scleritis Imaging Technique
1
When to Use B-scan for Posterior Scleritis

Posterior scleritis is one of the great diagnostic traps in ophthalmology. The patient presents with eye pain, proptosis, restricted movements, and reduced vision — a picture that can mimic orbital cellulitis, myositis, an orbital mass, or even a choroidal tumour. There are no visible external signs of scleritis. B-scan ultrasonography should be requested whenever you have a patient with orbital pain and no obvious anterior segment explanation, particularly if fundoscopy shows choroidal folds, disc oedema, or exudative retinal detachment.

If you see annular choroidal detachment on fundoscopy in a painful eye with no history of trauma or surgery, posterior scleritis should be your first differential. Get a B-scan the same day.
2
Performing the B-scan

The patient reclines or sits with the eye closed. Apply a coupling gel to the closed lid (or use a waterbath technique with the lid open). Place the probe transversely over the upper lid and fan the beam through the posterior pole. Ask the patient to look in different gaze directions to move the optic nerve into the scan plane. Adjust the gain to identify the optic nerve shadow clearly — it appears as a dark horizontal band extending posteriorly from the posterior sclera.

3
Recognising the T-sign

In posterior scleritis, oedema fluid accumulates in Tenon's capsule. Tenon's capsule wraps around the globe and the optic nerve sheath. When fluid fills this space, it creates an echolucent (dark, low-reflective) region around the optic nerve on B-scan. The optic nerve shadow runs horizontally across the image, and the fluid collection sits perpendicular to it at the nerve head entry, forming the shape of the letter T. The vertical bar of the T is the fluid in Tenon's capsule; the horizontal bar is the optic nerve shadow. This T-sign is pathognomonic of posterior scleritis. No other orbital condition produces this specific configuration.

In high myopia, you may see a posterior staphyloma on B-scan — an outpouching at the posterior pole. This should not be confused with the T-sign. The staphyloma shows ectatic bulging of the globe wall without the characteristic fluid collection alongside the optic nerve.
4
Additional B-scan Findings in Posterior Scleritis

Beyond the T-sign, B-scan in posterior scleritis may show thickening of the posterior scleral coat (normal sclera is 1 mm thick; in posterior scleritis it can be 2 to 3 mm), subretinal fluid from exudative retinal detachment, choroidal thickening, and disc oedema. These findings together in the right clinical context — a painful eye with orbital signs — make the diagnosis clear. Response to treatment (systemic NSAIDs or steroids) is usually rapid and dramatic, both clinically and on repeat B-scan, which can confirm the diagnosis retrospectively if any doubt remained.

This is pathognomonic and confirms the diagnosis. Fluorescein angiography would show secondary retinal/choroidal changes but would not diagnose posterior scleritis specifically.

1
Classify scleritis and describe its clinical features, systemic associations, and management.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition

Scleritis is inflammation of the sclera proper, involving the deep episcleral plexus and scleral stroma. It is a serious condition with significant risk of visual morbidity and systemic disease association.

Classification (Watson and Hayreh)

Anterior scleritis (95%): Diffuse (most common, generalised hyperaemia); Nodular (immovable tender nodule); Necrotising with inflammation (avascular area, risk of perforation, associated with systemic vasculitis); Necrotising without inflammation — Scleromalacia perforans (RA, painless, yellow-grey patches). Posterior scleritis (5%): Pain, proptosis, restricted motility, choroidal folds, disc oedema, exudative RD; T-sign on B-scan.

Clinical Features

Severe boring pain waking from sleep; radiation to temple and jaw. Deep blue-violet scleral hue (deep episcleral plexus). Tenderness on palpation of the globe. Does not blanch with phenylephrine 2.5%. Visual acuity may be reduced. Anterior chamber may show flare and cells in sclerouveitis.

Systemic Associations

Present in over 50% of scleritis cases. RA (most common overall); Wegener's granulomatosis/GPA (c-ANCA, necrotising form); SLE (ANA, anti-dsDNA); PAN; Relapsing polychondritis; IBD (less common than uveitis association). Investigations: FBC, ESR, CRP, ANA, ANCA, RF, anti-CCP, VDRL, chest X-ray, urine analysis.

Management

Step 1 — Systemic NSAIDs (indomethacin 25-50 mg TDS or ibuprofen). Step 2 — Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day (NSAIDs failed or severe disease). Step 3 — Immunosuppressives (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate). Step 4 — Biologics (rituximab for GPA, TNF inhibitors for RA-associated). Periocular steroid injection is contraindicated. Treat underlying systemic disease simultaneously with rheumatology. Scleral patch graft for structural support in necrotising forms with impending perforation.

2
Differentiate episcleritis from scleritis under the headings of aetiology, clinical features, investigations, and treatment.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Aetiology

Episcleritis: 70% idiopathic; 30% systemic — usually minor (rosacea, gout, IBD, atopy). Scleritis: 50%+ systemic — often serious vasculitic or connective tissue disease (RA, Wegener's, SLE, PAN, relapsing polychondritis).

Clinical Features

Episcleritis: Superficial vessels; bright salmon-pink redness; sectoral or diffuse; mild discomfort or none; nodule moves freely if present; VA unaffected; no scleral tenderness on globe palpation; self-limiting 2-3 weeks. Scleritis: Deep episcleral plexus; blue-violet hue; severe boring pain waking from sleep; radiates to temple and jaw; fixed immovable nodule if present; globe tender to touch; VA may be reduced; complications (peripheral keratitis, cataract, glaucoma, choroidal detachment in posterior form).

Phenylephrine 2.5% Test

Episcleritis: Blanches (superficial plexus responds to vasoconstrictor). Scleritis: Does not blanch (deep plexus unresponsive).

Investigations

Single episode episcleritis: No workup needed. Bilateral/recurrent: Basic screen (FBC, ESR, uric acid, ANA). Scleritis (all): FBC, ESR, CRP, RF, ANA, ANCA, anti-CCP, VDRL, chest X-ray, urine analysis. Posterior scleritis: B-scan (T-sign).

Treatment

Episcleritis: Lubricants; topical NSAID (ketorolac 0.5%); mild topical steroid (fluorometholone) for persistent cases; systemic NSAIDs rarely needed. Scleritis: Systemic NSAIDs first; oral prednisolone if NSAIDs fail; immunosuppressives (MTX, azathioprine); biologics for systemic vasculitis. Periocular injection contraindicated in scleritis.

3
Describe posterior scleritis — its clinical presentation, diagnosis, and management.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Introduction

Posterior scleritis is inflammation of the sclera posterior to the equator. It constitutes about 5% of all scleritis cases but is frequently misdiagnosed because it presents with orbital and posterior segment signs rather than the visible anterior scleral redness of anterior scleritis.

Clinical Features

Pain: Deep, severe, periorbital — may radiate to the occiput or jaw. Proptosis: Due to posterior scleral thickening and Tenon's oedema creating mass effect. Restricted ocular movements: Secondary to posterior scleral inflammation involving the muscle origins or surrounding orbital tissues. Reduced vision: From exudative retinal detachment, disc oedema, or cystoid macular oedema. Fundoscopic findings: Choroidal folds (parallel folds in the choroid and RPE — pathognomonic of any posterior segment mass effect), annular choroidal detachment, subretinal fluid, exudative RD, disc oedema. No visible anterior scleritis is necessarily present.

Investigations

B-scan ultrasonography (investigation of choice): T-sign — echolucent fluid in Tenon's capsule perpendicular to the optic nerve shadow; posterior scleral thickening (greater than 2 mm). MRI orbit: Shows scleral thickening as hypointense ring on T2. CT orbit: Scleral thickening and adjacent fat stranding. FFA: Choroidal folds, subretinal fluid, disc oedema patterns. Systemic workup: ANA, ANCA, RF, ESR, CRP.

Differential Diagnosis

Orbital pseudotumour, orbital myositis, choroidal tumour (melanoma, metastasis), uveal effusion syndrome, sympathetic ophthalmia. Posterior scleritis is the great mimic.

Management

Dramatic and rapid response to anti-inflammatory treatment is itself a diagnostic criterion. Systemic NSAIDs (indomethacin 50 mg TDS) — many cases respond within days. Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day for severe or NSAID-resistant cases. Immunosuppressives for recurrent or steroid-dependent disease. Treat underlying systemic disease. The rapid clinical and ultrasonographic response to treatment confirms the diagnosis retrospectively if any doubt existed.

Which test is used to differentiate episcleritis from anterior scleritis at the bedside?
NEET PG
Show Answer
Answer: Phenylephrine 2.5% test (blanching test). Instillation of phenylephrine 2.5% into the affected eye causes blanching in episcleritis (superficial episcleral vessels constrict) but no blanching in scleritis (deep episcleral plexus does not respond to vasoconstrictors). Describe the T-sign in B-scan ultrasonography. In which condition is it seen?
DNB
Show Answer
Answer: Posterior scleritis. The T-sign on B-scan ultrasonography is produced by fluid accumulating in Tenon's capsule around the optic nerve. On B-scan, the optic nerve appears as a horizontal dark band (the vertical component of the T). The echolucent (dark) fluid in Tenon's capsule creates a perpendicular shadow at the posterior sclera/optic nerve head, forming the crossbar of the letter T. NEET PG 2021
Which treatment is contraindicated in scleritis and why?
NEET PG
Show Answer
Answer: Periocular (subconjunctival or sub-Tenon's) steroid injection. This is contraindicated in scleritis because the scleral stroma is already inflamed, weakened, and potentially thinned. Depot steroid directly adjacent to diseased scleral tissue causes further collagen lysis — the anti-collagenase effects of steroids paradoxically accelerate scleral melt in actively inflamed sclera when administered locally. This can lead to scleral perforation. Systemic steroids (oral prednisolone) are safe and appropriate; it is only the route of administration (periocular injection) that is contraindicated.
