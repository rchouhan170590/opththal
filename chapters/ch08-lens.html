Chapter 8

Lens & Cataract

Lens anatomy, cataract classification, clinical features, surgery, and complications — essential for UG & PG exams.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Lens Anatomy & Structure

UG
▼
Key Fact: The lens is an avascular, transparent structure with a refractive index of 1.40 (nucleus), contributing ~17–19 D of accommodation power.
  • Dimensions: Diameter 9 mm, thickness 4 mm (increases with age)
  • Capsule: Elastic, acellular basement membrane; strongest at equator
  • Epithelium: Single layer under anterior capsule; active Na-K ATPase
  • Nucleus: Refractive index 1.40; dense, hard center (increases with sclerosis)
  • Cortex: Concentric lens fibres; refractive index 1.38–1.39; softer than nucleus
  • Zonule (ligaments): Connect lens equator to ciliary body; provide mechanical suspension

Clinical Features & Presentation

UG
▼

Symptoms

  • Decreased vision: Progressive, painless; insidious onset
  • Glare: Especially PSC and cortical cataracts; problematic at night
  • Monocular diplopia: Due to lens irregularities
  • Myopic shift: Nuclear sclerosis increases refractive index → hyperopes become myopic initially
  • Asymptomatic: Many early cataracts found incidentally

On Examination

  • Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA): Reduced disproportionate to opacity (cortical) vs. proportionate (nuclear)
  • Slit-lamp findings: Opacity, colour (brown in nuclear, white in cortical), location
  • Absence of red reflex: Indicates mature cataract
  • Lens-induced glaucoma: If mature white cataract (phacomorphic; protein leakage → phacolytic)
️ Exam Trap: A patient may have good VA with dense nuclear cataract (pinhole effect) but poor VA with cortical or PSC due to glare and multiple scattering sites.

Phacoemulsification & IOL Power Calculation

UGPG
▼

Indications for Surgery

  • BCVA <6/12 (or <20/40) due to cataract
  • Symptoms affecting daily life (glare, difficulty driving)
  • Professional requirement (pilot, driver)
  • Lens-induced glaucoma or phacomorphic glaucoma
  • Congenital cataract (within 6–8 weeks for visual development)

Phacoemulsification Steps

Key Sequence: Capsulorhexis → Hydrodissection → Nuclear Divide-and-Conquer/Chop → Cortical Aspiration → Implantation
  1. Capsulorhexis: Continuous curvilinear opening in anterior capsule; ensures support for IOL
  2. Hydrodissection: Inject saline to separate epinucleus from cortex; facilitates nuclear rotation
  3. Nuclear management:
    • Divide-and-Conquer: Nucleus split into 4 pie-shaped segments; each phacoed separately
    • Phaco Chop: Alternative; chop nucleus with minimal manipulation
  4. Cortical aspiration: Remove remaining soft cortical material with irrigation-aspiration probe
  5. Posterior capsule polishing: Remove residual epithelial cells (reduce PCO risk)
  6. IOL implantation: In-the-bag placement within capsular bag

IOL Power Calculation

  • SRK Formula: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K (basic, older)
  • Modern formulas: Barrett, Haigis, Holladay, Olsen (ray-tracing); account for anterior chamber depth, lens position
  • Biometry: Axial length (optical or ultrasound), keratometry, anterior chamber depth
  • A-constant: Lens-specific; ranges 117–120 depending on IOL model & insertion technology
  • Target refraction: Usually emmetropia or adjusted for monovision (plano dominant, −2.5 to −3.0 D non-dominant)

Surgical Complications

PG
▼

Intraoperative

  • Posterior capsule rupture: Most common; Nd:YAG capsulotomy often needed later
  • Dropped nucleus: Requires pars plana vitrectomy; poor visual outcome if not promptly managed
  • Suprachoroidal haemorrhage: Rare; expulsive (expulsion of intraocular contents); associated with risk factors (glaucoma, high myopia)
  • Corneal decompensation: Endothelial cell loss; bullous keratopathy in low endothelial reserve

Postoperative

  • Posterior capsule opacification (PCO): Most common; proliferation of lens epithelial cells; managed by Nd:YAG capsulotomy
  • Refractive error: Residual sphere/astigmatism; managed with glasses or LASIK
  • Striate keratopathy: Corneal striae from edema; usually transient
  • Cystoid macular oedema: Prostaglandin release; NSAIDs prophylactically given
  • IOL malposition: Decentration, tilt; if symptomatic, requires repositioning or explantation

Congenital Cataract — Special Management

PG
▼
Critical Window: Surgery must be done within 6–8 weeks of life to prevent irreversible amblyopia (dense cataract blocks visual input during critical period).

Key Principles

  • Early diagnosis: White pupil (leukocoria) must trigger immediate referral
  • Timing: Unilateral: surgery at 6–8 weeks; Bilateral: operate on denser eye first, 1–2 weeks interval
  • Surgical approach: Smaller incision, open vitrectomy often performed simultaneously (prevents retained lens material, secondary glaucoma)
  • Optical rehabilitation: IOL, contact lens, or glasses (depending on age, refraction); mandatory immediately post-op
  • Amblyopia risk: Requires aggressive patching, glasses/contact lens wear, regular follow-up

Common Causes

  • Intrauterine infection (rubella) → "cataract + cardiac defect + deafness" (rubella triad)
  • Genetic (familial lamellar cataracts)
  • Metabolic (galactosaemia, Lowe syndrome)
  • Idiopathic (most common)

MCQ Bank — Lens & Cataract

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
Lens diameter and thickness in adults are, respectively:
A7 mm, 3 mm
B9 mm, 4 mm
C12 mm, 6 mm
D10 mm, 5 mm
Correct Answer: B — 9 mm, 4 mm
Adult lens: diameter 9 mm, thickness 4 mm at rest. Thickness increases with age as new lens fibres are laid down throughout life. Refractive index of nucleus is 1.40.
Q2 UG
Which type of age-related cataract is most symptomatic for reading and glare?
ANuclear sclerotic
BCortical
CPosterior subcapsular (PSC)
DAnterior polar
Correct Answer: C — Posterior subcapsular (PSC)
PSC causes rapid vision loss for near (reading) and glare at night driving. Located on posterior surface directly in visual axis. Nuclear: slow, progressive; Cortical: spokes, moderate glare.
Q3 PG
A 70-year-old man with BCVA 6/18 OD due to suspected cataract comes for evaluation. He has good vision in OS (6/6). What is the most appropriate management?
AObserve; refer for surgery only if VA drops to <6/24
BDiscuss surgical options; surgery if patient is symptomatic and willing
CImmediate surgery is mandatory
DPrescribe glasses and review in 6 months
Correct Answer: B — Discuss surgical options; surgery if patient is symptomatic and willing
Surgery is indicated if BCVA is <6/12 AND patient is symptomatic AND functionally limited. Patient autonomy and symptoms are key. VA of 6/18 meets threshold, but patient preference and quality of life matter.
Q4 PG
The first step in phacoemulsification is:
AHydrodissection of the nucleus
BCortical aspiration
CContinuous curvilinear capsulorhexis
DIOL implantation
Correct Answer: C — Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis
Sequence: Capsulorhexis → Hydrodissection → Nuclear fragmentation → Cortical aspiration → IOL. Capsulorhexis creates the opening and ensures adequate capsular support for the IOL.
Q5 PG
Most common complication of cataract surgery is:
APosterior capsule opacification (PCO)
BSuprachoroidal haemorrhage
CDropped nucleus
DCorneal decompensation
Correct Answer: A — Posterior capsule opacification (PCO)
PCO occurs in ~20–50% of cases (higher in younger patients) due to proliferation of lens epithelial cells. Easily managed with Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy. Suprachoroidal haemorrhage and dropped nucleus are rare but serious.
Q6 UG
Rosette cataract is pathognomonic for:
ACongenital rubella
BRadiation exposure
CBlunt ocular trauma
DLong-term steroid use
Correct Answer: C — Blunt ocular trauma
Rosette (star-shaped) cataract is a characteristic finding after blunt trauma. Represents disruption of lens fibres in a radial pattern. Traumatic cataract may be immediate or delayed.
Q7 PG
SRK formula for IOL power calculation is: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K. What does 'A' represent?
AAnterior chamber depth
BA-constant (IOL-specific)
CAxial length
DAccommodation amplitude
Correct Answer: B — A-constant (IOL-specific)
SRK: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K. A = A-constant (117–120 range depending on IOL); L = axial length; K = keratometry. Modern formulas use ray-tracing and consider more biometric variables.
Q8 UG
Steroid-induced cataract typically manifests as:
ANuclear sclerotic cataract
BAnterior polar cataract
CCortical cataract with spokes
DPosterior subcapsular cataract
Correct Answer: D — Posterior subcapsular cataract
Long-term systemic or topical corticosteroid use causes PSC. Chlorpromazine causes anterior stellate cataract. Risk factors: duration >1 year, higher doses. Screening recommended.
Q9 PG
Critical timing for surgery in unilateral dense congenital cataract to prevent amblyopia is:
AWithin 4 weeks of life
BWithin 6–8 weeks of life
CWithin 3–4 months of life
DWithin 6 months of life
Correct Answer: B — Within 6–8 weeks of life
Critical window for congenital cataract surgery: 6–8 weeks to allow normal visual development. Bilateral: operate on denser eye first. Optical rehabilitation (IOL, contact lens, or glasses) is mandatory immediately post-op.
Q10 PG
A 5-year-old child presents with unilateral leukocoria and dense congenital cataract OS. What is the most critical aspect of management after early surgery?
AImmediate optical rehabilitation with appropriate glasses/contact lens/IOL
BObservation for 3–4 months post-op
CRoutine follow-up at 6 months
DCheck for secondary glaucoma
Correct Answer: A — Immediate optical rehabilitation with appropriate glasses/contact lens/IOL
Post-operative optical rehabilitation is mandatory immediately after cataract surgery in children. Delay in providing glasses/contact lens/IOL will result in irreversible amblyopia. Close monitoring for refractive error and compliance is essential.

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Classify cataracts. Describe the clinical features and indications for surgery. (8 marks)
Classification: Congenital (lamellar, nuclear, polar, total); Age-related (nuclear, cortical, PSC); Secondary; Complicated; Traumatic; Drug-induced. Clinical features: vision loss, glare, monocular diplopia, myopic shift. Indications: BCVA <6/12, symptoms, functional limitation.

Enumerate the steps of phacoemulsification and the most common complication. (6 marks)
Steps: Capsulorhexis → Hydrodissection → Nuclear fragmentation (Divide-and-Conquer/Chop) → Cortical aspiration → IOL implantation. Most common complication: PCO (posterior capsule opacification); managed by Nd:YAG capsulotomy.

Must-Know Dimensions

  • Lens diameter: 9 mm
  • Lens thickness: 4 mm (at rest)
  • Nucleus refractive index: 1.40
  • Cortex RI: 1.38–1.39
  • Accommodation power: ~17–19 D

Cataract Classification Mnemonics

  • Congenital: Lamellar (most common), Nuclear, Polar (ant/post), Total
  • Age-related: Nuclear (brown, myopic shift), Cortical (white spokes), PSC (worst for reading)
  • Drugs: Steroids → PSC; Chlorpromazine → ant. stellate
  • Trauma: Rosette (pathognomonic)

Surgical Indications

  • BCVA <6/12 due to cataract
  • Symptoms affecting daily life
  • Professional requirement
  • Phacomorphic/lens-induced glaucoma
  • Congenital: within 6–8 weeks

Phaco & IOL

  • Key sequence: Rhexis → Hydrodissection → Nuclear fragmentation → Cortical aspiration → IOL
  • SRK formula: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K
  • PCO management: Nd:YAG capsulotomy
  • Congenital post-op: Immediate optical rehabilitation (glasses/contact lens/IOL)
