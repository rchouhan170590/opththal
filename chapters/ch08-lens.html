Lens & Cataract

Lens anatomy and metabolism, cataract classification and grading, phacoemulsification and SICS technique, IOL power calculation, surgical complications, and lens dislocation — covered at PG examination depth.

UG PG High-Yield

High-yield lens and cataract facts condensed into revision cards. Each card targets one testable concept — anatomy, classification, surgery, or complications that MCQs repeatedly return to.

Lens Anatomy

The lens is a biconvex, avascular, transparent structure suspended by zonules of Zinn from the ciliary body. It weighs about 65 mg at birth and 200 to 250 mg in adults, growing throughout life by adding new fibre cells.

  • Capsule — thickest basement membrane in the body; thickest at pre-equatorial anterior zone (14 to 15 microns); thinnest at posterior pole (2 to 4 microns) — most vulnerable during surgery
  • Epithelium — single layer beneath the anterior capsule only; no posterior epithelium; divides mitotically at the equatorial bow region
  • Cortex — new lens fibres from equatorial epithelial cells; soft and hydrated; cortical cataract forms here
  • Nucleus — five zones from inside out: embryonal, fetal, infantile, adult, cortex; hardens and yellows with age
  • Power — approximately 15 to 20 D at rest; accommodation amplitude 10 to 12 D in youth; presbyopia symptomatic by age 40 to 45
High-Yield

Cataract Classification

The morphological classification determines symptoms, progression rate, and surgical approach.

  • Nuclear — central hardening and yellowing; worse distance vision; index myopia (patient reads without glasses temporarily); LOCS III grades N1 to N6
  • Cortical — spoke-like opacities periphery to centre; water clefts and vacuoles; vision affected when spokes reach visual axis
  • Posterior subcapsular (PSC) — most visually significant; small central PSC causes disproportionate blur in bright light and near work; steroids, diabetes, myotonic dystrophy, radiation
  • Anterior subcapsular — beneath anterior capsule; blunt trauma causes rosette pattern
  • Posterior polar — congenital; associated with posterior capsule weakness — warn the surgeon

Cataract Causes by Type

  • Senile — oxidative stress; nuclear most common subtype
  • Diabetic — sorbitol accumulation (osmotic); bilateral snowflake (true diabetic) in young; accelerated nuclear in older; hyperglycaemia causes myopic shift
  • Steroid-induced — PSC type; topical higher risk than systemic
  • Traumatic — blunt: rosette ASC; perforation: total; electric/lightning: bilateral ASC; infrared/glassblower: anterior capsule exfoliation
  • Complicated — uveitis, high myopia, RP, angle-closure (glaukomflecken — ASC from ischaemia)
  • Congenital — TORCH, galactosaemia (oil-drop nuclear), rubella (pearlescent nuclear), Lowe, Down syndrome
  • Myotonic dystrophy — Christmas tree (iridescent) cataract; pathognomonic

IOL Power Calculation Formulae

IOL power is calculated from axial length and keratometry. Formula choice depends on axial length.

  • SRK-T — most used for normal eyes (axial length 22 to 26 mm); third-generation theoretical formula
  • Hoffer Q — short eyes (axial length below 22 mm)
  • Holladay I — long eyes (above 26 mm)
  • Barrett Universal II — most accurate across all axial lengths; fourth-generation; preferred when available
  • SRK II — older regression formula (P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K); still tested in MCQs

Optical biometry (IOLMaster) is most accurate. Immersion A-scan > contact A-scan. Contact A-scan (corneal indentation) is the most common source of error — shortens measured axial length → hyperopic surprise.

Lens Dislocation — Causes and Directions

Ectopia lentis means the lens is displaced from its normal position. Subluxation = partial; dislocation = complete (into vitreous or AC).

  • Marfan syndrome — superotemporal; fibrillin-1 gene (AD); tall, arachnodactyly, aortic aneurysm; zonules weak but present; subluxation more common
  • Homocystinuria — inferonasal; cystathionine-β-synthase deficiency (AR); thromboembolism risk; intellectually disabled; complete dislocation more common; avoid N2O anaesthesia
  • Weill-Marchesani — inferior; short stature, brachydactyly; microspherophakia; pilocarpine preferred over cycloplegics for pupillary block
  • Trauma — most common cause overall; any direction

Mnemonic: Mar = Up (superotemporal), Homo = Down (inferonasal), Weill = Down (inferior).

High-Yield

Key Mnemonics and Exam Facts

PSC = "3 Ss": Steroids, Sunlight (UV), Sugar (diabetes) — the three most important causes of PSC.

Glaukomflecken: Permanent grey-white ASC opacities after an acute angle-closure glaucoma attack — ischaemic necrosis of lens epithelium. Use them as a forensic sign of a past attack.

Morgagnian cataract: Hypermature cataract; cortex liquefies completely; nucleus sinks (visible as yellow crescent); leaking protein → phacolytic glaucoma.

Posterior polar cataract: Posterior capsule is inherently weak or absent — warn the surgeon before surgery to avoid aggressive hydrodissection which can rupture the PC.

TASS vs Endophthalmitis: TASS within 12 to 24 hours, no vitritis, diffuse corneal oedema — treat with topical steroids only. Endophthalmitis day 2 to 5, vitritis, severe pain — intravitreal antibiotics.

Surgical Options — Which Technique for Which Situation

1
Phacoemulsification — Gold standard. Ultrasonic fragmentation through 2.2 to 2.8 mm self-sealing incision. No sutures, rapid recovery (1 to 2 days), minimal astigmatism. Preferred for nuclear grades 1 to 4.
2
SICS (Small Incision Cataract Surgery) — 6 to 7 mm self-sealing scleral tunnel; nucleus delivered whole; ideal for hard nuclei (grade 4 to 5) and high-volume settings. Cost-effective; widely used in India. Comparable visual outcomes to phaco.
3
ECCE (Extracapsular Cataract Extraction) — 10 to 12 mm limbal incision; nucleus expressed manually; requires sutures; indicated for very hard or subluxated lenses where phaco is unsafe.
4
ICCE (Intracapsular Cataract Extraction) — Entire lens removed with capsule; no capsular bag for IOL support; now obsolete for primary surgery; still relevant for subluxated lenses with absent capsular support. AC IOL or aphakic correction required.
5
PCR Management Algorithm: Stop phaco → fill AC with viscoelastic → remove phaco tip slowly → anterior vitrectomy if vitreous prolapse → convert to ECCE if nucleus remains → IOL in sulcus (three-piece) or optic capture if CCC intact → ACIOL/scleral-fixated IOL if no capsular support.

Lens Anatomy, Metabolism and Transparency

UGPG
▼

Structure

The crystalline lens is a biconvex, transparent, avascular, and aneural structure suspended within the hyaloid fossa by approximately 70 thin zonular fibres (zonules of Zinn) that attach to the pars plana of the ciliary body. The anterior surface has a radius of curvature of about 10 mm and the posterior surface 6 mm — more curved posteriorly. In total, the lens contributes approximately 15 to 20 dioptres of refractive power in the unaccommodated eye, roughly one-third of the eye's total power (the cornea providing the rest).

The lens capsule is the thickest basement membrane in the human body, produced continuously by the underlying epithelium. Composed largely of type IV collagen, laminin, and heparan sulfate proteoglycan, its thickness varies considerably: thickest at the pre-equatorial anterior zone (approximately 14 to 15 microns) and thinnest at the central posterior pole (approximately 2 to 4 microns). This posterior thinness is the critical surgical vulnerability — the posterior capsule is the weakest point in the entire lens complex and is at risk during any posterior cortex manipulation.

The lens epithelium forms a single layer of cuboidal cells situated exclusively beneath the anterior capsule — there is no epithelium on the posterior surface. The germinative zone is at the pre-equatorial region, where cells undergo mitosis throughout life. Daughter cells differentiate into lens fibres, elongate, lose their organelles including the nucleus, and fill with crystallin proteins. This continuous fibre addition is why the lens grows throughout life and why the central nuclear fibres, formed in embryonic life, are the oldest.

Nuclear Zones

The nucleus is stratified concentrically, reflecting successive periods of growth. From the inside outward: the embryonal nucleus (first two months of intrauterine life, primary lens fibres), the fetal nucleus (third month to birth; Y-sutures visible — upright anteriorly, inverted posteriorly), the infantile nucleus (postnatal to puberty), the adult nucleus, and finally the cortex (most recently formed, outermost fibres). Age-related nuclear changes begin in the embryonal and fetal zones — progressive hardening, yellowing, and eventually browning as crystallin cross-linking accumulates.

Metabolism and Transparency

The lens has no blood supply and relies entirely on diffusion from the aqueous humour. Glucose is the primary metabolite, entering glycolysis (Embden-Meyerhof pathway) to produce ATP. The inner nuclear fibres, lacking mitochondria, depend solely on anaerobic glycolysis. The hexose monophosphate shunt generates NADPH for glutathione reduction — the critical antioxidant mechanism that protects lens crystallins from oxidative damage.

The sorbitol pathway becomes significant in hyperglycaemia: aldose reductase converts excess glucose to sorbitol, which accumulates because the lens has very little sorbitol dehydrogenase. The osmotic gradient draws water into lens fibres, causing swelling, vacuole formation, and the early changes of cortical diabetic cataract.

Lens transparency depends on: (1) the regular arrangement of lens fibre cells, (2) the high concentration of soluble crystallin proteins packed in a short-range ordered arrangement that minimises light scatter, and (3) active maintenance of relative dehydration by the Na+/K+-ATPase pump-leak system. Any disruption of crystallin structure — by oxidation, glycation, or denaturation — causes aggregation, light scattering, and opacity.

Accommodation

When the ciliary muscle contracts, the circular fibres shorten the ciliary ring, releasing tension on the zonules. The elastic capsule causes the lens to become more spherical, increasing curvature and power — this is accommodation. Presbyopia develops as the nucleus hardens and loses elasticity, making the lens unable to change shape adequately. Symptoms appear around age 40 to 45 when the amplitude of accommodation falls below 4 dioptres.

Index myopia is a clinically useful sign. In nuclear cataract, the increasing refractive index of the sclerosing nucleus causes a myopic shift. Presbyopic patients may find they can suddenly read without glasses again — the so-called "second sight." This is temporary and followed by progressive opacification. Asking about this in the history helps date the onset of nuclear sclerosis.

Cataract — Classification, Stages and Special Types

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

What Is Cataract?

Cataract is any opacity in the crystalline lens or its capsule. It is the leading cause of reversible blindness worldwide, accounting for approximately 50% of global blindness and 60 to 65% of blindness in India. The condition is overwhelmingly senile in aetiology, though a wide range of other causes are disproportionately represented in examinations.

Nuclear Cataract

The nucleus gradually scleroses and opacifies. It yellows first (nuclear sclerosis), then browns (brunescent cataract), and in extreme cases becomes black (cataracta nigra). Because nuclear density increases, the refractive index rises and patients develop index myopia with temporarily improved near vision. Distance vision deteriorates more than near vision. The LOCS III system grades nuclear opacity from N1 (trace) through N6 (black).

Cortical Cataract

Wedge-shaped or spoke-like opacities develop from the equatorial periphery toward the visual axis. Water clefts and vacuoles form as electrolyte imbalance disrupts fibre arrangement. Vision is relatively preserved until spokes reach the central axis. On retroillumination, the spoke pattern is striking. This is the most common type in diabetic patients.

Posterior Subcapsular Cataract

A granular or plaque-like opacity at the central posterior capsule. Because it sits at the nodal point of the optical system, even a small PSC causes disproportionate blur — particularly in bright conditions (the pupil constricts, making the opacity occupy a larger fraction of the aperture) and during near work. On retroillumination, it appears as a dark central plaque. Associated with steroid use, diabetes, atopic dermatitis, myotonic dystrophy, and radiation.

Stages of Senile Cataract

The classic four stages describe natural cortical cataract progression. Incipient: peripheral spoke opacities, vision unaffected. Immature: opacities extending toward centre, vision variably reduced, iris shadow present (the iris casts a shadow on the clear anterior zone). Mature: total opacification, white lens, iris shadow absent, vision reduced to PL+PR. Hypermature: cortex liquefies; if the nucleus sinks completely, this is a Morgagnian cataract with a visible yellow crescent at the bottom; leaking cortical protein causes phacolytic glaucoma.

Iris shadow test: In immature cataract, some clear anterior cortex allows oblique light to cast a shadow of the iris on the lens. In mature cataract, total opacification eliminates this shadow. This simple test distinguishes the two stages at the bedside.

Special Cataracts

Congenital cataract: The critical concern is deprivation amblyopia. Dense central or total congenital cataracts require prompt visual axis clearance — within 6 to 8 weeks for bilateral cases, within 4 to 6 weeks for unilateral. Lamellar (zonular) cataract is the most common type — a discrete shell of opacity surrounding a clear embryonal nucleus, with clear cortex outside. Rubella cataract is nuclear and pearlescent. Galactosaemia produces an "oil-drop" nuclear cataract on retroillumination — a central discoid green reflex.

Traumatic cataract: Blunt injury causes a rosette-shaped anterior subcapsular opacity at the point of maximum concussive force — typically stable and may not progress. Perforating injury causes rapid total opacification as aqueous enters the lens. Glassblower's (infrared) cataract causes exfoliation of the anterior capsule — the outermost layer peels and curls at its edge. This is distinct from pseudoexfoliation syndrome.

Myotonic dystrophy: The Christmas tree or iridescent cataract — multicoloured needle-shaped crystals in the posterior cortex — is pathognomonic. PSC also develops early. These patients have myotonia, frontal balding, ptosis, and cardiac conduction defects.

Cataract Surgery — Technique, IOL, and Complications

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Phacoemulsification — Steps

Phacoemulsification is performed under topical anaesthesia (proparacaine or oxybuprocaine) or sub-Tenon's block in the majority of modern cases. A 2.2 to 2.8 mm clear corneal incision is made at the steepest corneal meridian to simultaneously correct minor astigmatism. Viscoelastic (OVD) is injected to maintain anterior chamber depth and protect the endothelium.

The continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC) creates a circular anterior capsular opening of 5 to 5.5 mm. This is one of the most technically critical steps: a capsulorhexis too small risks radial tears during nucleus manipulation; one too large fails to provide the IOL-capsule overlap needed to minimise PCO.

Hydrodissection injects BSS beneath the capsule to free the cortex. Hydrodelineation separates epinucleus from nucleus. These allow free nuclear rotation. The nucleus is emulsified using "divide and conquer" or "stop and chop" technique, with each quadrant aspirated at high vacuum. Residual cortex is removed by irrigation-aspiration (I/A). A foldable IOL is injected into the capsular bag through the small incision.

Intraocular Lenses

Modern foldable IOLs are made of hydrophobic acrylic (AcrySof — lowest PCO rate due to "sticky" IOL-capsule interface), hydrophilic acrylic (higher PCO rate), or silicone (avoid in patients likely to need silicone oil — silicone oil deposits on and within the IOL, causing permanent haziness). PMMA is rigid and requires a larger incision. Toric IOLs correct astigmatism; multifocal and EDOF IOLs address presbyopia.

Posterior Capsule Opacification (PCO)

PCO is the most common complication of cataract surgery, occurring in 20 to 40% of patients within 5 years. Residual lens epithelial cells proliferate across the posterior capsule (Elschnig's pearls, Soemmerring's ring). The patient has gradual visual deterioration after initially successful surgery. Treatment is Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy — a 3 to 4 mm central opening using photodisruption. Complications: transient IOP spike (give prophylactic apraclonidine or acetazolamide), IOL pitting, cystoid macular oedema, rarely retinal detachment.

Posterior capsule rupture (PCR) is the most feared intraoperative complication, occurring in 1 to 3% of phaco cases. Signs: sudden deepening of AC, uncontrolled nuclear rotation, vitreous strands in AC, loss of red reflex. Management: stop phaco immediately, fill AC with viscoelastic, remove phaco tip slowly, perform anterior vitrectomy if vitreous prolapse, convert to ECCE for remaining nucleus, place three-piece IOL in sulcus or optic capture if CCC intact. A dropped nucleus requires vitreoretinal surgical intervention.

Other Complications

Endophthalmitis (0.03 to 0.05%) presents day 2 to 5 post-op with pain, loss of vision, hypopyon, and vitritis. Most common organism: Staphylococcus epidermidis. EVS: PPV + intravitreal antibiotics for LP-only vision; tap + intravitreal antibiotics for HM or better. Prophylaxis: intracameral cefuroxime at end of surgery reduces risk approximately 5-fold.

Cystoid macular oedema (Irvine-Gass syndrome) occurs 4 to 12 weeks post-op in 1 to 2% of uncomplicated cases (more after complicated surgery). OCT shows petal-shaped cysts. Treatment: topical NSAID + steroid; intravitreal triamcinolone or anti-VEGF for refractory cases.

TASS (Toxic Anterior Segment Syndrome) presents within 12 to 24 hours (earlier than infective endophthalmitis). Caused by toxic substance entering the AC — contaminated irrigating solution, denatured viscoelastic, sterilisation residues. Diffuse corneal oedema and AC reaction without vitritis. Treatment: intensive topical steroids only. No antibiotics needed.

Lens Dislocation and Ectopia Lentis

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Types

Subluxation means the lens is partially displaced but remains in the pupillary plane — the equatorial edge crosses the pupil, causing monocular diplopia and irregular astigmatism. Complete dislocation into the vitreous cavity causes aphakic optics and requires vitreoretinal intervention. Anterior dislocation into the AC causes corneal touch and pupillary block glaucoma — an ophthalmic emergency.

Systemic Causes

Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant fibrillinopathy (FBN1 gene, chromosome 15). The zonular fibres stretch rather than rupture, so the lens typically subluxates superotemporally. These patients are tall with arachnodactyly, pectus deformities, joint hypermobility, and most critically ascending aortic dilatation. Echocardiography is mandatory before any surgery. Beta-blockers or losartan are prescribed to slow aortic dilatation.

Homocystinuria is autosomal recessive (cystathionine-β-synthase deficiency). Zonular fibres rupture, so complete dislocation is more common than subluxation, and the direction is inferonasal. These patients are intellectually disabled with a marfanoid habitus. The major surgical risk is thromboembolic — homocysteine damages vascular endothelium and promotes coagulation. Nitrous oxide is absolutely contraindicated because it inactivates vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase, further elevating homocysteine. Pre-operative hydration and anticoagulation planning are essential. Pyridoxine (B6) reduces homocysteine in responsive patients.

Weill-Marchesani syndrome is the phenotypic opposite of Marfan: short stocky build, brachydactyly, and microspherophakia (a small spherical lens). The lens dislocates inferiorly and tends to move forward, causing pupillary block glaucoma. Crucially, pilocarpine is used instead of cycloplegics for this pupillary block — pilocarpine contracts the ciliary muscle, tightens the zonules, and pulls the lens posteriorly. Cycloplegics would loosen the zonules and allow the spherical lens to move forward, worsening the block.

Phacomorphic vs Phacolytic glaucoma: Phacomorphic = intumescent lens (immature stage) causes angle closure (shallow AC); treat urgently with lens extraction after IOP control. Phacolytic = hypermature lens leaks protein through capsule; macrophages block the trabecular meshwork (deep AC, open angle); also treat with lens extraction. Both are lens-induced (phacogenic) emergencies.

Surgical Management

For mild subluxation (<90° of zonular loss): Capsular tension ring (CTR) inserted into capsular bag to redistribute stress and allow safe phacoemulsification. For 90 to 180° loss: Modified Cionni CTR with scleral fixation hooks. For greater than 180° loss or complete vitreous dislocation: Pars plana vitrectomy + lensectomy. For anterior chamber dislocation: Urgent lens removal to prevent endothelial decompensation.

Biometry and IOL Power Calculation

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Parameters Required

Three measurements drive IOL power calculation: axial length (AL), keratometry (K values — anterior corneal curvature), and estimated effective lens position (ELP — where the IOL sits). Axial length is the most important source of error: a 1 mm error translates to approximately 2.5 to 3 dioptres of postoperative refractive error.

Methods of Axial Length Measurement

Contact A-scan: Probe placed directly on the anaesthetised cornea; corneal indentation artificially shortens the axial length; the most common source of biometric error, causing a hyperopic surprise (IOL underpowered). Immersion A-scan: Probe in a fluid-filled shell; no corneal contact; significantly more accurate. Optical biometry (IOLMaster, Lenstar): Low-coherence interferometry; 10-fold greater precision than A-scan; non-contact; gold standard. Cannot penetrate dense cataracts with no red reflex — in those cases, immersion A-scan is the fallback.

Formula Generations

First generation (Binkhorst, Colenbrander): theoretical vergence calculations; poor ELP estimation; large errors. Second generation (SRK I, SRK II): regression-based; SRK formula = P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K; simple but inaccurate for outliers. Third generation (SRK-T for normal AL; Hoffer Q for short eyes below 22 mm; Holladay I for long eyes above 26 mm): improved ELP prediction using postoperative outcome regression; widely used. Fourth generation and newer (Barrett Universal II, Holladay II, Hill-RBF): additional biometric inputs, machine learning; Barrett Universal II is currently the most accurate across all axial lengths.

Post-refractive surgery biometry is a recognised challenge. LASIK flattens the anterior corneal curvature but standard keratometry assumes a fixed relationship between anterior and posterior corneal radii that is violated after ablation. The result is overestimation of effective corneal power, which leads to a hyperopic surprise. Specialised formulae (Haigis-L, Barrett True-K, or the clinical history method using pre-LASIK K values) are required. This is a significant medicolegal issue and a frequent PG exam question.

Two skills every ophthalmologist must master — grading a cataract systematically at the slit lamp, and knowing exactly what to do when the posterior capsule ruptures intraoperatively.

Cataract Grading at the Slit Lamp Examination Technique
1
Preparation and Pupil Dilation

Dilate the pupil adequately before grading — tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5% twenty to thirty minutes before. An undilated pupil makes it impossible to assess the peripheral lens or posterior zone adequately. Note the pupil dilation response: poor dilation suggests posterior synechiae from past uveitis or prior angle-closure attack, and indicates intraoperative difficulty in achieving a good surgical view. Set the slit lamp to medium magnification (10 to 16x).

2
Grading Nuclear Sclerosis

Use a narrow to medium slit beam at 45 degrees through the anterior segment. Compare the nuclear colour and density to the surrounding cortex. Grade 1 (nuclear sclerosis, NS1): faint yellow tinge, nucleus barely distinguishable from cortex. NS2: mild amber tinge, posterior lens details still visible. NS3: clear brown discolouration. NS4: deep brown (brunescent). NS5 to NS6: dark brown or black (cataracta nigra). A practical intraoperative guide: if you cannot see the posterior capsule through the nucleus on slit beam, you are dealing with a NS3 or harder nucleus — adjust your surgical plan accordingly.

Always document the red reflex on direct ophthalmoscopy. A dim or absent red reflex indicates significant lens opacity and is the clearest indication of when surgery is clinically justified regardless of Snellen acuity.
3
Retroillumination for Cortical and PSC Opacities

Defocus the slit beam or use co-axial illumination from the oculars. The lens should appear uniformly red from the choroidal reflex. Cortical opacities show as dark spoke-like areas against the red background. A PSC appears as a dark granular plaque at the posterior pole. Estimate the fraction of the pupillary aperture occupied by the PSC — even a 2 mm central PSC causes significant visual symptoms. Record the findings on a standard diagram marking the anterior and posterior lens zones.

For posterior polar cataract: look carefully for any wrinkling or whitish appearance of the posterior capsule in that zone. If the posterior capsule appears abnormal, note it clearly in the surgical record. The operating surgeon must be informed before the procedure to avoid aggressive hydrodissection, which can rupture a deficient posterior capsule.
4
Iris Shadow Test

With a pen torch held from the temporal side and the patient looking straight ahead, shine oblique light across the eye. In an immature cataract, clear anterior cortex is present — the iris casts a crescent-shaped shadow on the lens visible from the nasal side (positive shadow test). In a mature cataract, total opacification eliminates the clear cortex zone and no shadow forms (negative shadow test). This is the simplest bedside method to stage cataract maturity before formal slit lamp examination.

Managing Posterior Capsule Rupture Intraoperatively Surgical Decision-Making
1
Recognising PCR

Warning signs of PCR during phacoemulsification: sudden deepening of the anterior chamber (posterior support is lost), uncontrolled rapid rotation of the nuclear fragment, the phaco tip feels reduced resistance, vitreous strands appear in the anterior chamber (look like fibrous material streaming toward the wound), and loss of the red reflex through the pupil. Early subtle signs include widening of the capsulorhexis edge and anterior bulge of the posterior capsule. Stop phaco at the first sign.

2
Immediate Steps

Do not remove the phaco handpiece abruptly — this drops the AC pressure and worsens vitreous prolapse. Inject viscoelastic through the sideport to fill and stabilise the anterior chamber and push vitreous posteriorly. Then remove the phaco tip slowly with the AC maintained. If nuclear material remains, decide: can it be safely emulsified from the AC under viscoelastic protection, or should you convert to ECCE? For a large nucleus or a bulging vitreous face, conversion to ECCE with a 10 mm limbal incision and manual expression is safer.

Intracameral triamcinolone acetonide (preservative-free) injected into the AC stains vitreous white, making its extent and distribution immediately visible and allowing precise targeted anterior vitrectomy.
3
Anterior Vitrectomy

If vitreous has prolapsed into the AC or is incarcerated in the wound, anterior vitrectomy is essential before closing. Set the vitrector to low suction and high cut rate (at least 600 cuts per minute) to avoid traction on the peripheral vitreous and retina. Clear all vitreous from the wound and the pupillary plane. After vitrectomy, check the wound carefully — any strand remaining at the wound predisposes to chronic cystoid macular oedema and late retinal detachment.

4
IOL Placement Decision

IOL placement after PCR depends on remaining capsular support. If the peripheral capsular bag is intact and the CCC is intact, a three-piece IOL can be placed with haptics in the sulcus and optic captured posteriorly through the CCC (optic capture — this prevents IOL drop and reduces PCO). If bag is intact but CCC is torn, the IOL may still be placed in the bag with care. If there is no capsular support: anterior chamber IOL (ACIOL), iris-claw IOL, or scleral-fixated IOL are options. Make the decision calmly, systematically, and document everything. Always inform the patient of what occurred.

1
Classify cataract and describe the clinical features, stages, and complications of senile cataract.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition

Cataract is any opacity in the crystalline lens or its capsule. It is the leading cause of reversible blindness worldwide (50% globally; 60 to 65% in India).

Classification

By aetiology: Senile (most common), congenital, traumatic, complicated (secondary to ocular disease), metabolic (diabetic, galactosaemic, hypocalcaemic), toxic (steroid, chlorpromazine, amiodarone), radiation. By morphology: Nuclear, cortical, PSC, anterior subcapsular, polar, lamellar (zonular), total. By maturity: Incipient, immature, mature, hypermature (Morgagnian).

Clinical Features of Senile Cataract

Symptoms: Gradual painless blurring (nuclear — worse distance; PSC — worse bright light and near). Monocular diplopia. Glare and haloes. Index myopia (nuclear — second sight). Colour desaturation (advanced nuclear). Signs: Reduced Snellen acuity. Slit lamp: nuclear yellowing/browning, spoke-shaped cortical opacities, PSC plaque on retroillumination. Reduced or absent red reflex.

Stages (Cortical Senile)

Incipient: Peripheral spokes, VA normal, iris shadow positive. Immature: Spokes toward centre, VA reduced, iris shadow positive (clear cortex remains). Mature: Total opacification, VA = PL+PR, white lens, iris shadow absent, no red reflex. Hypermature: Cortex liquefied (phacolytic risk); Morgagnian = nucleus sunk, yellow crescent inferiorly.

Complications

Phacomorphic glaucoma (intumescent lens, angle closure, shallow AC). Phacolytic glaucoma (hypermature, leaking protein, open-angle). Lens-induced uveitis (phacoanaphylactic — capsule rupture, lens protein as antigen). Subluxation/dislocation. Visual deprivation amblyopia (congenital dense unilateral).

2
Describe phacoemulsification — steps, advantages, and complications.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Introduction

Phacoemulsification is the gold standard cataract surgical technique, using ultrasonic energy to emulsify the nucleus through a small self-sealing incision. Introduced by Kelman in 1967.

Steps

1. Anaesthesia: Topical or sub-Tenon's. 2. Clear corneal incision: 2.2 to 2.8 mm self-sealing, at steepest meridian. 3. OVD injection: Maintains AC, protects endothelium. 4. Capsulorhexis (CCC): 5 to 5.5 mm continuous circular opening. Critical step. 5. Hydrodissection: BSS under capsule to free cortex. Hydrodelineation separates epinucleus. 6. Nuclear emulsification: Divide and conquer or stop and chop; high vacuum aspiration. 7. Irrigation-aspiration (I/A): Removes residual cortex. 8. IOL insertion: Foldable IOL injected into capsular bag via injector. 9. Wound seal: Stromal hydration; no sutures.

Advantages

Small 2.2 to 2.8 mm self-sealing incision; no sutures; minimal induced astigmatism; rapid visual recovery (1 to 2 days); topical anaesthesia possible; low intraoperative complication rate with modern technique.

Complications

Intraoperative: Posterior capsule rupture (1 to 3%), zonular dehiscence, dropped nucleus, wound burns (phaco energy), corneal endothelial damage, suprachoroidal haemorrhage (rare). Early post-op: IOP spike, corneal oedema, TASS (12 to 24 hours), wound leak. Late: PCO (20 to 40% within 5 years — YAG laser), endophthalmitis (0.03%), Irvine-Gass CMO (1 to 2%), retinal detachment (1%), IOL decentration, dysphotopsias.

Discuss ectopia lentis — causes, clinical features, and management.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Definition

Ectopia lentis is displacement of the crystalline lens from its normal position in the hyaloid fossa. Subluxation = partial displacement (lens edge visible in pupil). Dislocation = complete (into vitreous or AC).

Causes and Directions

Marfan syndrome: Superotemporal; FBN1 gene (AD); zonules stretched but present; subluxation more common; echocardiography essential. Homocystinuria: Inferonasal; cystathionine-β-synthase deficiency (AR); zonules rupture; complete dislocation more common; thromboembolism risk; avoid N2O. Weill-Marchesani: Inferior; brachydactyly, short stature; microspherophakia; pilocarpine preferred for pupillary block. Trauma: Most common overall; any direction. Others: Aniridia, Ehlers-Danlos, hyperlysinaemia.

Clinical Features

Monocular diplopia (lens edge crosses pupil), irregular astigmatism, accommodative failure, sudden visual loss (anterior dislocation), iridodonesis and phacodonesis (zonular weakness signs), visible lens equator in dilated pupil.

Complications

Pupillary block glaucoma, phacomorphic/phacolytic glaucoma, cataract, retinal detachment, amblyopia (children).

Management

Conservative: Optical correction, amblyopia treatment in children. Surgical: Less than 90° zonular loss — phaco with CTR in bag. 90 to 180° loss — modified Cionni CTR + scleral fixation. Greater than 180° or complete vitreous dislocation — PPV + lensectomy. Anterior dislocation — urgent lens removal. Systemic: Marfan — beta-blocker or losartan (aorta); Homocystinuria — pyridoxine (B6 in responders), low-methionine diet, anticoagulation planning, avoid N2O.

4
Discuss IOL power calculation — formulae, biometry methods, and sources of error.
10 Marks
View Answer Guide
Parameters Required

Axial length (AL), keratometry (K), and effective lens position (ELP). Axial length is the most critical — 1 mm error = 2.5 to 3 D refractive error postoperatively.

Biometry Methods

Contact A-scan: Probe on cornea; corneal indentation shortens AL; least accurate; most common cause of hyperopic surprise. Immersion A-scan: Probe in fluid shell; no corneal contact; more accurate. Optical biometry (IOLMaster/Lenstar): Low-coherence interferometry; 10-fold greater precision; gold standard; cannot penetrate dense cataract with absent red reflex.

Formulae

1st generation (Binkhorst, Colenbrander): theoretical; poor ELP; large errors. 2nd generation (SRK I, SRK II): regression-based; SRK = P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K. 3rd generation (SRK-T for normal; Hoffer Q for short below 22 mm; Holladay I for long above 26 mm): improved ELP; widely used. 4th generation and newer (Barrett Universal II, Hill-RBF): machine learning; most accurate across all ALs. Post-refractive: Haigis-L, Barrett True-K, history method — mandatory post-LASIK/PRK.

Sources of Error

Corneal indentation (contact A-scan — most common, causes hyperopic surprise). Wrong keratometry (post-refractive cornea). Staphyloma alignment (high myopia — measure perpendicular to fovea). Silicone oil in vitreous (alters sound velocity). Dense cataract preventing optical biometry. IOL tilt/decentration postoperatively.

What is the most common complication of cataract surgery and how is it treated?
DNB

Answer: Posterior capsule opacification (PCO) — treated by Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy.
