Chapter 12

Strabismus

Covering the full spectrum of squint — from terminology and classification to amblyopia management, binocular vision testing, Hess chart interpretation, and surgical correction.

UGPG

Six cards covering the highest-yield facts in strabismus — the definitions that examiners test repeatedly, the classification rules for esotropia and exotropia, amblyopia management, Hess chart logic, and the mnemonic to understand yoke muscle relationships. Use these for rapid revision before any OSCE or written paper.

Key Terminology HY

Tropia is a manifest deviation — the eye is visibly misaligned at rest, even with both eyes open. Phoria is a latent deviation that only becomes apparent when fusion is interrupted, for instance by the cover test.

  • Esotropia: Eye turns inward (convergent)
  • Exotropia: Eye turns outward (divergent)
  • Hypertropia: Eye turns upward
  • Hypotropia: Eye turns downward

Comitant squint: The angle of deviation remains the same in all directions of gaze. Incomitant squint: The angle changes with gaze direction, which points toward an extraocular muscle palsy or mechanical restriction.

Esotropia Types HY

Infantile (Congenital) Esotropia: Onset before 6 months, large angle typically over 30 PD, no significant hypermetropia, cross-fixation present (the child uses the right medial rectus to fixate in the left field and vice versa). Inferior oblique overaction and DVD develop later. Surgery by age 2 is the goal to give the visual system the best chance of developing binocularity.

Accommodative Esotropia: Appears in a hypermetropic child around age 2 to 3 years. The excess accommodation needed to compensate for hyperopia drives convergence through the AC/A synkinesis. Fully accommodative — corrects completely with glasses. Partially accommodative — glasses reduce but do not eliminate the deviation; the residual non-accommodative component needs surgery.

Amblyopia HY

Reduced BCVA below 6/9 in a structurally normal eye that cannot be improved by glasses alone, caused by abnormal visual experience during the critical period from birth to approximately 7 to 8 years.

  • Anisometropic: Most common overall type — unequal refractive error between the two eyes
  • Strabismic: The deviating eye is suppressed; most common in clinical practice settings
  • Deprivation: Most severe — dense cataract, ptosis covering the pupil, corneal opacity; requires the most urgent treatment

Treatment: Correct spectacle refraction first, allow 6 to 10 weeks of adaptation, then occlusion therapy — patching the dominant eye 2 to 6 hours per day depending on severity. Atropine penalisation to the dominant eye is an alternative with fewer compliance issues.

Hess Chart Interpretation HY

The Hess chart maps ocular movements in all nine positions of gaze by dissociating the eyes with red-green glasses. Two charts are produced — one for each eye.

  • Smaller chart belongs to the affected eye — the underacting muscle makes that field contracted
  • Larger chart belongs to the fellow eye — the yoke muscle overacts by Hering's law

To identify the paretic muscle: find the most contracted field on the smaller chart. The muscle responsible for movement in that direction is the culprit. For example, if the smaller chart is contracted on lateral gaze to the right, the right lateral rectus is paretic.

Hering's law states that equal and simultaneous innervation is sent to yoke muscles — so when one muscle underacts, its contralateral yoke overacts, producing the characteristic asymmetry on the Hess chart.

Prism Cover Test HY

The most accurate method for measuring the angle of deviation. After the alternate cover test reveals the direction of the squint, prisms are placed in front of the deviating eye and the strength is increased until no corrective movement occurs when the cover is moved from one eye to the other.

  • Base-out prism for esotropia (neutralises inward deviation)
  • Base-in prism for exotropia
  • Base-down prism for hypertropia

The result in prism dioptres guides surgical planning. As a rough guide, each millimetre of recession or resection corrects approximately 3 to 5 PD of deviation, though the dose-response varies by muscle and individual.

Mnemonics & Key Ratios

Yoke muscles (Hering's law pairs):

  • Right lateral rectus pairs with left medial rectus (right gaze)
  • Right superior rectus pairs with left inferior oblique (right and up)
  • Right inferior rectus pairs with left superior oblique (right and down)

AC/A ratio (accommodative convergence to accommodation): normal is 3 to 5 prism dioptres per dioptre of accommodation. A high AC/A ratio means the eye converges excessively for each unit of accommodation — this produces esotropia preferentially at near, as seen in convergence excess and high AC/A accommodative esotropia. Bifocals reduce the near demand and are the optical solution.

Kappa angle: A positive angle kappa (nasal fovea displacement) gives a false appearance of exotropia — pseudoexotropia. A negative angle kappa (temporal fovea) gives pseudoesotropia. Epicanthal folds cause the most common apparent squint in children.

Classification and Terminology

UGPG
▼

Manifest versus Latent Deviation

The first distinction to make in any squinting patient is whether the deviation is manifest or latent. A tropia is present constantly — the eye deviates even with both eyes open, and fusion is insufficient to overcome it. A phoria is latent — the eyes appear straight when both are open because fusion is maintaining alignment, but the underlying tendency to deviate becomes visible when fusion is disrupted by the cover test. Phorias are extremely common and generally asymptomatic; they only become problematic when the vergence reserve fails, leading to decompensation.

Comitant versus Incomitant

In comitant strabismus, the angle of deviation is the same regardless of the direction of gaze. The classic example is infantile esotropia, where the large inward deviation is constant in all positions. In incomitant strabismus, the angle varies with gaze direction — typically it is greatest in the field of action of the paretic or restricted muscle. This pattern should always raise the suspicion of an extraocular muscle palsy, mechanical restriction (as in thyroid eye disease), or a restrictive syndrome such as Duane's or Brown's.

Methods of Measuring the Angle of Deviation

The Hirschberg corneal reflex test is the simplest estimate. A penlight is held 33 cm in front of the patient and the position of the corneal reflex is noted in each eye. Normally the reflex sits slightly nasal to centre, at approximately the centre of the pupil. Each millimetre of displacement from the pupil centre corresponds to approximately 15 to 20 prism dioptres of deviation. The test is quick and works in young children and those who cannot cooperate with prisms.

The Krimsky test is a refinement of Hirschberg. Prisms are placed in front of the deviating eye and increased until the corneal reflex is centred. The prism strength at that point is the estimated angle. It does not require fixation on a target and is useful in very young children.

The prism and cover test is the gold standard. It requires alternating cover combined with prisms until no movement is seen — it measures the full angle including both phoric and tropic components. Always measure at distance (6 metres) and at near (1/3 metre) separately, because the two measurements differ in conditions such as accommodative esotropia and convergence insufficiency exotropia.

Pseudoesotropia and the Kappa angle: The most common reason a child is referred for squint and found to have none is pseudoesotropia from epicanthal folds. The broad nasal bridge and skin folds cover the nasal sclera, giving the appearance of an inward deviation. The corneal reflex test is symmetrical and the cover test shows no movement. Explanation and reassurance are all that is needed. The kappa angle refers to the angle between the visual axis and the pupillary axis. A positive kappa angle (nasal displacement of the fovea relative to the pupil) makes the reflex appear temporal and creates an impression of exotropia. A negative kappa angle creates the impression of esotropia.

Naming Convention

By convention, the prefixes describe the direction of the deviation: eso for convergent, exo for divergent, hyper for upward, and hypo for downward. The suffix tropia indicates a manifest deviation and phoria a latent one. So esophoria is a latent tendency for the eye to converge, while esotropia is a manifest convergent squint. Vertical deviations are named by the eye that is higher — if the right eye is elevated, the patient has a right hypertropia, which is the same as a left hypotropia.

Esotropia in Detail

UGPG
▼

Infantile Esotropia

By definition, infantile esotropia presents before six months of age. The angle is characteristically large, usually between 30 and 60 prism dioptres, and comitant. There is no significant hypermetropic refractive error driving the deviation — the condition is not accommodative. Cross-fixation is a useful clinical sign: the child uses the left eye to fixate in the right field of gaze and the right eye to fixate in the left field, employing each medial rectus for fixation in the contralateral field. This can mimic bilateral abduction failure, but is distinguished by the fact that both eyes abduct normally when the vestibulo-ocular reflex is tested (doll's head manoeuvre).

As these children grow, inferior oblique overaction commonly develops, and dissociated vertical deviation (DVD) also appears. In DVD, one eye drifts up and outward when it is not being used for fixation, but crucially this upward drift is equal in each eye — it is not a true hypertropia because there is no corresponding depression of the other eye. DVD is driven by abnormal visual development rather than a specific muscle palsy.

Management is surgical. Spectacles should be prescribed if there is significant hypermetropia, but the primary treatment is bilateral medial rectus recession. The goal is alignment before the age of two years, when binocular cortical development still has some plasticity. Alignment achieved by two years offers a reasonable chance of peripheral fusion even if central stereoacuity is not achieved.

Accommodative Esotropia

This is perhaps the most conceptually important esotropia for exam purposes. The child is hypermetropic — typically +2.50 dioptres or more — and uses accommodation to obtain a clear retinal image. Accommodation drives convergence through the synkinetic relationship quantified by the AC/A ratio. In a susceptible child, this convergence exceeds the divergence fusional reserve, and esotropia develops. The typical presentation is a child between the ages of 18 months and 3 years who was noticed to have an inward turn, often after a febrile illness or period of visual stress.

On cycloplegic refraction, significant hypermetropia is found. Prescribing the full cycloplegic refraction and asking the child to wear the glasses consistently is the first and most important step. In fully accommodative esotropia, the deviation corrects completely with glasses — surgery is not needed, and the goal is simply to maintain spectacle wear through the critical period. The eyes may straighten within weeks of wearing glasses.

Partially accommodative esotropia has a residual deviation despite glasses because of an additional non-accommodative component — often a decompensated esophoria. In this case, glasses reduce but do not eliminate the deviation, and the residual angle requires surgery, typically bilateral medial rectus recession.

High AC/A Ratio Esotropia

In some children, the AC/A ratio is abnormally high — meaning the eyes converge far more than expected for a given amount of accommodation. The practical consequence is that the esotropia is much worse at near (where accommodation is high) than at distance (where accommodation is minimal). These children are straight at distance but cross at near. The treatment is bifocal spectacles — the addition in the lower segment reduces the accommodation demand at near, reducing the convergence response and straightening the eyes at near distance.

Exotropia

UGPG
▼

Intermittent Exotropia

This is the most common form of exotropia. The deviation is not present all the time — the eyes are straight under good lighting conditions and when the child is attentive, but the deviation becomes manifest when the child is tired, unwell, or looking into bright light. The reason it tends to break in bright light is that the child closes or squints one eye to avoid diplopia. Parents often describe the child closing one eye in the sun before any frank deviation is noticed.

Suppression is the mechanism that keeps these children symptom-free despite the intermittent turn. When the eye deviates, the cortex suppresses the image from that eye, preventing diplopia. Because suppression is intermittent rather than constant, binocular vision is preserved to a degree. This is why intermittent exotropia has a better prognosis for binocularity than constant exotropia or suppression-based infantile esotropia.

Two patterns are important to recognise. In divergence excess type, the exotropia is larger at distance than at near — the divergence control is weakest when convergence demand is lowest. In convergence insufficiency type, the exotropia is worse at near, driven by an inability to sustain convergence. Near convergence insufficiency responds well to convergence exercises and base-in prisms.

Management of Exotropia

Part-time patching of the dominant eye can stimulate motor fusion in intermittent exotropia. Orthoptic exercises to improve fusional convergence amplitude are helpful in convergence insufficiency. When the deviation is constant or breaking down frequently, surgery is indicated. The standard procedure for divergence excess type is bilateral lateral rectus recession; for convergence insufficiency, bilateral medial rectus resection is preferred. Overcorrection by 10 to 15 PD at the time of surgery is acceptable and expected because the initial alignment tends to drift back toward exotropia over time.

Constant versus intermittent exotropia: Constant exotropia in infancy (before 12 months) is unusual and should raise the suspicion of a sensory exotropia — a deviation driven by poor vision in one eye, where the visual cortex suppresses a chronically blurred image and the eye drifts outward. The cause is usually a significant unilateral refractive error, amblyopia, or intraocular pathology. Always check visual acuity and refraction before attributing any deviation to a primary motor problem.

Amblyopia

UGPG
▼

Pathophysiology and the Critical Period

Amblyopia results from abnormal visual experience during the critical period of visual cortical development. The critical period extends from birth to approximately 7 to 8 years, with the peak of cortical plasticity between 18 months and 5 years. During this window, the visual cortex is actively forming and refining the connections that underlie spatial vision. If one eye provides a consistently degraded, blurred, or suppressed signal to the cortex, the connections for that eye are not established properly, and the resulting reduction in acuity cannot be corrected by optical means alone once the critical period closes.

Types of Amblyopia

Anisometropic amblyopia results from a significant difference in refractive error between the two eyes. The eye with the higher refractive error — most commonly the more hyperopic or more astigmatic eye — receives a chronically blurred image regardless of where the eyes are pointing. There is no manifest squint, so parents rarely notice it, which is why this type tends to present late. Hyperopic anisometropia causes amblyopia more readily than myopic anisometropia because in myopia the child can bring objects close to compensate, while in hyperopia no such strategy exists without spectacles.

Strabismic amblyopia occurs because the cortex develops a strong preference for the fixating eye and actively suppresses the signal from the deviating eye to avoid diplopia. Over time this suppression deepens into true amblyopia. The suppression scotoma is central — it covers the fovea of the deviating eye — which is why the acuity loss is specifically in the central vision.

Deprivation amblyopia is the most severe type because a complete barrier to form vision — a dense congenital cataract, a corneal opacity, or a complete ptosis covering the pupil — prevents any image formation during the critical period. The cortex never receives the visual input it needs to develop normal connections. Even a few weeks of deprivation in a newborn can produce profound, irreversible amblyopia. This is why congenital cataract must be extracted within the first six to eight weeks of life and optical correction provided immediately.

Management

Treatment follows a logical sequence. The first step is always to remove the cause: surgery for a deprivation amblyopia (cataract extraction, ptosis repair), and full optical correction for refractive amblyopia. The spectacles alone produce improvement over weeks to months in anisometropic amblyopia because even the act of wearing the correct correction improves the retinal image quality — a phenomenon called refractive adaptation.

Once optical correction is in place, active occlusion therapy begins. Patching the dominant eye forces the amblyopic eye to take over fixation, stimulating the dormant cortical connections. The prescription depends on severity: approximately 2 hours daily for mild amblyopia and up to 6 hours daily for moderate or severe cases. Treatment must continue until the acuity is equal or has plateaued at maximum improvement — stopping too early causes regression.

Atropine penalisation is an alternative for children who will not tolerate a patch. A drop of atropine 1% in the dominant eye blurs its near vision, forcing the amblyopic eye to take over for near tasks. It is particularly useful in mild to moderate amblyopia and for maintaining gains after successful patching. Compliance is generally better than with patching.

The critical window closes by approximately 8 years of age. Treatment is still worth attempting up to age 10 to 12 in motivated children, but the results diminish significantly with age. After 12 years, treatment rarely produces meaningful improvement. This is why every child should have vision screening at school entry — undetected anisometropic amblyopia at age 5 is treatable; discovered at age 12, it rarely is.
Practical point — always correct the refraction first: It is a common mistake to patch before giving spectacles. Prescribing spectacles first and waiting 6 to 10 weeks for adaptation is essential because some of the apparent amblyopia is simply blur from uncorrected refractive error. After adaptation, reassess acuity; the residual deficit after full refractive correction is the true depth of the amblyopia, and that is what patching then addresses.

Paralytic Strabismus — General Features

In paralytic strabismus, an extraocular muscle or its nerve supply has failed. The key clinical features distinguish it from comitant squint: sudden onset in an adult with binocular diplopia; the diplopia is worst in the direction of action of the paretic muscle; and the secondary deviation (the deviation when the paretic eye is fixating) is greater than the primary deviation (when the normal eye fixates). This difference is explained by Hering's law — when the paretic eye fixates, extra innervation must be sent to it to maintain fixation, and by the law of equal innervation, that same excess signal is sent to the yoke muscle of the other eye, causing it to overact and swing further in the opposite direction.

Patients develop a compensatory head posture to move their eyes away from the field of action of the paretic muscle, reducing diplopia. This posture is a useful clinical sign and should prompt examination of ocular motility in any child or adult with a habitual head tilt or face turn.

Superior Oblique Palsy (CN IV)

Fourth nerve palsy is the most common cause of a vertical diplopia and the most common cranial nerve palsy to affect ocular motility. The superior oblique depresses and intorts the eye, with its main depressor action being greatest in adduction. A superior oblique palsy therefore produces a hypertropia of the affected eye that is worst on adduction and on head tilt to the ipsilateral side.

The Bielschowsky head tilt test is the diagnostic test. When the head is tilted toward the shoulder on the side of the palsy, the ipsilateral eye intorts to maintain correct orientation. This intorsion should be performed by the superior oblique. With the superior oblique paralysed, the superior rectus (the only other intortor) takes over, and because the superior rectus is an elevator, the hypertropia of the affected eye worsens on ipsilateral head tilt. The test is positive when the hypertropia increases on head tilt toward the affected side.

The compensatory head posture in CN IV palsy is a contralateral head tilt — tilting away from the affected side to use the unopposed intortion of the fellow eye and reduce the vertical diplopia. A child with long-standing CN IV palsy will often have facial asymmetry from the sustained head tilt, which is an important clue to the chronicity of the condition.

Duane's Retraction Syndrome

Duane's syndrome is a congenital co-innervation anomaly caused by absence or hypoplasia of the abducens nucleus, with the lateral rectus being supplied by a branch of the third nerve. The consequence is co-contraction of the medial and lateral recti on attempted adduction, causing the globe to retract into the orbit and the palpebral fissure to narrow. Type I is by far the most common — abduction is limited or absent while adduction is normal or near-normal. Type II shows limited adduction with relatively preserved abduction. Type III shows limitation of both movements.

The globe retraction and fissure narrowing on adduction are pathognomonic. There is no diplopia in most cases because suppression develops in childhood. A face turn is common — toward the affected side in Type I to keep the eye in the abducted position where it functions best. Surgery is indicated when there is a significant head posture, a large deviation in primary position, or troublesome upshooting or downshooting on adduction (from slippage of the co-contracting recti). The aim is always to improve the primary position and head posture, not to restore full motility.

A and V patterns: Some patients show a change in horizontal alignment with vertical gaze. An A pattern is present when the eyes are more esotropic in upgaze or more exotropic in downgaze — the deviation narrows toward the top of the letter A. A V pattern is present when the eyes are more exotropic in upgaze or more esotropic in downgaze. A patterns are usually associated with superior oblique overaction or inferior oblique underaction; V patterns more commonly reflect inferior oblique overaction. Surgical correction involves oblique weakening or vertical transposition of the horizontal recti.

Binocular Vision and Hess Chart

Grades of Binocular Single Vision

Normal binocular vision has three grades, assessed classically using the synoptophore. The first grade is simultaneous perception — the brain can process an image from each eye at the same time without suppressing either. This is the most basic level. The second grade is fusion — the brain combines slightly different images from the two eyes into a single percept. Fusion has a motor component (vergence movements to maintain alignment) and a sensory component (the actual combining of images). The third and highest grade is stereopsis — the perception of depth that arises from the disparity between the two retinal images.

Stereoacuity tests include the Frisby test (random dot, no glasses needed — the most "natural" test), the TNO test (random dot anaglyph requiring red-green glasses), the Lang stereo test (no glasses, suitable for young children), the Randot test, and the Titmus fly test. For detailed clinical measurement across all three grades, the synoptophore is used. The Worth 4-dot test assesses fusion at near and distance using red-green glasses and a torch with coloured dots — it can identify suppression, normal fusion, or diplopia.

The Hess Chart in Detail

The Hess chart (and its variant, the Lees screen) tests ocular motility by dissociating the eyes with red-green goggles. The patient sits 50 cm from a calibrated screen. With one eye covered by the red filter and the other by the green, the examiner projects a light spot to each of the nine positions of gaze and asks the patient to superimpose their own torch onto the projected spot. Because of dissociation, each eye independently indicates where it thinks it is pointing in each position of gaze.

The chart of the affected eye will be contracted — its movements are limited, so it falls short of the correct positions in the field of action of the paretic muscle. The chart of the fellow eye will be expanded — the yoke muscle overacts (Hering's law), so it goes beyond the correct positions in the conjugate direction.

To interpret a Hess chart, always first determine which chart is smaller. That eye is affected. Then look for the most contracted part of the smaller chart — the direction of maximum underaction identifies the paretic muscle. For example, a right lateral rectus palsy will produce a contracted chart of the right eye on right lateral gaze, with an expanded chart of the left eye on left lateral gaze (left medial rectus overaction, the yoke muscle).

Hess chart serial monitoring: In an acute paralytic strabismus where the aetiology is known to be benign (such as microvascular CN III or IV palsy in diabetes), serial Hess charts are extremely useful for tracking recovery. A progressively expanding affected chart indicates recovery of the paretic muscle. Spread of comitance — when the two charts begin to approach equal size — indicates secondary contracture of the antagonist muscle, a sign of longstanding palsy.

Surgical Management of Strabismus

UGPG
▼

Principles: Weakening and Strengthening Procedures

All strabismus surgery is built on two fundamental operations. Recession weakens a muscle by detaching it from its insertion on the sclera and reattaching it at a more posterior point. By moving the insertion backward, the effective pulling force of the muscle is reduced. A bilateral medial rectus recession is the standard approach for esotropia — weakening both convergence-producing muscles to allow the eyes to straighten.

Resection strengthens a muscle by removing a segment of it and reattaching the muscle at the original insertion point. The shortened muscle has more resting tension and pulls the eye more effectively in its direction of action. Medial rectus resection strengthens convergence; lateral rectus resection strengthens abduction.

Operations are often combined. A recession-resection procedure on one eye involves recessing the antagonist (weakening the overacting or deviating muscle) and resecting the agonist (strengthening the underacting muscle) on the same eye. For example, in an esotropia treated unilaterally, the medial rectus is recessed and the lateral rectus resected.

Dose Planning

The amount of recession or resection is calculated from the measured angle of deviation. The relationship is not linear and depends on the muscle being operated and the pre-operative angle. As a rough clinical guide, 1 mm of medial rectus recession corrects approximately 3 to 4 prism dioptres of esotropia; 1 mm of lateral rectus recession corrects approximately 2 to 3 prism dioptres of exotropia. Dose tables are available and adjusted for the specific muscle. The pre-operative angle must be stable and measured carefully at both distance and near before planning surgery.

Adjustable Sutures

In cooperative adults, adjustable sutures allow the surgeon to modify the surgical result under topical anaesthesia on the operating table or more commonly on the day after surgery. The muscle is initially attached with a slip-knot; once the patient is awake and alignment can be assessed in the clinic, the suture is adjusted to advance or recess the muscle further. This approach is particularly valuable in patients with thyroid eye disease, longstanding paralytic strabismus with contracture, or re-operations where the response to surgery is unpredictable.

Complications

The most feared surgical complication is a slipped or lost muscle, where the muscle retracts under the conjunctiva or behind Tenon's capsule. This causes a sudden large overcorrection in the direction away from the operated muscle. Urgent re-exploration and retrieval are needed. Globe perforation by the scleral suture needle is rare but serious; it is more common during resection and in high myopes with thin sclera. Anterior segment ischaemia can develop if surgery is performed on more than two recti from the same eye in one session, because the anterior ciliary arteries — which travel within the recti — are the primary blood supply to the anterior segment. This risk is highest in the elderly and in patients with vascular disease.

Recession versus Resection
Recession weakens — the muscle is moved back (posteriorly), reducing its mechanical advantage. Think of a rubber band stretched further before it has to contract. Resection strengthens — the muscle is shortened, making it tighter. The operated eye will move more easily in the direction of the resected muscle. When confused, remember: Recession = Relax; Resection = Reinforce.

These guides describe the core examination techniques in strabismus practice. Knowing the steps is necessary but not sufficient — understanding what each step is designed to reveal, and what a finding actually means, is what separates a competent examiner from one who simply goes through the motions.

Cover-Uncover Test and Alternate Cover Test Bedside
1
Setup and Target

Seat the patient comfortably at the appropriate distance — 6 metres for distance fixation, 33 cm for near. Use an accommodative target: a detailed letter or picture that the child must look at carefully to keep accommodation active. In young children, an interesting toy with fine detail works well. If accommodation is not controlled, an accommodative esotropia will appear straight. Always assess both distance and near because the deviation may differ.

2
Cover-Uncover Test — Detecting the Tropia

Cover one eye with an occluder and watch the other (uncovered) eye. If the uncovered eye moves to take up fixation when the fellow eye is covered, a tropia is present in the uncovered eye. The direction of the movement tells you the type of deviation: if the uncovered eye moves inward to pick up fixation, it was deviated outward — exotropia. If it moves outward, the eye was in esotropia. Now uncover the covered eye and watch it: if it drifts before returning to fixation, a phoria is present. The cover-uncover test thus detects both tropias (movement of the uncovered eye) and phorias (drift and recovery of the covered eye on uncovering).

A common exam error is not watching the right eye. When you cover the right eye, watch the left eye for movement (detecting tropia in the left eye). When you cover the left eye, watch the right eye. Then repeat the uncover component for each side.
3
Alternate Cover Test — Measuring Total Deviation

Move the cover briskly from one eye to the other without allowing any period of binocular viewing. This fully dissociates the eyes, breaking fusion. The total deviation — phoria plus tropia — is now visible. Each time the cover is moved, watch the eye that is being uncovered: the direction it moves from when uncovered indicates the direction of the latent or manifest deviation. This is the test you combine with prisms to measure the full angle. The alternate cover test will always show a larger movement than the cover-uncover test in the same patient, because it includes the phoric component.

4
Interpreting the Findings

If the cover-uncover test is negative but the alternate cover test reveals a deviation, the patient has a phoria — there is a latent tendency to deviate that is maintained by fusion in normal binocular viewing. If the cover-uncover test is positive, there is a tropia. The size of the movement gives a rough estimate of the angle; for accurate measurement, prisms are needed. Always test in at least the six cardinal positions of gaze in addition to primary position to determine whether the deviation is comitant.

Performing cover tests in a child requires patience. Use an interesting target, work quickly, and accept that multiple attempts may be needed. A brief non-fixating moment should not be interpreted as a tropia — ensure the child is genuinely fixating before you cover.
Hirschberg Corneal Reflex Test and Krimsky Test Bedside
1
Hirschberg Test

Hold a pen torch at arm's length (approximately 33 cm) directly in front of the patient. Ask the patient to look at the light. The corneal reflex normally falls just slightly nasal to the centre of the pupil in each eye. If one reflex is displaced, the eye is deviated. A reflex at the pupil margin corresponds to approximately 15 prism dioptres; at the mid-iris, approximately 30 PD; at the limbus, approximately 45 PD. The direction of displacement is opposite to the direction of deviation — a reflex displaced temporally means the eye is deviated nasally (esotropia); a reflex displaced nasally means exotropia.

2
Krimsky Test

This test refines the Hirschberg estimate by using prisms to centre the displaced reflex. Place prisms of increasing strength in front of the deviating eye, apex toward the direction of deviation, until the corneal reflexes appear symmetric. The prism power at which symmetry is achieved is the estimated angle. The Krimsky test is particularly useful when the patient cannot cooperate with a formal prism cover test — for instance, in infants or patients with very poor vision in one eye who cannot alternate fixation.

Remember the Krimsky convention: for esotropia, the prism base is placed outward (base-out) in front of the esotropic eye. For exotropia, base-in in front of the exotropic eye. The prism displaces the image to match the position of the deviated eye.
3
Angle Kappa and Pseudostrabismus

Always check for a significant angle kappa before concluding a deviation is present. If the corneal reflex appears to be displaced but the cover test reveals no movement, the apparent displacement is due to the angle kappa. A positive angle kappa (fovea displaced nasally) makes the reflex appear temporal and simulates exotropia. Epicanthal folds are a far more common cause of apparent esotropia in infants than any true deviation — the fold narrows the apparent nasal sclera, creating the impression of inward deviation. The cover-uncover test immediately distinguishes the two.

Prism Cover Test Technique Clinic
1
Prerequisites

The patient must be wearing their full refractive correction. The target must be an accommodative fixation target at the correct distance. You need a prism bar (loose prisms are an acceptable alternative for high angles). Perform the alternate cover test first without prisms to confirm the direction of the deviation and get a rough estimate of the angle. This tells you which prism orientation to use and where to start.

2
Technique

Hold the prism bar in front of one eye — conventionally the deviating eye, though in practice either eye may be used as long as it is consistent. Begin with a low prism value and perform the alternate cover test while maintaining the prism in position. Increase the prism strength incrementally. The endpoint is reached when there is no movement of the eye when the cover is transferred from one eye to the other. At this point, the prism exactly neutralises the deviation — all the image displacement caused by the squint is being corrected by the prism, and there is no stimulus for a corrective movement.

3
Recording and What to Measure

Record the prism value at the endpoint, the eye in front of which the prism was held, and whether the measurement was at distance or near. Always measure at both distances. Note any difference between distance and near measurements — a deviation that is significantly larger at near indicates a high AC/A ratio or convergence insufficiency, while a deviation larger at distance suggests divergence excess. The difference between distance and near measurements is as diagnostically important as either value alone.

Avoid looking through the prism yourself when assessing the patient's eye movement — the prism will distort your view. Keep the prism in front of the patient's eye and look past it to observe the movement of the eye you are covering and uncovering.
Reading a Hess Chart Investigations
1
Orientation

The Hess chart records the movement of each eye in nine positions of gaze. Two charts are produced — one for the right eye, one for the left. The charts are printed on a standard grid with the nine gaze positions arranged in the same anatomical configuration as gaze: centre is primary position, right is right gaze, upper right is right and up, and so on. The patient's own eye movements are plotted as points, which are then connected to form a polygon. A normal polygon fills the grid symmetrically in all nine positions.

2
Identifying the Affected Eye

The smaller chart belongs to the affected eye. When a muscle is paretic, the eye falls short of the correct position in that muscle's field of action, making the plotted polygon smaller in that direction. Compare the two charts: whichever is clearly smaller (contracted) contains the paretic eye. This is a fundamental rule that should not be applied in reverse — the larger chart does not belong to the affected eye.

3
Identifying the Paretic Muscle

On the smaller chart, find the field of action that is most contracted — the position where the polygon is furthest from the outer border of the grid. The muscle responsible for movement in that direction is the paretic muscle. Use the standard muscle actions: lateral rectus — lateral gaze; medial rectus — medial gaze; superior rectus — up and in; inferior rectus — down and in; superior oblique — down and in (primarily); inferior oblique — up and out. Cross-reference with the larger chart to confirm: the most expanded field of the fellow eye should be in the conjugate position, indicating overaction of the yoke muscle by Hering's law.

A practical sequence: (1) Find the smaller chart. (2) Find its most contracted field. (3) Name the eye and the gaze direction. (4) Name the muscle responsible for that gaze direction in that eye. (5) Confirm by checking the fellow eye's chart shows expansion in the conjugate field.
4
Paralytic versus Restrictive Pattern

A paralytic pattern shows the smaller chart on the side of the affected muscle. A restrictive pattern — such as thyroid eye disease limiting elevation — will show an unusual finding: the restricted eye cannot elevate, but because restriction mechanically prevents the eye from moving (rather than failure of innervation), the Hering's law effect on the fellow eye is different. In restriction, the fellow eye's chart may actually be contracted in the opposite direction because the restricted eye needs less innervation than expected. Serial Hess charts over weeks allow tracking of recovery or progression and are far more informative than a single measurement.

1 Classify amblyopia and describe the management of strabismic and anisometropic amblyopia. 10 Marks
Definition

Amblyopia is a reduction in BCVA in a structurally normal eye to below 6/9, not correctable by glasses alone, resulting from abnormal visual experience during the critical period of visual cortical development (birth to approximately 7 to 8 years).

Classification

Three main types. Strabismic: caused by suppression of the deviating eye; cortex develops fixation preference for the non-deviating eye. Anisometropic: unequal refractive error causes one eye to receive a chronically blurred image; hyperopic anisometropia is more amblyogenic than myopic; astigmatic amblyopia can occur with high unilateral astigmatism. Deprivation: most severe; caused by complete obstruction of the visual axis — dense congenital cataract, corneal opacity, complete ptosis; even brief deprivation in the neonatal period produces profound amblyopia. Bilateral deprivation (bilateral cataracts) can occur but is rarer and the prognosis with early treatment is better than unilateral.

Management of Strabismic Amblyopia

Correct any refractive error first. Treat the strabismus (if surgery is indicated, this may be done before or after amblyopia treatment depending on the case). Occlusion therapy: patch the dominant (fixating) eye for 2 to 6 hours daily — the duration depends on depth of amblyopia. Monitor acuity monthly. Continue until acuity equalises or plateaus over 3 months. After successful patching, maintenance patching is often continued at a reduced dose (1 hour per day) until age 7 to 8 years to prevent regression.

Management of Anisometropic Amblyopia

Full cycloplegic refraction prescription first. Allow 6 to 10 weeks of adaptation — significant improvement in acuity occurs from spectacles alone during this period. Reassess BCVA after adaptation; the residual deficit is the true amblyopic depth. Then add occlusion therapy (patching dominant eye 2 hours daily for mild, up to 6 hours for severe). Atropine 1% to the dominant eye is an alternative — less intrusive, shown in PEDIG trials to produce equivalent outcomes to patching for mild to moderate amblyopia. Monitor monthly. Prognosis depends on depth of amblyopia and age at presentation; younger age = better outcome.

Prognosis Factors

Age at presentation and start of treatment: the younger, the better. Depth of amblyopia: mild amblyopia responds faster and more completely than dense amblyopia. Type: deprivation = worst; anisometropic = good if treated early; strabismic = intermediate. Compliance with patching: the single most modifiable prognostic factor.

2 Describe the cover test and prism cover test in the assessment of strabismus. How is the angle of deviation measured? 10 Marks
Cover-Uncover Test

Detects manifest deviation (tropia). Cover one eye and observe the uncovered eye — if it moves to take fixation, a tropia is present. The direction of movement indicates the type: inward movement (nasal) indicates the eye was deviated outward (exotropia); outward movement (temporal) indicates esotropia. Then uncover the covered eye — if it drifts before recovering fixation, a phoria is present. Repeat for the other eye. Test at both 6 metres and 33 cm with an accommodative target and full spectacle correction.

Alternate Cover Test

Measures total deviation (phoria + tropia). The cover is moved briskly from eye to eye without any period of binocular fusion. Observe the uncovered eye each time — the direction of refixation movement indicates the full angle of deviation. The alternate cover test always reveals a larger movement than the cover-uncover test in the same patient because it includes the latent phoric component.

Hirschberg and Krimsky Tests

Hirschberg: penlight 33 cm in front, assess corneal reflex symmetry. 1 mm displacement = 15 to 20 PD. At pupil margin = 15 PD; mid-iris = 30 PD; limbus = 45 PD. Krimsky: prisms are placed to centre the asymmetric reflex — a more accurate estimate than Hirschberg alone. Useful when the patient cannot cooperate with cover tests.

Prism Cover Test — Gold Standard

After the alternate cover test reveals the direction of deviation, prisms are placed apex toward the deviation in front of the deviating (or fixating) eye. The alternate cover test is repeated with prisms of increasing strength until no movement is seen on switching the cover. The neutralising prism power in prism dioptres is the angle of deviation. Always measure at distance (6 m) and near (33 cm) separately. Record separately for each distance and note the difference, which is diagnostically significant. Prism base orientation: base-out for esotropia, base-in for exotropia, base-down for hypertropia of the higher eye.

Interpretation of Difference Between Distances

Esotropia worse at near: high AC/A ratio or convergence excess — consider bifocals. Esotropia worse at distance: divergence insufficiency (or distant fixation-dependent esophoria breakdown). Exotropia worse at near: convergence insufficiency — exercises and base-in prisms. Exotropia worse at distance: divergence excess — bilateral lateral rectus recession.

3 Discuss accommodative esotropia — pathophysiology, classification, and management. 10 Marks
Pathophysiology

The accommodative convergence to accommodation (AC/A) relationship means that every dioptre of accommodation drives a certain number of prism dioptres of convergence. In a hypermetropic child, correcting the blur on the retina requires active accommodation. This drives excessive convergence through the synkinesis, overcoming the divergence fusional reserve and resulting in esotropia. The degree of esotropia depends on the amount of hypermetropia, the AC/A ratio, and the strength of the divergence fusional reserve.

Classification

Fully accommodative esotropia: high hypermetropia, normal AC/A ratio, deviation fully corrects with spectacles. No surgery needed. Partially accommodative esotropia: glasses correct part of the deviation; a non-accommodative component persists (often a decompensated esophoria). Requires surgery for the residual angle. High AC/A ratio (convergence excess) esotropia: deviation much worse at near than distance, even with full hypermetropic correction. AC/A ratio above 6:1. Managed with bifocal addition to reduce near accommodation demand.

Diagnosis

Cycloplegic refraction is essential — atropine 1% for 3 days before refraction in children under 6 years (cyclopentolate 1% is acceptable over 6 years). Prescribe the full cycloplegic refraction. Measure deviation at distance and near with glasses. If deviation is more than 10 PD larger at near than at distance with glasses, the AC/A ratio is high.

Management

Fully accommodative: prescribe full cycloplegic refraction; review every 6 months; update glasses as needed. Partially accommodative: glasses first, then surgery (bilateral MR recession) for the residual deviation at distance after 3 to 6 months of spectacle wear. High AC/A ratio: bifocal addition (+2.50 to +3.00 D addition in the lower segment); reduces near accommodation, reduces convergence, straightens eyes at near. Surgery may eventually be needed if bifocal dependence becomes problematic. Amblyopia management throughout — patch if there is fixation preference.

Prognosis

Fully accommodative esotropia: excellent — most children maintain alignment as the hypermetropia gradually reduces with growth. A proportion develop good stereoacuity. Partially accommodative: depends on how early treatment is started and how much residual deviation remains after surgery. High AC/A: may need bifocals indefinitely through childhood; many outgrow it by teenage years as the AC/A ratio normalises.

4 Describe the surgical management of strabismus — principles of recession and resection, indications for adjustable sutures, and important complications. 10 Marks
Surgical Principles

Strabismus surgery modifies the mechanical properties of the extraocular muscles to change the resting position of the eyes and their movement in different gazes. Two fundamental procedures: recession (weakening) and resection (strengthening). For each, the muscle is first disinserted from the sclera. Recession: the muscle is reattached more posteriorly — reducing its moment arm and mechanical advantage. Resection: a segment of the muscle is excised and the shortened muscle is reattached at the original insertion — increasing its tension and action.

Dose Planning

Based on the angle of deviation measured by prism cover test at distance and near. As a rough guide: medial rectus recession corrects 3 to 4 PD per millimetre; lateral rectus recession corrects 2 to 3 PD per mm. These are approximations — surgeon-specific and patient-specific dose tables refine these estimates. For very large angles, combined procedures on multiple muscles are needed. The pre-operative measurements must be stable over at least two sessions to be reliable for planning.

Types of Procedures

Bilateral recession (symmetric weakening): used for comitant strabismus where the deviation is equal in both eyes — standard approach for infantile esotropia (bilateral MR recession) and divergence excess exotropia (bilateral LR recession). Recession-resection on one eye: used for smaller angles or when a monocular approach is preferred; weakens the dominant muscle and strengthens its antagonist on the same eye. Oblique procedures: IO weakening (myectomy, recession, or anterior transposition) for IO overaction and V patterns; SO tuck or harada-ito for SO palsy with torsional diplopia.

Adjustable Sutures

Indicated in: thyroid eye disease (unpredictable response), longstanding paralytic strabismus with contracture, re-operations after previous surgery, cases where the pre-operative deviation is unstable. The muscle is attached with a slip-knot at surgery. Postoperatively (same day or next morning), under topical anaesthesia, the alignment is assessed and the suture adjusted to advance or recess the muscle further. Not suitable for children or uncooperative adults. Shown to improve surgical accuracy in complex cases.

Complications

Slipped/lost muscle: muscle retracts behind Tenon's capsule — emergency re-exploration needed. Globe perforation: needle penetrates sclera during muscle reattachment — examine for retinal breaks; higher risk in high myopes. Anterior segment ischaemia: if more than two recti disinserted from one eye — compromises anterior ciliary artery supply; presents as corneal oedema, severe iritis, hypotony. Undercorrection and overcorrection: most common; may need re-operation. Conjunctival scarring and chemosis.

AIIMS Nov 2019 A 2-year-old child presents with an esotropia of 40 prism dioptres. Cycloplegic refraction reveals +3.50 D hypermetropia. Glasses are prescribed and worn for 8 weeks. The deviation decreases to 15 PD at distance and 20 PD at near. What is the next step? AIIMS PG
Answer: Surgery for the residual non-accommodative component — bilateral medial rectus recession for the residual deviation

This is partially accommodative esotropia. The glasses have corrected the accommodative component (40 to 15 PD at distance), but a significant residual deviation of 15 PD at distance and 20 PD at near persists. This residual non-accommodative angle is an indication for surgery. Bilateral medial rectus recession is performed for the residual distance deviation. The near excess (5 PD more at near than distance) may reflect a mild high AC/A component and can be addressed with bifocal consideration. The glasses must continue to be worn after surgery to maintain the accommodative correction.

NEET PG 2020 Which clinical test is positive in superior oblique palsy? NEET PG
Answer: Bielschowsky head tilt test

The Bielschowsky head tilt test is positive in superior oblique palsy. When the head is tilted toward the shoulder on the side of the palsy (ipsilateral tilt), the hypertropia of the affected eye worsens. This is because tilting toward the affected side creates a stimulus for intorsion of that eye. With the SO paralysed, the only remaining intortor is the superior rectus — and because the superior rectus also elevates the eye, the hyptertropia increases. Contralateral head tilt compensates and is the habitual posture these patients adopt. The three steps of the Parks-Bielschowsky test identify the affected eye: (1) which eye is hypertropic in primary position, (2) which gaze direction worsens the hypertropia, (3) which head tilt worsens it.

DNB Dec 2018 What is the most common type of exotropia in children? DNB
Answer: Intermittent exotropia

Intermittent exotropia is by far the most common form of exotropia in children. The eyes are straight under normal binocular viewing conditions and in bright lighting, but the deviation breaks down at distance, when the child is tired or unwell, and in monocular suppression conditions. The child typically squints or closes one eye in bright sunlight — a pathognomonic clinical history. Bifoveal fusion maintains alignment during near tasks. Management is conservative initially (part-time patching, convergence exercises), with surgery reserved for frequent breakdowns or a cosmetically unacceptable deviation.

AIIMS May 2021 In Hess chart, the smaller field belongs to which eye? AIIMS PG
Answer: The affected (paretic) eye

The smaller (contracted) chart on the Hess chart always belongs to the affected eye. The paretic muscle fails to move the eye to the correct position in its field of action, so the plotted points fall short of the outer grid boundary, making the polygon smaller in that direction. By Hering's law, the yoke muscle in the fellow eye receives excess innervation (to compensate for the paretic muscle's underaction), causing it to overact — this produces the expanded (larger) chart in the fellow eye. The direction of maximum contraction on the smaller chart identifies the paretic muscle. This is the fundamental rule for all Hess chart interpretation.

NEET PG 2022 A child closes one eye in bright sunlight and otherwise appears to have normal eye alignment. What is the likely diagnosis? NEET PG
Answer: Intermittent exotropia

Closing one eye in bright sunlight is a classic and highly characteristic complaint of intermittent exotropia. The reason is not fully established but is thought to be related to the loss of binocular suppression in bright conditions — the child closes one eye to avoid diplopia rather than allowing the deviation to manifest. The eye alignment appears normal in the clinic because the child maintains fusion actively. The diagnosis is confirmed by a cover test at distance (preferably outdoors or in a large room with a distant fixation target) which reveals the exotropia. This history alone should prompt a cover test and referral for orthoptic assessment if the deviation is confirmed.

University Final MBBS 2021 Describe the cover test and how it distinguishes a phoria from a tropia. (5 marks) University
Answer: Cover-uncover test detects tropia; alternate cover test detects full deviation including phoria

Cover-Uncover Test: Cover one eye and watch the other. If the uncovered eye moves to fixate, a tropia is present in the uncovered eye. The direction of refixation indicates deviation type (inward movement = exotropia was present; outward movement = esotropia). Then uncover and watch the formerly covered eye — if it drifts and then recovers, a phoria is present in that eye. This step detects the latent deviation that fusion was controlling.

Alternate Cover Test: Move the cover briskly from eye to eye without allowing binocular fusion. This reveals the maximum possible deviation — both the manifest (tropia) and latent (phoria) components. The movement seen is larger than in the cover-uncover test because fusion is not allowed to recover.

Key distinction: If the cover-uncover test is negative but the alternate cover test shows movement, the patient has a phoria only — the deviation is controlled by fusion under binocular conditions. If the cover-uncover test is positive, a tropia is present — the eyes deviate even with both eyes open. Combining both tests gives a complete picture of the binocular status.
