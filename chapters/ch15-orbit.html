Chapter 15

Diseases of the Orbit

Proptosis, thyroid eye disease, orbital cellulitis, orbital tumours, and cavernous sinus thrombosis — the orbit as a diagnostic and surgical challenge at every level of training.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Five cards covering the highest-yield orbital topics. Thyroid eye disease, Chandler's classification, and the tumour prevalence lists appear across UG spotters and PG entrance examinations.

Proptosis — Axial vs Non-Axial

Proptosis is forward displacement of the globe, measured with a Hertel exophthalmometer. Normal range is approximately 12–16 mm; a reading of 18 mm or more, or an asymmetry of more than 2 mm between eyes, is abnormal. The direction matters clinically: axial proptosis (straight forward) indicates a lesion within the muscle cone (intraconal), typically an optic nerve tumour or cavernous haemangioma. Non-axial proptosis (globe displaced sideways, up, or down) indicates a lesion outside the muscle cone — lacrimal gland tumour, sinus mucocele, or lymphoma.

Thyroid Eye Disease — Key Points

The most common cause of both unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults. Driven by TSH receptor antibodies causing T-cell and B-cell infiltration of orbital fat and muscles. The inferior rectus is the most commonly involved muscle. Classic signs: Dalrymple's (upper lid retraction — stare), von Graefe's (lid lag on downgaze), Collier's (lid retraction on downgaze). VISA classification grades severity: Vision, Inflammation, Strabismus, Appearance. The CAS (Clinical Activity Score) guides whether active treatment is needed. Sequential surgery in inactive disease: decompression first, then strabismus, then eyelid surgery.

Chandler's Classification

Orbital cellulitis most commonly arises from ethmoid sinusitis in children. Chandler's five stages describe increasing severity: Stage I (preseptal/periorbital cellulitis — lid oedema, no proptosis); Stage II (orbital cellulitis — proptosis, early EOM restriction); Stage III (subperiosteal abscess — purulent collection under periosteum); Stage IV (orbital abscess — severe proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, vision at risk); Stage V (cavernous sinus thrombosis — bilateral signs, fever, altered mental status, medical emergency). Stages III–V need surgical drainage in addition to IV antibiotics.

Orbital Tumours — Who Gets What

In children: the most common benign orbital tumour is a capillary haemangioma (may regress spontaneously with age; propranolol accelerates regression). The most common primary malignant orbital tumour is rhabdomyosarcoma — rapid proptosis in a child is rhabdomyosarcoma until proven otherwise. In adults: the most common benign orbital tumour is a cavernous haemangioma (slow-growing, well-circumscribed, intraconal). The most common malignant orbital tumour in adults is metastatic carcinoma — from breast, lung, or prostate.

Orbital Anatomy & Proptosis

Orbital Structure

The orbit is a pear-shaped bony cavity with its apex pointing posteromedially toward the optic canal and superior orbital fissure. The four walls are formed by: the roof (orbital plate of the frontal bone), the floor (orbital plate of the maxilla — the thinnest wall, most susceptible to blowout fractures), the medial wall (ethmoid bone — also thin, and explains why ethmoid sinusitis spreads so readily into the orbit), and the lateral wall (zygomatic and greater wing of sphenoid — the strongest wall). The orbital volume is approximately 30 mL, and even a small increase in orbital contents (as little as 4 mL) can produce significant proptosis.

Proptosis Classification

The Hertel exophthalmometer measures the anterior projection of the corneal apex relative to the lateral orbital rim. Readings above 18 mm or an asymmetry of more than 2 mm between the two eyes are considered abnormal. The direction of the proptosis provides the first localisation clue. Axial displacement — straight forward — almost always indicates a lesion within the muscle cone (intraconal), because the extraocular muscles constrain the globe to move only forward when a mass expands symmetrically from within. Non-axial displacement indicates a lesion outside the muscle cone (extraconal) that pushes the globe in the opposite direction.

A useful way to remember the ethmoid sinusitis to orbital cellulitis route: the medial orbital wall (lamina papyracea of the ethmoid) is wafer-thin. Infection needs to do almost nothing to cross from sinus to orbit. This anatomical vulnerability is why ethmoid sinusitis is the most common source of orbital cellulitis in children.

Thyroid Eye Disease

Pathophysiology

TED is an autoimmune condition driven by antibodies against the TSH receptor (TRAb) and the IGF-1 receptor expressed on orbital fibroblasts and muscle cells. When these receptors are activated, fibroblasts differentiate into glycosaminoglycan-producing myofibroblasts, which expand the orbital fat volume. T-lymphocytes infiltrate the extraocular muscles and cause myositis, leading to inflammation, oedema, and eventual fibrosis. The inferior rectus is involved most frequently (causing upgaze restriction and diplopia), followed by the medial rectus. The resulting restrictive strabismus in TED is mechanically different from neurogenic causes — the muscles are not paralysed but are scarred and tethered.

VISA Classification

The VISA classification provides a structured framework for assessing disease severity. Vision encompasses visual acuity, visual field, and any evidence of dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) — the most dangerous complication, where the optic nerve is compressed by swollen muscles at the orbital apex. Inflammation is scored using the Clinical Activity Score (CAS, 0–7); a score of 3 or more indicates active disease requiring medical immunosuppression rather than surgery. Strabismus describes the degree of diplopia and restriction of extraocular movements. Appearance covers the cosmetically significant features: lid retraction, proptosis, lagophthalmos, and corneal exposure.

Management: Active vs Inactive

During the active phase, the goal is to suppress inflammation and prevent permanent damage. High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (500 mg weekly for six weeks, then 250 mg weekly for six weeks — the EUGOGO protocol) are first-line. Oral prednisolone is an alternative but carries more systemic side effects. Rituximab (anti-CD20) and teprotumumab (anti-IGF-1R) represent newer targeted approaches — teprotumumab is particularly effective at reducing proptosis in active disease. Selenium supplementation (200 µg daily) has been shown to reduce progression in mild active TED. During the inactive phase (CAS below 3, disease stable for at least 6 months), sequential surgical rehabilitation can begin: orbital decompression first (for proptosis or threatened optic nerve), strabismus surgery second (once proptosis is corrected, the motility pattern may shift), and eyelid surgery last (since lid retraction is affected by proptosis and strabismus correction).

Dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) is a sight-threatening emergency. The presentation is often surprisingly subtle — a patient with TED who reports a new or progressive visual field defect, colour desaturation, or a decrease in visual acuity needs urgent assessment and often emergency orbital decompression or high-dose IV steroids, not a routine follow-up appointment.

The Five Chandler Stages

Chandler's classification describes the anatomical progression of infection from the sinuses into the orbit and ultimately into the intracranial compartment. Stage I is preseptal (periorbital) cellulitis — the infection is confined anterior to the orbital septum. The lid is oedematous and erythematous, but the globe is not involved: EOM is full, vision is normal, and proptosis is absent. Stage II is true orbital cellulitis — infection has crossed the orbital septum. Proptosis develops, eye movements become restricted and painful, and vision may begin to deteriorate. Stage III is a subperiosteal abscess, where pus collects between the periosteum and the bony orbital wall, typically medially from the ethmoid. Stage IV is an orbital abscess with frank pus in the orbital fat, causing severe proptosis, complete ophthalmoplegia, and serious vision loss. Stage V is cavernous sinus thrombosis — septic thrombophlebitis has spread intracranially, producing bilateral signs, high fever, and altered consciousness. This is the most dangerous stage, with mortality that remains significant even with optimal treatment.

Management

Stage I can often be managed with oral antibiotics (in mild cases) or IV antibiotics and close observation. Stages II–IV require high-dose intravenous antibiotics — a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone) combined with vancomycin for MRSA cover, and metronidazole if an anaerobic source is suspected. CT scanning is essential to delineate the extent of involvement and detect any abscess. Stages III and IV require surgical drainage — endoscopic sinus surgery for medial subperiosteal abscesses, or external drainage for lateral orbital abscesses. Stage V (CST) requires the same antibiotic regimen with the addition of anticoagulation (heparin) to limit thrombus propagation, a decision that remains somewhat controversial but is widely practised.

Orbital Tumours

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Capillary Haemangioma (Children)

The most common benign orbital tumour in children, usually appearing in the first weeks of life, growing rapidly over the first year, and then slowly involuting over the subsequent years. It may be superficial (strawberry naevus) or deep within the orbit, causing proptosis. Amblyopia is the main functional risk — either from induced astigmatism if the mass distorts the globe, or from occlusion of the visual axis. Management options include observation (most involute by age 7), topical or oral propranolol (dramatically accelerates involution), systemic steroids, and rarely surgical debulking for large vision-threatening lesions.

Rhabdomyosarcoma (Children)

The most common primary malignant orbital tumour in children. It arises from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells (not differentiated rhabdomyoblasts) and presents with a rapid, typically painless proptosis developing over days to weeks in a child, often without a preceding history of infection or trauma. The embryonal subtype is most common and carries the best prognosis; the alveolar subtype is least common but most aggressive. Diagnosis requires biopsy. Treatment is multimodal: combination chemotherapy (vincristine, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide) and radiotherapy form the backbone; surgery is reserved for cases with poor chemotherapy response.

Cavernous Haemangioma (Adults)

The most common benign orbital tumour in adults, typically presenting in middle-aged women as a slowly progressive, axial proptosis with well-preserved visual function. On CT, the lesion is well-circumscribed, homogeneous, intraconal, and often located just behind the globe. Dynamic CT or MRI shows progressive enhancement over time (filling in from the periphery). Management is observation for small asymptomatic lesions; surgical excision for those causing significant proptosis, vision impairment, or discomfort.

Optic Nerve Glioma and Meningioma

Optic nerve gliomas are low-grade astrocytomas associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) in about 30% of cases. They cause a slowly progressive proptosis with relative preservation of visual acuity, and on MRI appear as a fusiform enlargement of the optic nerve without optic nerve sheath involvement. Most are managed conservatively with serial imaging and visual monitoring; treatment is reserved for progressive visual deterioration and consists of chemotherapy rather than surgery, which risks optic nerve injury. Optic nerve sheath meningiomas arise from the meningothelial cells of the nerve sheath and are distinguished from glioma by their CT appearance — the "tram track sign" (bilateral calcification along the nerve sheath with a clear canal in between) is pathognomonic on CT. They compress rather than expand the nerve, causing progressive visual loss with an afferent pupillary defect.

Tumour prevalence — quick recall
Children benign: Capillary haemangioma. Children malignant: Rhabdomyosarcoma. Adults benign: Cavernous haemangioma. Adults malignant: Metastatic carcinoma. Special: Optic nerve glioma (NF1), Optic nerve sheath meningioma (tram track sign).

Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis (CST)

CST represents the intracranial extension of orbital infection (Chandler Stage V). Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative organism. The cavernous sinus receives venous drainage from the orbit, the face, and the sinuses, and the absence of valves in these emissary veins means infection can spread bidirectionally. Within the cavernous sinus run the oculomotor nerve (CN III), the trochlear nerve (CN IV), both divisions of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal (V1 and V2), the abducens nerve (CN VI), and the internal carotid artery — involvement of all these structures produces a characteristic complete ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, and forehead anaesthesia. The bilateral presentation distinguishes CST from an isolated orbital abscess — once one cavernous sinus is thrombosed, the thrombus readily extends to the contralateral side through the intercavernous sinuses.

Orbital Pseudotumour (Idiopathic Orbital Inflammation)

Orbital pseudotumour is a diagnosis of exclusion — it describes a non-specific, non-infectious, non-granulomatous inflammatory infiltrate of the orbit that mimics a neoplasm. It is the most common cause of a painful orbital mass in adults. Presentations vary depending on the structure involved: when the muscles are affected, it is called myositis (presents like thyroid eye disease but is painful and typically unilateral); when the lacrimal gland is involved, it produces a painful, enlarged lacrimal gland; when the entire orbital fat is involved, it causes diffuse proptosis with pain. Before accepting a diagnosis of pseudotumour, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, and IgG4-related disease must be considered. The hallmark of pseudotumour is its dramatic and rapid response to systemic corticosteroids — significant improvement within 24–48 hours of starting high-dose prednisolone is strongly supportive of the diagnosis. Failure to respond should prompt biopsy.

The rapid steroid response of orbital pseudotumour is helpful diagnostically but can be misleading — orbital lymphoma also responds to steroids temporarily. Any mass that recurs after steroid taper, is atypical in presentation, or fails to respond promptly should be biopsied to exclude lymphoma.

Three practical skills for evaluating orbital disease. Accurate examination of proptosis and systematic assessment of thyroid eye disease activity are examinable skills that require structured practice.

Examining a Patient with Proptosis
Clinical Examination
1
Confirm proptosis and measure it

Stand behind the patient, look from above, and compare the prominence of the two globes — subtle unilateral proptosis is often easier to detect this way than from the front. Use a Hertel exophthalmometer, place the concave notches on the lateral orbital rims, and measure the anterior projection of each corneal apex. Record the base (the distance between the lateral orbital rims) and both readings. A difference of more than 2 mm, or either eye measuring above 20 mm, warrants investigation.

If you have no exophthalmometer, a simple ruler held horizontally against the lateral orbital rims while the patient looks up gives a rough measure. Or look from above — the more proptotic eye will appear more prominent.
2
Determine the direction of displacement

Note whether the globe is displaced straight forward (axial — intraconal lesion) or pushed in a specific direction: inferior displacement from a lacrimal gland tumour at the superolateral aspect; medial displacement from a lateral wall lesion; and so on. The direction of displacement points to the quadrant opposite the tumour.

3
Assess reducibility and pulsation

Gently push the globe backward into the orbit with a finger and assess whether it reduces easily (reducible — suggests a soft lesion, low venous pressure) or resists reduction (non-reducible — suggests a firm or infiltrative mass). Pulsating proptosis without a bruit suggests a vascular connection — typically a carotid-cavernous fistula or an absent orbital roof allowing transmission of intracranial pulsations. Pulsating proptosis with a bruit is essentially diagnostic of a carotid-cavernous fistula. Auscultate over the closed eyelid with the stethoscope — you can often hear the bruit clearly.

4
Examine for regional lymphadenopathy and systemic signs

Palpate preauricular, submandibular, and cervical lymph nodes. In orbital lymphoma, systemic lymphadenopathy is a major clue. Ask about thyroid symptoms, and examine the thyroid if TED is suspected. A full cranial nerve examination — including fundoscopy for disc swelling or optic nerve pallor — completes the orbital evaluation.

Always examine the contralateral eye and orbit. TED is often bilateral even when proptosis appears unilateral. And bilateral proptosis in the context of fever and headache should immediately make you think of CST.
Assessing CAS (Clinical Activity Score) in TED
TED Assessment
1
Spontaneous orbital pain (1 point)

Ask specifically about pain behind the eye at rest — not related to eye movement. Patients often describe it as an ache or pressure. This is distinct from the pain on eye movement which is a separate CAS criterion.

2
Gaze-evoked pain (1 point), eyelid oedema, erythema, conjunctival injection, chemosis, caruncular swelling (1 point each)

These seven items (spontaneous pain, gaze pain, lid oedema, lid erythema, conjunctival injection, chemosis, caruncle swelling) each score 1 point. A total CAS of 3 or more out of 7 indicates active disease and predicts a meaningful response to immunosuppressive treatment. A CAS of 0–2 indicates inactive disease — proceeding to surgery in an active patient risks poor outcomes and worsening inflammation.

Lid erythema is often missed when examining TED patients — lift the upper lid against the brow skin and look at the lid skin rather than the conjunctiva. Redness of the lid skin itself is the criterion, not conjunctival injection.
3
Document baseline and serial CAS

Record the CAS at every visit. A declining CAS documents response to treatment and helps time the transition from medical to surgical management. The CAS often improves before the proptosis improves — the orbital fat volume changes lag behind the inflammatory changes by months to years.

Differentiating Preseptal from Orbital Cellulitis
Paediatric Emergency
1
Examine eye movement and visual acuity

This is the single most important step. In preseptal cellulitis, the globe is entirely uninvolved — the child can move their eye fully in all directions, and visual acuity is normal. In orbital cellulitis, proptosis is present, eye movements are restricted and painful, and visual acuity may be reduced. If a young child is too unwell or distressed to cooperate with formal assessment, look for fixation and following in each eye separately and whether they resist eye movement when you gently attempt to move the globe.

A child who has a very swollen, shut eye is not automatically orbital cellulitis. The lid oedema in preseptal cellulitis can be so severe that you cannot open the eye without effort. Use your thumbs to gently open the lids and assess the globe — this distinguishes the two conditions.
2
Look for proptosis

Open the swollen lids and assess the position of the globe. Proptosis establishes orbital involvement. In preseptal cellulitis, the globe is in its normal position. The lid may be enormously swollen, but looking from behind the pinna or from above the brow may reveal symmetrical globe positions.

3
CT scan and decision threshold

If there is any clinical uncertainty — any proptosis, any restriction of movement, any decline in vision — CT orbit with IV contrast is mandatory. CT will show the extent of involvement and detect a subperiosteal or orbital abscess requiring drainage. The management escalates significantly from Stage I (oral/IV antibiotics, outpatient in mild cases) to Stage II–IV (IV antibiotics, hospital admission, surgical drainage). Stage V (CST with bilateral signs) is a medical emergency requiring ICU-level care.

When in doubt in a child with peri-orbital cellulitis, err on the side of admission for IV antibiotics and close monitoring. Preseptal cellulitis that is deteriorating despite 24–48 hours of appropriate antibiotics needs imaging — it may have progressed to orbital involvement.

Question 6
Axial proptosis (straight forward displacement of the globe) most commonly indicates:
UG

Axial proptosis — displacement of the globe directly forward — occurs when the expanding mass is symmetrically positioned within the muscle cone. Classic intraconal lesions include cavernous haemangioma and optic nerve tumours. Extraconal lesions push the globe sideways (non-axial proptosis) — lacrimal gland tumours cause downward and medial displacement, sinus mucoceles cause lateral displacement.

UG

B Orbital lymphoma
C Cavernous haemangioma
D Rhabdomyosarcoma
Explanation
Cavernous haemangioma is the most common benign orbital tumour in adults. It is a well-circumscribed, slow-growing, intraconal vascular lesion that typically presents in middle-aged women with gradual painless axial proptosis. Capillary haemangioma is most common in children. Lymphoma and rhabdomyosarcoma are malignant.
Question 8
In thyroid eye disease, the sequential surgical order in the inactive phase is:
UG
A Eyelid surgery → Strabismus → Orbital decompression
B Strabismus surgery → Orbital decompression → Eyelid surgery
C Orbital decompression → Eyelid surgery → Strabismus surgery
D Orbital decompression → Strabismus surgery → Eyelid surgery
Explanation
The sequential surgical order in inactive TED is: orbital decompression first (addresses proptosis and optic nerve compression, and changes the strabismus pattern); strabismus surgery second (after the new proptosis baseline is established); eyelid surgery last (since both decompression and strabismus surgery affect lid position). Performing eyelid surgery first would require re-doing it after the proptosis changes.
Question 9
Orbital cellulitis in children most commonly arises from infection spreading from:
UG
A Maxillary sinusitis
B Ethmoid sinusitis
C Frontal sinusitis
D Sphenoid sinusitis
Explanation
Ethmoid sinusitis is the most common source of orbital cellulitis in children because the medial orbital wall (lamina papyracea of the ethmoid bone) is extremely thin, allowing infection to spread directly from the ethmoid cells into the orbit. The ethmoid sinuses are the first paranasal sinuses to develop fully in children, making them the most common site of paediatric sinusitis-related orbital complications.
Question 10

Optic nerve glioma (pilocytic astrocytoma) is associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) in approximately 30% of cases. Ten postgraduate-level questions requiring mechanistic understanding, scenario analysis, and knowledge of management nuances.

PG Question 1
A 42-year-old woman with known Graves' disease develops a new right visual field defect and reports that colours look washed out in that eye. IOP is normal. CAS is 5. The most appropriate immediate management is:
PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A Increase topical lubricants and review in 2 weeks
B Oral prednisolone 20 mg daily and routine MRI
C Urgent high-dose IV methylprednisolone and/or emergency orbital decompression
D Refer to strabismus clinic for muscle surgery
Explanation
This is dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) — a sight-threatening emergency. Visual field defect and colour desaturation indicate compression or ischaemia of the optic nerve, typically at the orbital apex by swollen muscles. With an active CAS of 5, high-dose IV methylprednisolone is the first-line intervention. If there is no rapid improvement within 48–72 hours, emergency orbital decompression (typically two-wall decompression) is required. Routine review or low-dose oral steroids are entirely inappropriate for DON.
PG Question 2
The pathophysiology of the restrictive strabismus in thyroid eye disease is different from neurogenic causes because:
PG
A The muscles are paralysed due to CN III compression
B The muscles are fibrosed and mechanically tethered, not paretic — forced duction is positive
C Intraorbital pressure elevation blocks nerve conduction
D The muscles atrophy due to TSH receptor autoantibodies
Explanation
TED produces a restrictive (not paretic) strabismus. The muscles are not paralysed — they are infiltrated by glycosaminoglycans and eventually fibrosed, creating mechanical tethering. The forced duction test is positive in restrictive strabismus (you cannot move the eye passively in the direction of limited movement). This distinguishes TED from CN palsy, where forced duction is negative. Management with prisms may help; surgical correction involves recession of the fibrosed muscle, not resection.
PG Question 3
A 7-year-old child presents with unilateral proptosis developing over 3 weeks, a firm orbital mass on palpation, and no fever. CT shows a well-defined intraconal mass. Biopsy shows embryonal spindle cells with cross-striations. The most appropriate treatment is:
PGClinical
A Immediate surgical excision of the mass
B Oral corticosteroids and observation
C Radiotherapy alone
D Combination chemotherapy (VAC) and radiotherapy
Explanation
This is orbital rhabdomyosarcoma (embryonal subtype — the most common, with cross-striations). The Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study (IRS) protocol uses VAC chemotherapy (Vincristine, Actinomycin D, Cyclophosphamide) combined with radiotherapy as the primary treatment. Surgery is not first-line — rhabdomyosarcoma is chemosensitive, and orbital exenteration is rarely required. With combination treatment, survival rates exceed 90% for localised orbital disease.
PG Question 4
The "tram track sign" on CT is pathognomonic of:
PG
A Optic nerve sheath meningioma
B Optic nerve glioma
C Retinoblastoma with optic nerve involvement
D Cavernous haemangioma of the optic nerve
Explanation
The tram track sign on CT describes bilateral calcification along the optic nerve sheath with a clear, patent optic nerve canal visible between them, resembling train tracks. This is pathognomonic of optic nerve sheath meningioma. The calcification is in the meninges, not the nerve itself. Optic nerve glioma causes fusiform expansion of the nerve itself (no sheath calcification). This CT sign is a favourite in radiology and orbital examination questions.
PG Question 5
An adult patient with known orbital pseudotumour fails to respond to 72 hours of high-dose oral prednisolone. The next most appropriate step is:
PGClinical
A Increase the steroid dose and wait 2 more weeks
B Add topical steroids
C Orbital biopsy to exclude lymphoma
D IV ceftriaxone in case this is orbital cellulitis
Explanation
Orbital pseudotumour typically responds dramatically to steroids within 24–48 hours. Failure to respond promptly is a red flag that should prompt biopsy. The most important diagnosis to exclude is orbital lymphoma, which also responds transiently to steroids but will ultimately progress and requires specific treatment. IgG4-related disease and sarcoidosis can also mimic pseudotumour. Continuing to increase steroids without a tissue diagnosis risks missing a treatable malignancy.
PG Question 6
In cavernous sinus thrombosis, all of the following cranial nerves are typically involved EXCEPT:
PG
A CN III (oculomotor)
B CN VII (facial nerve)
C CN V1 (ophthalmic branch of trigeminal)
D CN VI (abducens)
Explanation
The cranial nerves within the cavernous sinus are CN III, CN IV, CN V1, CN V2, and CN VI. The facial nerve (CN VII) runs through the internal auditory meatus and the facial canal — it is NOT in the cavernous sinus. CST therefore produces ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, and forehead/upper face sensory loss, but facial motor function is preserved. This helps distinguish CST from other conditions causing multiple cranial nerve palsies.
PG Question 7
Teprotumumab is used in the treatment of active TED. Its mechanism of action is:
PG
A Anti-VEGF inhibiting orbital neovascularisation
B Anti-CD20 depleting B lymphocytes in orbital tissue
C TSH receptor antagonism blocking orbital fibroblast stimulation
D Anti-IGF-1R (insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor) inhibition
Explanation
Teprotumumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R), which is co-expressed with the TSH receptor on orbital fibroblasts and implicated in the pathogenesis of TED. By blocking IGF-1R signalling, teprotumumab reduces fibroblast activation, glycosaminoglycan production, and orbital tissue expansion. Clinical trials showed dramatic reduction in proptosis (more than any previous medical therapy) and CAS. Rituximab targets CD20 (B-cell depletion) and is an alternative second-line agent.

D The rebound inflammation after steroid withdrawal
Explanation
Rundle's curve describes the natural history of TED activity over time — disease activity increases, peaks around 18–24 months after onset, then plateaus and eventually stabilises at a lower level. This is clinically important because it explains why surgery should wait for the plateau: operating during the active phase risks worsening inflammation and unpredictable outcome. Medical immunosuppression is timed to the active phase; surgical rehabilitation follows once the curve has flattened.
PG Question 9
A Hertel reading of 22 mm in the right eye and 15 mm in the left eye is found in a patient presenting to clinic. The significance of the asymmetry is:
PG
A Normal — asymmetry of up to 5 mm is within normal limits
B Mildly abnormal — needs only observation
C Significantly abnormal — asymmetry of more than 2 mm warrants investigation for orbital pathology
D Could be normal if both eyes had prior surgery
Explanation
An asymmetry of more than 2 mm between the two eyes on Hertel exophthalmometry is considered abnormal and warrants investigation for an orbital lesion. In this case the difference is 7 mm — significantly abnormal. The right eye at 22 mm also exceeds the upper normal limit of approximately 18–20 mm. Both the absolute value and the asymmetry are important diagnostic parameters. This patient needs CT or MRI of the orbits.
PG Question 10
In a child with capillary haemangioma causing visual axis obstruction and amblyopia risk, the preferred current medical treatment to accelerate involution is:
PG
A Systemic corticosteroids
B Oral propranolol
C Pulsed dye laser
D Surgical excision immediately
Explanation
Propranolol (a non-selective beta-blocker) has become the first-line medical treatment for significant capillary haemangiomas, particularly those threatening vision or causing amblyopia. Its mechanism involves vasoconstriction, inhibition of angiogenic factors (VEGF, bFGF), and induction of apoptosis in haemangioma endothelial cells. Systemic steroids were the previous standard but are now largely replaced by propranolol due to its better safety profile. Topical timolol 0.5% can be used for superficial lesions.

Three long-answer questions structured with scoring breakdown for examination preparation.

Long Answer 1
Describe the clinical features, classification (VISA), and management of thyroid eye disease.
10 Marks
Pathophysiology and Epidemiology (2 marks)

TED is an autoimmune condition — TSH receptor antibodies and IGF-1R antibodies activate orbital fibroblasts, leading to glycosaminoglycan deposition and muscle infiltration. Most common cause of unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults. Associated with Graves' disease (hyperthyroid) but can occur with euthyroid or hypothyroid states. Female predominance; smoking is a major modifiable risk factor.

Clinical Signs (2 marks)

Dalrymple's (lid retraction on primary gaze), von Graefe's (lid lag on downgaze), Collier's (lid retraction on downgaze). Proptosis (measured with Hertel), lagophthalmos, chemosis, corneal exposure. Restrictive strabismus (inferior rectus most common — upgaze limitation, diplopia). Dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) — visual field defect, colour desaturation, RAPD.

VISA Classification (2 marks)

Vision (VA, VF, colour, DON), Inflammation (CAS 0–7; CAS ≥3 = active), Strabismus (degrees of restricted motility), Appearance (proptosis, lid retraction, soft tissue changes).

Management (4 marks)

Active phase (CAS ≥3): IV methylprednisolone pulses (EUGOGO protocol — 500 mg ×6 then 250 mg ×6 weeks); orbital radiotherapy as adjunct; selenium 200 µg for mild disease. Teprotumumab (anti-IGF-1R) for active proptosis-dominant disease. Inactive phase: sequential surgery — decompression → strabismus → eyelid. DON: emergency IV steroids and/or decompression. Smoking cessation mandatory throughout.

Long Answer 2
Describe Chandler's classification of orbital cellulitis and outline the management of each stage.
10 Marks
Source and Anatomy (2 marks)

Ethmoid sinusitis is the most common source in children (thin lamina papyracea allows easy spread). Any acute sinusitis can be a source. Orbital cellulitis is a medical and sometimes surgical emergency with risk of vision loss and intracranial spread.

Chandler's Five Stages (5 marks)

Stage I (Periorbital/Preseptal): anterior to septum, lid oedema, no proptosis, normal EOM — oral antibiotics, close observation. Stage II (Orbital Cellulitis): posterior to septum, proptosis, painful EOM restriction, no abscess — IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + vancomycin), CT scan. Stage III (Subperiosteal Abscess): pus under periosteum (usually medial), increasing proptosis — IV antibiotics + endoscopic or external drainage. Stage IV (Orbital Abscess): frank orbital pus, severe proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, vision at risk — urgent surgical drainage. Stage V (Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis): bilateral signs, fever, altered consciousness — IV antibiotics + anticoagulation (heparin), intensive care, treat source.

Complications and Prognosis (3 marks)

Vision loss from optic neuropathy, central retinal artery occlusion, or choroidal ischaemia. Intracranial extension: meningitis, brain abscess. CST mortality approximately 10–15% despite treatment. Early aggressive management with IV antibiotics and timely surgery is vision and life-saving.

Long Answer 3
Compare orbital tumours in children and adults under the headings: type, clinical presentation, imaging, and management.
10 Marks
Children — Benign (2 marks)

Capillary haemangioma (most common benign): appears in first weeks of life, grows rapidly, involutes spontaneously by age 7. Superficial (strawberry naevus) or deep (orbital proptosis). Risk: amblyopia from astigmatism or visual axis occlusion. Treatment: propranolol (first-line), systemic steroids, rarely surgery.

Children — Malignant (3 marks)

Rhabdomyosarcoma (most common primary malignant): rapid painless proptosis in a child, no fever. Biopsy shows embryonal (most common) or alveolar (worst prognosis) subtypes. CT: irregular, poorly defined mass. Treatment: VAC chemotherapy + radiotherapy; exenteration rarely needed. Survival >90% for localised orbital disease.

Adults — Benign (2 marks)

Cavernous haemangioma (most common benign): slow-growing, well-circumscribed intraconal lesion; middle-aged women; axial proptosis; progressive enhancement on dynamic CT. Treatment: observation or excision if symptomatic.

Adults — Malignant (2 marks)

Metastatic carcinoma (most common malignant): breast, lung, prostate. Rapidly progressive proptosis in older adult; often painful; may cause enophthalmos (scirrhous breast cancer). Orbital lymphoma: B-cell, often bilateral, may be primary or secondary to systemic lymphoma. Treatment: radiotherapy (lymphoma), systemic oncology management (metastases).

Special Entities (1 mark)

Optic nerve glioma (NF1 association, observe unless vision deteriorating). Optic nerve sheath meningioma (tram track sign on CT, progressive visual loss, treat with fractionated radiotherapy).

Five questions from postgraduate and undergraduate entrance examinations with brief model answers.

AIIMS PG 2021
Which is the most common cause of proptosis in adults — unilateral or bilateral?
AIIMS PG

Thyroid eye disease (TED / Graves' ophthalmopathy) is the most common cause of both unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults. This is a frequently tested fact. Note: TED can be asymmetric and appear unilateral even though both orbits are affected. In children, the answer changes — capillary haemangioma (benign) and rhabdomyosarcoma (malignant) are the most common. Answer: Thyroid eye disease.

NEET PG 2022
Which sign in TED indicates lag of the upper lid on downgaze?
NEET PG

Von Graefe's sign is the lag of the upper lid on downgaze — the upper lid descends more slowly than the globe. Dalrymple's sign is lid retraction on primary gaze. Collier's sign is lid retraction on downgaze. These three signs are consistently mixed up in examination MCQs — the key is von Graefe = lid lags behind on downgaze. Answer: Von Graefe's sign.

DNB 2021
A 5-year-old child has rapidly progressive unilateral proptosis over 2 weeks without fever. What is the most likely diagnosis?
DNB

Rhabdomyosarcoma. The key features pointing to this diagnosis are: child (under 20 years), rapidly progressive proptosis (weeks), absence of fever (distinguishes from orbital cellulitis), and no preceding trauma. Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common primary malignant orbital tumour in children and its typical presentation is exactly this scenario. The diagnosis requires biopsy. Answer: Rhabdomyosarcoma.

PGI 2022
Bilateral proptosis, fever, complete ophthalmoplegia, and altered consciousness following orbital cellulitis represents which Chandler stage?
PGI

This is Chandler Stage IV — Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis. The bilateral presentation is the critical clue: orbital cellulitis is unilateral, but once infection reaches the cavernous sinus, the thrombus can extend through the intercavernous sinuses to the contralateral side, producing bilateral proptosis and ophthalmoplegia. The fever and altered consciousness complete the picture of septic CST. This is a medical emergency with significant mortality. Answer: Chandler Stage IV / Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis.

University Final MBBS
Enumerate the signs of thyroid eye disease. Describe the sequential surgical management. (5 marks)
University

Signs of TED: Dalrymple's (lid retraction on primary gaze), von Graefe's (lid lag on downgaze), Collier's (lid retraction on downgaze), Rundle's curve (natural history), proptosis (measured with Hertel), lagophthalmos, chemosis, conjunctival oedema, restrictive strabismus (IR most common — upgaze limitation), and dysthyroid optic neuropathy (the most dangerous complication). Sequential surgical management in the inactive phase: (1) Orbital decompression first — addresses proptosis and optic nerve compression; (2) Strabismus surgery second — after the proptosis has been corrected, as decompression changes the strabismus pattern; (3) Eyelid surgery last — lid position depends on both proptosis and motility. Operating out of this sequence leads to unpredictable results.
