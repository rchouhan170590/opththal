Chapter 15

Diseases of the Orbit

Proptosis, thyroid eye disease, orbital cellulitis, tumours, cavernous sinus thrombosis — diagnosis and management in orbital pathology.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield

Orbital Anatomy & Proptosis Definition

UG
▼

Orbit Overview

  • Shape: Cone-shaped cavity; apex medial (optic foramen, superior orbital fissure)
  • Volume: ~30 mL; limited space → increases crowding with masses
  • Four walls: Roof (frontal), floor (maxilla), lateral (sphenoid, zygomatic), medial (ethmoid)
  • Contents: Eyeball, muscles, nerves, vessels, fat, lacrimal gland

Proptosis (Exophthalmos)

  • Definition: Forward displacement of globe; exophthalmos ≥18 mm (Hertel exophthalmometer)
  • Measurement: Hertel exophthalmometer; normal ~12–16 mm; asymmetry >2 mm = abnormal
  • Classification:
    • Axial: Straight forward; lesion within muscle cone (optic nerve tumours, cavernous haemangioma)
    • Non-axial: Displaced (lateral, medial, up, down); lesion outside muscle cone (lacrimal gland, sinus mucocele, lymphoma)
Key Exam Point: Axial proptosis suggests retrobulbar pathology; non-axial suggests anterior or external orbital lesion.

Thyroid Eye Disease (TED) / Graves' Ophthalmopathy

UGPG
▼

Epidemiology & Pathophysiology

  • Most common cause of unilateral AND bilateral proptosis in adults
  • Autoimmune: TSH receptor antibodies → T-cell, B-cell infiltration of muscles & fat → inflammation, edema, fibrosis
  • Inferior rectus most commonly involved (followed by medial rectus)

VISA Classification (Severity)

  • Vision — VF defects, diplopia, optic neuropathy
  • Inflammation — signs of active inflammation
  • Strabismus — restricted EOM, diplopia
  • Appearance — lid retraction, proptosis, lid lag

Clinical Signs

  • Dalrymple's sign: Upper lid retraction (wide palpebral fissure) = "stare"
  • von Graefe's sign: Slower descent of upper lid on downgaze (lid lag)
  • Collier's sign: Retraction of upper lid on downgaze (sympathetic overactivity)
  • Rundle's curve: Disease activity waxes and wanes; peaks around 18–24 months then stabilizes

Management Stages

  • Active phase (inflammation present): High-dose oral prednisolone (~40–60 mg/day tapered) OR IV methylprednisolone pulses; Rituximab (anti-CD20 B-cell) — newer agent
  • Inactive phase (stable, no inflammation):Sequential surgery:
    • Orbital decompression (if severe proptosis/optic neuropathy)
    • Strabismus surgery (correct restrictive motility)
    • Eyelid surgery (levator resection, lid retraction repair)
  • Adjuvant: Teprotumumab (IGF-1 receptor inhibitor) — reduces proptosis and improves symptoms in active TED

CAS (Clinical Activity Score)

  • Grades 0–7; ≥3 = active inflammation; guides treatment intensity

Orbital Cellulitis — Chandler's Classification

UGPG
▼

Chandler's Staging (Severity Progression)

  • Stage I: Periorbital cellulitis (preseptal)
    • Infection anterior to orbital septum; lid edema, chemosis
    • Vision normal; pupil normal; EOM normal
  • Stage II: Orbital cellulitis with subperiosteal abscess
    • Infection posterior to septum but outside periosteum
    • Proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, reduced vision beginning
  • Stage III: Orbital abscess
    • Purulent collection within orbit
    • Severe proptosis, complete ophthalmoplegia, vision loss
    • Requires drainage
  • Stage IV: Cavernous sinus thrombosis (CST)
    • Bilateral proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, fever, altered mental status
    • Medical emergency; high mortality
  • Stage V: Intracranial extension (meningitis, brain abscess)

Common Cause in Children

  • Ethmoid sinusitis (most common — anatomically close to orbit)

Treatment

  • IV antibiotics: 3rd-generation cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone) + Vancomycin (MRSA coverage) for 10–14 days
  • Imaging: CT/MRI to delineate extent
  • Surgical drainage: If abscess (Stage III) or sinusitis source
️ Distinguish: Preseptal (anterior) cellulitis is less urgent. Orbital cellulitis (posterior to septum) is medical emergency with risk of vision loss and intracranial complications.

Orbital Tumours — Benign & Malignant

PG
▼

Children (Primary Tumours)

  • Capillary haemangioma — Most common BENIGN
    • Superficial or deep; may present with proptosis, astigmatism
    • Can regress spontaneously; treatment: observation, steroids, propranolol
  • Rhabdomyosarcoma — Most common PRIMARY MALIGNANT in children
    • Embryonal (spindle/botryoid) most common; alveolar = worst prognosis
    • Presents with rapid proptosis, EOM restriction
    • Treatment: Chemotherapy + radiotherapy ± surgery

Adults

  • Cavernous haemangioma — Most common BENIGN in adults
    • Well-circumscribed, deep orbital; slow-growing
    • Treatment: Observation or surgical excision if symptomatic
  • Metastatic carcinoma — Most common MALIGNANT in adults
    • From lung, breast, prostate (in men); lacrimal gland region
  • Lymphoma (orbital): B-cell; can be primary or secondary; often bilateral

Special Entities

  • Optic nerve glioma: Benign; associated with NF1 (~30% of NF1); often asymptomatic; vision usually preserved
  • Optic nerve sheath meningioma: Benign; hyperostotic bone reaction; "tram track sign" on CT (calcification along optic nerve)

Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis (CST)

PG
▼

Pathophysiology

  • Septic thrombosis: Usually Staphylococcus aureus (most common), Streptococcus, Gram-negatives from sinuses, ear, face
  • Non-septic: Thromboembolism, vasculitis (rare)

Clinical Features (Medical Emergency)

  • Bilateral signs: Proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, ptosis
  • Systemic: High fever, headache, altered consciousness, seizures
  • CN palsies: CN III, IV, VI, V1, V2 (all involved) → complete ophthalmoplegia, sensory loss forehead/upper face

Diagnosis

  • Clinical: Bilateral presentation, fever, rapid deterioration
  • Imaging: MRI with contrast (thrombus in cavernous sinus); loss of normal flow void
  • Culture: Blood cultures, pus if source identified

Treatment (Mortality ~10–15% even with treatment)

  • Emergency IV antibiotics: High-dose IV Cephalosporin + Vancomycin + potentially Metronidazole
  • Anticoagulation: IV heparin (controversial but often used to prevent thrombus propagation)
  • Treat source: Drainage of sinusitis, abscess if present
️ Critical: CST is a medical emergency. Early recognition and aggressive IV antibiotic therapy (before culture results) can be life-saving. Mortality high even with treatment.

Other Orbital Conditions — Osteomyelitis, Pseudotumour

UG
▼

Orbital Osteomyelitis

  • From: Sinusitis, trauma, post-surgical
  • Signs: Proptosis, swelling, pain; fever
  • Imaging: CT shows bone destruction
  • Treatment: IV antibiotics, imaging guidance; surgical drainage if needed

Orbital Pseudotumour (Idiopathic Orbital Inflammation)

  • Definition: Non-specific inflammatory infiltrate; diagnosis of exclusion (rule out lymphoma, infectious, vasculitic causes)
  • Presentation: Proptosis, pain, ophthalmoplegia, diplopia; can mimic malignancy
  • Imaging: Enhancement on MRI; can involve muscles, fat, lacrimal gland
  • Treatment: Steroids (oral or IV); responds well

Sinus Mucocele

  • From: Blocked paranasal sinus (usually maxillary or ethmoid); accumulation of mucus
  • Proptosis: Gradual; non-axial displacement
  • Imaging: Large cystic mass, bony erosion
  • Treatment: Surgical marsupialization/drainage

MCQ Bank — Orbit

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
A 35-year-old woman presents with bilateral proptosis, lid retraction, diplopia (infraduction weakness), and thyroid dysfunction. What is the most likely diagnosis?
AThyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy)
BOrbital cellulitis
CCavernous sinus thrombosis
DOrbital lymphoma
Correct Answer: A — Thyroid eye disease
TED is most common cause of bilateral proptosis in adults. Presents with lid retraction (Dalrymple's sign), inferior rectus restriction (diplopia on upgaze), thyroid dysfunction. CST would have fever, systemic toxicity. Cellulitis unilateral.
Q2 UG
A 6-year-old presents with unilateral proptosis and rapid progression over 3 weeks. Imaging shows a large orbital mass. Biopsy reveals spindle cell embryonal type. What is the diagnosis?
ACapillary haemangioma
BCavernous haemangioma
CRhabdomyosarcoma
DOptic nerve glioma
Correct Answer: C — Rhabdomyosarcoma
Rhabdomyosarcoma is most common primary malignant orbital tumour in children. Rapid presentation, embryonal type most common (spindle/botryoid). Requires multimodal treatment: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, possibly surgery.
Q3 PG
A 45-year-old with orbital cellulitis from ethmoid sinusitis develops sudden severe headache, bilateral proptosis, and complete ophthalmoplegia with fever. Which stage of Chandler's classification is this?
AStage II (subperiosteal abscess)
BStage III (orbital abscess)
CStage IV (cavernous sinus thrombosis)
DStage V (intracranial extension)
Correct Answer: C — Stage IV: Cavernous sinus thrombosis
Bilateral signs (proptosis, ophthalmoplegia), severe headache, fever = CST. This is a medical emergency requiring immediate IV antibiotics and anticoagulation. Mortality ~10–15% even with treatment.
Q4 UG
A 55-year-old man with proptosis is found to have an axial globe displacement on examination. Which location of lesion is most likely?
AWithin the muscle cone (retrobulbar) — optic nerve tumour, cavernous haemangioma
BOutside muscle cone — lacrimal gland, sinus mucocele
CAnterior orbit — lid lesion, superficial
DIntraconal and extraconal equally
Correct Answer: A — Within muscle cone (intraconal)
Axial proptosis = lesion within muscle cone (intraconal). Non-axial displacement = extraconal (outside muscle cone). Helpful diagnostic clue for localization.
Q5 PG
A patient with thyroid eye disease in the active inflammatory phase is treated with high-dose prednisolone. After 6 months, inflammation has subsided. The patient now complains of persistent diplopia from restrictive IR myopathy. What is the appropriate next step in sequential surgical management?
AImmediate IR recession
BContinue medical management indefinitely
COrbital decompression first, then strabismus surgery
DStrabismus surgery (IR recession) only after orbital decompression and disease stabilization
Correct Answer: D — IR recession after stabilization
TED surgical sequence: Orbital decompression (if needed) → Strabismus surgery → Eyelid surgery. Do NOT operate during active inflammation. Wait for stable inactive phase (6+ months).
Q6 UG
A 7-year-old with fever, lid swelling, and proptosis presents from an ethmoid sinusitis source. Examination shows some restriction of eye movement but otherwise normal. What is the Chandler stage?
AStage I (periorbital cellulitis)
BStage II (subperiosteal abscess)
CStage III (orbital abscess)
DStage IV (cavernous sinus thrombosis)
Correct Answer: C — Stage III: Orbital abscess
Proptosis + EOM restriction + fever from sinus source = orbital abscess (Stage III). Requires drainage. Stage II (subperiosteal) has abscess under periosteum but less severe EOM involvement.
Q7 PG
An adult patient with orbital cellulitis has been treated with IV antibiotics. MRI shows a well-defined collection within the orbit. What is the next management step?
AContinue IV antibiotics alone for 4–6 weeks
BSurgical drainage of the abscess
CAdd oral antibiotics to IV
DTopical antibiotics
Correct Answer: B — Surgical drainage
Orbital abscess (Stage III with frank collection) requires drainage in addition to antibiotics. Imaging confirms need for intervention.
Q8 UG
Which of the following is the most common BENIGN orbital tumour in adults?
ACavernous haemangioma
BCapillary haemangioma
CRhabdomyosarcoma
DLymphoma
Correct Answer: A — Cavernous haemangioma
In adults, cavernous haemangioma is most common benign tumour (well-circumscribed, slow-growing). Capillary haemangioma is most common in children. Rhabdomyosarcoma = malignant in children.
Q9 PG
An optic nerve glioma is discovered incidentally in a 40-year-old with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Vision is 6/6 and there is no proptosis. What is the recommended management?
AImmediate surgical excision
BRadiotherapy immediately
CObservation with serial imaging and VA monitoring
DChemotherapy
Correct Answer: C — Observation
Optic nerve gliomas in NF1 are usually benign, asymptomatic, and vision-preserving. Observation with serial imaging is standard unless vision deteriorates or proptosis develops. No surgery needed unless vision threatened.
Q10 PG
A patient with cavernous sinus thrombosis is being treated with IV cephalosporin and vancomycin. Which additional agent is commonly used to prevent thrombus propagation?
AAspirin
BClopidogrel
CIV heparin
DWarfarin
Correct Answer: C — IV heparin
IV heparin is often added to high-dose antibiotics in CST (controversial but commonly used) to prevent clot propagation and recurrence. Aggressive early antibiotic therapy is critical for survival.

Discuss the management of thyroid eye disease, emphasizing the VISA classification and sequential surgical approach. (5 marks)
VISA: Vision, Inflammation, Strabismus, Appearance. Active phase = steroids/rituximab. Inactive phase = sequential surgery: decompression → strabismus → eyelid. CAS score ≥3 = active inflammation.

Proptosis & TED

  • Hertel exophthalmometer: ≥18 mm = proptosis; asymmetry >2 mm = abnormal
  • Axial: Within muscle cone (retrobulbar)
  • Non-axial: Outside muscle cone (lacrimal, sinus)
  • TED most common cause: Bilateral proptosis in adults
  • VISA: Vision, Inflammation, Strabismus, Appearance

Orbital Cellulitis (Chandler)

  • Stage I: Periorbital (preseptal) — less urgent
  • Stage II: Subperiosteal abscess — proptosis, EOM restriction starting
  • Stage III: Orbital abscess — severe proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, vision loss
  • Stage IV: CST — bilateral, fever, altered mental status (emergency)
  • Stage V: Intracranial extension
  • Ethmoid sinusitis = most common source in children

Orbital Tumours

  • Children benign: Capillary haemangioma (most common)
  • Children malignant: Rhabdomyosarcoma (most common primary; embryonal worst)
  • Adults benign: Cavernous haemangioma (most common)
  • Adults malignant: Metastatic carcinoma (most common)
  • Special: Optic nerve glioma (NF1 associated; usually asymptomatic) — observe

CST Emergency

  • Bilateral proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, fever, headache, altered mental status
  • Organism: Usually Staphylococcus aureus
  • Treatment: IV antibiotics (Cef + Vanco), IV heparin, treat source
  • Mortality: ~10–15% even with treatment
  • No time for cultures — start empiric therapy immediately
