Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) — Classification & Management

UGPG
▼

Classification: NPDR to PDR

  • Non-Proliferative DR (NPDR): No new vessels
    • Mild: Microaneurysms only
    • Moderate: Microaneurysms + ≥4 quadrants haemorrhages OR ≥2 quadrants venous beading OR ≥1 quadrant IRMA
    • Severe:4-2-1 rule: ≥4 quadrants haemorrhages AND ≥2 quadrants venous beading AND ≥1 quadrant IRMA (any one criterion = severe)
  • Proliferative DR (PDR): Presence of new vessels
    • Neovascularization of disc (NVD) — most ominous
    • Neovascularization elsewhere (NVE)
    • Vitreous haemorrhage (VH)
    • Tractional retinal detachment (TRD)

Clinically Significant Macular Oedema (CSME)

  • Presence of any ONE:
    • Retinal thickening within 500 µm of fovea
    • Hard exudates within 500 µm of fovea
    • Retinal thickening ≥1 disc area (1.5 mm) within 1 disc diameter of fovea

Management of DR

  • NPDR: Strict glycaemic control; manage hypertension; consider anti-VEGF if CSME present
  • Severe NPDR: Close follow-up; laser may be considered if high-risk features
  • PDR:
    • Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP): Gold standard historically; now anti-VEGF preferred first-line
    • Anti-VEGF (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept): First-line for PDR; intravitreal injection
    • Vitrectomy: If vitreous haemorrhage preventing laser or if TRD threatening macula
  • CSME:
    • ETDRS trial: Focal laser for focal leakage; grid laser for diffuse leakage
    • Anti-VEGF: Now first-line for DME (diabetic macular oedema); equivalent or superior to laser
    • Intravitreal steroids if anti-VEGF inadequate response
ETDRS Trial Landmark: Laser photocoagulation reduces vision loss risk by 50%; anti-VEGF now preferred with better visual outcomes.

Age-Related Macular Degeneration (ARMD)

UGPG
▼

Dry (Atrophic) ARMD

  • Features: Drusen (lipofuscin deposits), RPE changes, geographic atrophy (sharply demarcated areas of RPE loss)
  • Prevalence: ~85–90% of ARMD cases
  • Visual loss: Gradual; may progress to severe if foveal atrophy develops
  • Treatment: No proven intervention for dry ARMD
    • AREDS2 supplements: Lutein, zeaxanthin, omega-3 (slows progression in intermediate-to-advanced dry)
    • Antioxidants + zinc: slows geographic atrophy progression

Wet (Exudative/Neovascular) ARMD

  • Pathology: Choroidal neovascularization (CNV); subretinal/intraretinal fluid; haemorrhage
  • Incidence: ~10–15% of ARMD but accounts for 80% of severe vision loss
  • Presentation: Sudden/rapid metamorphopsia (distorted vision); scotoma; vision loss over days to weeks
  • Management:
    • Anti-VEGF (gold standard): Ranibizumab (Lucentis), bevacizumab (Avastin), aflibercept (Eylea)
    • CATT trial: Bevacizumab ≈ ranibizumab efficacy; similar safety profiles; bevacizumab more cost-effective
    • Intravitreal injections monthly (loading) or PRN (pro re nata)
    • Photodynamic therapy (PDT): Less commonly used now (superseded by anti-VEGF)

Retinal Detachment (RD) — Types & Management

UGPG
▼

Rhegmatogenous RD (Most Common, ~90%)

  • Pathophysiology: Retinal break (hole/tear) + fluid seeps through → RPE cannot pump fluid out → separation
  • Types of breaks:
    • Horseshoe tears: Tractional; flap of retina remains attached at apex
    • Atrophic/round holes: Full-thickness defect; no traction
    • Dialysis: Disinsertion at ora serrata; usually peripheral
  • Signs:
    • Tobacco dust (Shafer's sign) = pigment in vitreous from RPE disruption
    • C-sign = detached retina margin
    • Buckled retina elevation; folds
  • Treatment:
    • Scleral buckling: Creates indent; elevates detached retina back toward pigment epithelium
    • Pneumatic retinopexy: Gas bubble tamponade; patient positioning critical
    • Vitrectomy + endotamponade: Gas (C3F8, SF6) or silicone oil (for proliferative vitreoretinopathy, PVR)

Tractional RD

  • Cause: Fibrovascular membranes contract; no retinal breaks; concave detachment
  • Associated with: Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), advanced retinitis pigmentosa, sickle cell retinopathy
  • Management: Vitrectomy to relieve traction; careful membrane peeling

Exudative RD

  • Features: No break, no traction; fluid accumulation beneath retina from choroid
  • Causes: Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH), Coats' disease, malignant hypertension, pregnancy-related eclampsia
  • Management: Treat underlying cause; diuretics; laser photocoagulation if indicated

Central & Branch Retinal Vein Occlusions (CRVO/BRVO) & Artery Occlusions (CRAO)

UGPG
▼

Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO)

  • Presentation: Sudden painless vision loss; haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants ("blood and thunder" appearance)
  • Findings: Disc oedema, dilated tortuous veins, flame and dot-blot haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, macular oedema
  • Classification:
    • Non-ischaemic: Better prognosis; preserved visual function; ~75% of cases
    • Ischaemic: Severe vision loss; risk of neovascular glaucoma (NVG) ~50%; poorer prognosis
  • Management:
    • Anti-VEGF: First-line for macular oedema and NVG prevention
    • Grid laser (ETDRS) for persistent macular oedema
    • Monitor for NVG development; prophylactic pan-retinal photocoagulation if ischaemic

Branch Retinal Vein Occlusion (BRVO)

  • Presentation: Vision loss in affected sector; limited to one quadrant
  • Findings: Sector haemorrhages, venous dilation in affected area, macular oedema if foveal involvement
  • Trials:
    • SCORE trial: Intravitreal triamcinolone ≈ laser for macular oedema; anti-VEGF now preferred
    • CRUISE trial: Ranibizumab superior to sham for macular oedema
  • Management: Anti-VEGF (ranibizumab/aflibercept) for macular oedema; grid laser as adjunct

Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO) — EMERGENCY

  • Presentation: Sudden, painless, severe vision loss; afferent pupil defect present
  • Findings:Cherry red spot: Central fovea (thin retina, visible choroid) appears red against whitish oedematous retina
  • Pathophysiology: End artery; occlusion → sudden ischaemia; no collateral circulation
  • Emergency Management: Urgent intervention within 90 minutes (golden window)
    • Decrease IOP: Anterior chamber paracentesis, IV mannitol, topical/systemic CAI
    • Orbital massage: Mechanical dislodgement of clot
    • Inhaled CO₂: Vasodilation, increased blood flow
    • Thrombolysis: Limited evidence; risky if contraindicated
    • Cilioretinal artery (present 20%): Spares vision if present (papillomacular bundle supplied)
  • Prognosis: ~90% remain with severe vision loss <6/60 despite treatment

Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP)

PG
▼

Clinical Features

  • Night blindness (nyctalopia): Progressive; earliest symptom; rod photoreceptor loss
  • Tubular vision: Progressive peripheral field loss; normal central vision initially
  • Pigmentary changes: Bone spicule (spicular) pigmentation in mid-periphery; atrophic areas; RPE granularity
  • Vascular changes: Attenuated (thin) retinal vessels; constricted arterioles
  • Optic disc: Waxy pallor (characteristic); normal or slightly pale
  • Cataract: Posterior subcapsular common; develops early (~40–50% of RP patients)
  • Cystoid macular oedema (CMO): Reduces central vision; may respond to carbonic anhydrase inhibitors

Investigations

  • Electroretinography (ERG): Extinguished or severely reduced (diagnostic); non-recordable in advanced disease
  • Visual fields: Concentric field loss; progressive from periphery centripetal
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT): Reveals macular changes, CMO, outer segment loss

Associated Syndromes

  • Usher syndrome: RP + congenital sensorineural deafness; most common syndromic form
  • Refsum disease: RP + peripheral neuropathy + cerebellar ataxia + cardiac conduction defects
  • Kearns-Sayre syndrome: RP + progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO) + cardiac conduction abnormalities
  • Bassen-Kornzweig syndrome (abetalipoproteinaemia): RP + malabsorption + ataxia
  • Bardet-Biedl syndrome: RP + polydactyly + renal dysplasia + obesity + infertility

Management

  • Vitamin A supplementation: May slow progression in some forms
  • Management of CMO: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide, acetazolamide)
  • Cataract surgery if visually significant
  • Low-vision aids; genetic counselling (autosomal recessive, dominant, X-linked forms)

Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)

PG
▼

Pathophysiology

  • Risk factors: Gestational age <32 weeks, birth weight <1500g, oxygen therapy (hyperoxia)
  • Mechanism: Abnormal retinal vascularization; initial vessel regression (hyperoxia), then excessive neovascularization

Classification (International ROP)

  • Zone (distance from fovea):
    • Zone I: Closest to fovea (most posterior)
    • Zone II: Intermediate
    • Zone III: Most peripheral
  • Stage (1–5):
    • Stage 1: Demarcation line
    • Stage 2: Ridge
    • Stage 3: Ridge with extraretinal fibrovascular tissue
    • Stage 4: Partial retinal detachment
    • Stage 5: Total retinal detachment (end stage; worst prognosis)
  • Plus disease: Vascular tortuosity + iris/pupil abnormalities; indicates aggressive ROP

Treatment Indications (Type 1 ROP)

  • Type 1 (requires treatment):
    • Zone I, any stage with plus disease
    • Zone I, stage 3 without plus disease
    • Zone II, stage 2 or 3 with plus disease
  • Type 2 (observe closely): Zone I stage 1–2 without plus, Zone II stage 3 without plus

Management

  • Laser photocoagulation: Ablates avascular retina; reduces NV stimulus; gold standard historically
  • Anti-VEGF (bevacizumab, aflibercept, ranibizumab): Intravitreal injection; increasingly used
    • BEAT-ROP trial: Anti-VEGF superior to laser for Zone I ROP; reduces structural failure & unfavourable visual outcome
  • Vitrectomy: For retinal detachment (stage 4–5)

Screening Guidelines

  • Indications: Gestational age ≤32 weeks OR birth weight ≤1500g
  • Timing of first exam: 4 weeks postnatal age (or 31 weeks postmenstrual age, whichever is later)
  • Frequency: Weekly exams until full vascularization or disease regression (typically 4–8 weeks)

Coats' Disease

PG
▼

Features

  • Epidemiology: Unilateral (90%), males, young boys (typically <10 years old)
  • Pathology: Retinal telangiectasia (abnormally dilated retinal vessels) + exudation (no neovascularization)
  • Key difference from PDR: Coats = primary vascular abnormality + exudation (NO new vessel proliferation); PDR = neovascularization + ischaemia

Clinical Presentation

  • White pupil (leukocoria): Most common presentation; yellow-white subretinal exudation
  • Vision loss: Due to macular involvement or total RD
  • Exudation: Yellow-white intraretinal and subretinal lipid-rich exudates; "hard exudates" (different from CSME)
  • Retinal breaks: NO breaks (important distinction from rhegmatogenous RD) → exudative RD if extensive

Diagnosis

  • Fundus examination: Telangiectasia, exudation, no breaks
  • B-scan ultrasound: Rules out vitreous haemorrhage or mass
  • Differential: Must exclude retinoblastoma (which is more common cause of leukocoria; unilateral or bilateral)

Management

  • Laser photocoagulation: Ablates telangiectatic vessels; reduces exudation
  • Cryotherapy (cryo): Used if peripheral disease or ischemia; freezes abnormal vessels
  • Anti-VEGF: Emerging role; may reduce exudation and neovascular complications
  • Monitoring: Regular follow-up for progression, secondary glaucoma, media opacity

MCQ Bank — Retinal Diseases

Q1 UG
The 4-2-1 rule defines SEVERE NPDR in diabetic retinopathy. Which of these is CORRECT?
A≥4 quadrants haemorrhages required (mandatory)
BAll three criteria (4,2,1) must be present simultaneously
CANY ONE of the criteria qualifies as severe (≥4 quads haem OR ≥2 quads VB OR ≥1 quad IRMA)
DOnly intraretinal microvascular abnormalities matter
Correct Answer: C — ANY ONE of the criteria qualifies as severe
4-2-1 rule: ANY ONE criterion present = severe NPDR. Either ≥4 quadrants haemorrhages, OR ≥2 quadrants venous beading, OR ≥1 quadrant IRMA. This indicates higher risk of progression to PDR.
Q2 PG
Clinically Significant Macular Oedema (CSME) is defined by presence of ANY of:
ARetinal thickening within 500µm of fovea, hard exudates within 500µm of fovea, or thickening ≥1 disc area within 1 DD
BMicroaneurysms only
CMacular haemorrhages only
DVitreous haemorrhage
Correct Answer: A — Retinal thickening within 500µm, hard exudates within 500µm, or thickening ≥1 disc area within 1 disc diameter
CSME definition (ETDRS): presence of ANY ONE of these three findings. This is the basis for laser photocoagulation indication (focal or grid laser depending on pattern).
Q3 PG
The CATT trial compared bevacizumab and ranibizumab for wet ARMD. Which statement is TRUE?
ARanibizumab was significantly superior to bevacizumab
BBevacizumab showed equivalent efficacy to ranibizumab with similar safety; bevacizumab is more cost-effective
CBevacizumab had significantly higher adverse effects
DBoth agents were inferior to photodynamic therapy (PDT)
Correct Answer: B — Bevacizumab showed equivalent efficacy to ranibizumab with similar safety; bevacizumab is more cost-effective
CATT trial (Comparison of Age-related macular degeneration Treatments with Ranibizumab): Bevacizumab (Avastin, off-label) ≈ ranibizumab (Lucentis, approved). Similar visual outcomes, safety profiles. Bevacizumab cheaper → widely used in developing countries.
Q4 UG
Which finding is PATHOGNOMONIC for Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO)?
ADisc oedema with haemorrhages in 4 quadrants
BSudden vision loss with afferent pupil defect
CCherry red spot at fovea
DRetinal folds (Paton's lines)
Correct Answer: C — Cherry red spot at fovea
Cherry red spot: Central fovea (thin retina, normal choroid visible = red) contrasts with surrounding whitish oedematous retina. Pathognomonic for CRAO. APD and vision loss also present, but cherry red spot is the characteristic ophthalmoscopic sign.
Q5 PG
Which type of retinal detachment has a retinal break or tear?
ATractional RD
BExudative RD
CSecondary RD to scleritis
DRhegmatogenous RD
Correct Answer: D — Rhegmatogenous RD
Rhegmatogenous (Greek "rhegma" = break): REQUIRES a retinal break (horseshoe tear, round hole, dialysis). Tractional = fibrosis without break. Exudative = no break, no traction.
Q6 PG
Tobacco dust (Shafer's sign) in vitreous indicates:
ARetinal pigment epithelium disruption; commonly seen in retinal breaks/detachment
BBacterial infection
CAsteroid hyalosis
DChronic alcohol use
Correct Answer: A — RPE disruption; commonly seen in retinal breaks/detachment
Tobacco dust (Shafer's sign): Brown/black pigment granules in vitreous from disruption of retinal pigment epithelium. Sign of retinal break and/or detachment. Indicates tractional forces at work.
Q7 UG
Most common presenting symptom of Retinitis Pigmentosa:
ASudden vision loss
BMetamorphopsia (distorted vision)
CNight blindness (nyctalopia); progressive peripheral field loss
DFlashes and floaters
Correct Answer: C — Night blindness (nyctalopia); progressive peripheral field loss
RP affects rod photoreceptors first → night blindness earliest symptom. Gradually progresses to tubular vision (peripheral fields contract). Central vision preserved initially. Bone spicule pigmentation, attenuated vessels, waxy pallor disc on exam.
Q8 PG
The BEAT-ROP trial demonstrated anti-VEGF superiority to laser in treatment of:
AProliferative diabetic retinopathy
BZone I Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)
CWet ARMD
DRetinal vein occlusion
Correct Answer: B — Zone I Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)
BEAT-ROP (Bevacizumab Eliminates Angiogenesis Through Aggressive Retinal Photocoagulation ROP) trial: Anti-VEGF intravitreal injection superior to laser for Type 1 ROP in Zone I. Reduces structural failure and unfavourable visual outcomes.
Q9 UG
Coats' disease is characterized by:
ANeovascularization with traction (like PDR)
BTelangiectasia + exudation; NO neovascularization; unilateral; young males
CBilateral presentation in girls
DRetinal breaks with rhegmatogenous detachment
Correct Answer: B — Telangiectasia + exudation; NO neovascularization; unilateral; young males
Coats' disease: Primary retinal vascular abnormality (dilated telangiectatic vessels) + excessive exudation (yellow-white lipid-rich). KEY: No neovascularization (unlike PDR). No retinal breaks (unlike rhegmatogenous RD). Most common in young boys; unilateral (90%).
Q10 PG
ROP screening should begin at:
A4 weeks postnatal age (or 31 weeks PMA, whichever later)
BBirth
C8 weeks postnatal
DClinically apparent symptoms only
Correct Answer: A — 4 weeks postnatal age (or 31 weeks PMA, whichever is later)
ROP screening eligibility: gestational age ≤32 weeks OR birth weight ≤1500g. First exam at 4 weeks postnatal (whichever is later than 31 weeks PMA). Weekly exams until full vascularization or regression.
Q11 PG
Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO) can be classified as ischaemic or non-ischaemic. Which has higher neovascular glaucoma (NVG) risk?
ANon-ischaemic CRVO
BBoth have equal risk
CIschaemic CRVO (~50% risk); requires pan-PRP prophylaxis
DNVG does not occur in CRVO
Correct Answer: C — Ischaemic CRVO (~50% risk); requires pan-PRP prophylaxis
Ischaemic CRVO (15–20% of CRVO cases) has ~50% risk of NVG due to retinal ischaemia triggering VEGF production. Non-ischaemic (~75–80%) has better prognosis. Ischaemic requires PRP prophylaxis + anti-VEGF; close monitoring for iris neovascularization.
Q12 UG
Electroretinography (ERG) is MOST useful in diagnosing:
ADiabetic retinopathy
BRetinitis pigmentosa (extinguished or severely reduced ERG)
CCentral serous retinopathy
DWet ARMD
Correct Answer: B — Retinitis pigmentosa (extinguished or severely reduced ERG)
ERG is gold standard diagnostic test for RP. Shows absent or severely reduced response due to widespread photoreceptor dysfunction. Useful in hereditary retinal dystrophies. Other conditions diagnosed clinically and with imaging (OCT, angiography).

University Exam 2019
Describe the clinical features of retinitis pigmentosa and its management. (7 marks)
Features: Night blindness (earliest), tubular vision (peripheral loss), bone spicule pigmentation, attenuated vessels, waxy disc, ERG extinguished. Management: Vitamin A, CMO treatment (CAI), cataract surgery, low-vision aids, genetic counselling.

DR Classification

  • NPDR: Mild (MA), Moderate, Severe (4-2-1)
  • PDR: NVD, NVE, VH, TRD
  • CSME: Any ONE: thickening within 500µm fovea, exudates within 500µm, thickening ≥1 DD within 1 DD
  • ETDRS: Focal laser for focal, grid for diffuse

ARMD Comparison

  • Dry (85–90%): Drusen, atrophy; slow loss; AREDS2 supplements
  • Wet (10–15%): CNV, sudden loss; anti-VEGF (bevacizumab≈ranibizumab; CATT trial)

RD Types

  • Rhegmatogenous (90%): Retinal break + fluid; tobacco dust; C-sign; treatment: buckle/vitrectomy
  • Tractional: Fibrosis (PDR); vitrectomy
  • Exudative: No break, no traction (VKH, Coats'); treat cause

Vascular Occlusions

  • CRVO: 4 quads; disc oedema; non-isch 75% vs isch 25%; NVG risk high in isch
  • BRVO: Sector; CRUISE trial (anti-VEGF); SCORE trial (steroid)
  • CRAO: CHERRY RED SPOT; emergency <90 min; poor prognosis
