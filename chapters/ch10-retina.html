Diseases of the Retina

From diabetic retinopathy and ARMD to retinal detachment, ROP, and vascular occlusions — covering all major retinal diseases at PG-exam depth with clinical staging, investigation findings, and management protocols.

These cards cover the highest-yield facts in retinal disease — the classification rules, the mnemonics, and the management decisions that appear most often in both UG and PG exams. Use them for last-minute revision before any clinical posting or written paper.

DR Classification HY

Non-Proliferative DR (NPDR)

  • Mild: Microaneurysms only
  • Moderate: MA + hard/soft exudates + dot-blot haemorrhages, but not meeting severe criteria
  • Severe (4-2-1 rule): Haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants, OR venous beading in 2+ quadrants, OR IRMA in 1+ quadrant

Proliferative DR (PDR): Neovascularisation (NVD or NVE), vitreous haemorrhage, tractional RD

CSME criteria (any one): Retinal thickening within 500 µm of foveal centre; hard exudates within 500 µm with adjacent thickening; retinal thickening >1 disc area within 1 DD of centre

ARMD at a Glance HY

Dry ARMD (90%): Drusen beneath RPE, progressive geographic atrophy, slow central vision loss over years. No vascular component.

Wet ARMD (10%, 90% of blindness): Choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) breaches Bruch's membrane, leaks fluid and blood. Vision can fall over days.

  • FFA: Classic CNV (well-defined hyperfluorescence early, leaks late); Occult CNV (ill-defined, late staining)
  • OCT: Subretinal fluid, pigment epithelial detachment, hyperreflective CNV membrane
  • Treatment: Anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept, brolucizumab) for wet ARMD
  • AREDS supplements (zinc + antioxidants) slow progression from intermediate to advanced dry

Retinal Detachment Types HY

Rhegmatogenous RD (most common): PVD creates a tear or break; liquid vitreous enters the subretinal space. Convex, mobile, corrugated appearance. Tobacco dust (Shafer's sign) in vitreous.

Tractional RD: Fibrovascular membranes (diabetic, sickle cell, ROP) pull the retina without a break. Concave, immobile surface. No shifting fluid.

Exudative/Serous RD: Fluid accumulates from choroidal disease (tumour, posterior scleritis, VKH, Coats). No break, no traction. Shifting fluid on posture change — the hallmark finding.

CRVO vs BRVO HY

  • CRVO: Occlusion at or behind lamina cribrosa. Fundus: flame haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants, disc swelling, dilated tortuous veins ("stormy sunset"). Risk: hypertension, hyperviscosity.
  • BRVO: Occlusion at an AV crossing, upper temporal quadrant most common. Sectoral haemorrhages in that quadrant only. Better visual prognosis.
  • Ischaemic CRVO: >10 DD of capillary non-perfusion on FFA; risk of rubeosis iridis (100-day glaucoma).
  • Management: Anti-VEGF for macular oedema (both); PRP if neovascularisation develops; treat underlying systemic cause.

ROP Essentials HY

Zones (ICROP3): Zone I — circle centred on disc, radius = 2× disc-to-fovea distance. Zone II — extends from Zone I edge to nasal ora serrata. Zone III — remaining temporal crescent.

Stages: 1 = demarcation line; 2 = ridge; 3 = ridge + extraretinal fibrovascular proliferation; 4A = partial RD (fovea on); 4B = partial RD (fovea off); 5 = total RD.

Plus disease: Vascular dilation and tortuosity in 2+ quadrants of posterior pole, meeting or exceeding a standard photograph.

Treatment indications: Zone I any stage with plus, Zone I stage 3 without plus, Zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus. Treatment: laser photocoagulation or intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB).

Key Mnemonics

4-2-1 Rule for Severe NPDR

  • 4 quadrants of dot-blot haemorrhages
  • 2 quadrants of venous beading
  • 1 quadrant of IRMA

Any one of these alone = Severe NPDR. 50% progress to PDR within 1 year.

SWAP for DR Investigations

  • S — Slit lamp biomicroscopy (90D/78D)
  • W — Wide-field fundus photography
  • A — Angiography (FFA for ischaemia/leakage)
  • P — Perimetry + OCT (structural/functional)

DR Management Algorithm HY

1 Mild / Moderate NPDR without DME: Optimise systemic control (HbA1c, BP, lipids). Fundus review every 6–12 months. No ocular intervention unless DME present.
2 Severe NPDR / Early PDR: PRP (panretinal photocoagulation) — 1200–1600 burns over 2–3 sessions. Reduces neovascularisation risk by 50%. Anti-VEGF can defer or replace PRP in some cases.
3 Centre-involving DME: Intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, or aflibercept) as first-line. Monthly loading dose × 3, then PRN. Intravitreal steroid (triamcinolone, dexamethasone implant) for refractory or pseudophakic cases.
4 Advanced PDR (vitreous haemorrhage, tractional RD): Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with membrane peeling, endolaser, tamponade if required. Anti-VEGF given 3–7 days pre-op to reduce intraoperative bleeding.

Retinal Anatomy and Physiology

The Ten Layers — Inside Out

The retina is a transparent, stratified neural tissue that lines the posterior two-thirds of the globe. Its ten layers, arranged from vitreous inward to choroid, are:

  1. Internal limiting membrane (ILM) — Müller cell footplates, forms the vitreoretinal interface
  2. Nerve fibre layer (NFL) — axons of ganglion cells converging toward the disc
  3. Ganglion cell layer (GCL)
  4. Inner plexiform layer (IPL) — synapses between bipolar and ganglion cells
  5. Inner nuclear layer (INL) — bipolar, amacrine, horizontal, and Müller cell nuclei
  6. Outer plexiform layer (OPL) — synapses between photoreceptors and bipolar cells; Henle fibre layer at macula
  7. Outer nuclear layer (ONL) — photoreceptor cell bodies
  8. External limiting membrane (ELM) — junctional complexes between photoreceptors and Müller cells
  9. Photoreceptor inner and outer segments (IS/OS)
  10. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) — single layer of hexagonal cells, vital for visual cycle and phagocytosis of shed outer segments

Cross-section of the retina showing the ten histological layers from vitreous (top) to choroid (bottom). The outer retina (layers 7–10) is avascular and depends on the choriocapillaris for oxygen.

Blood Supply

The retina has a dual blood supply. The inner five layers are supplied by the central retinal artery (CRA), a branch of the ophthalmic artery that enters the optic nerve. The outer retinal layers — photoreceptors and RPE — are avascular and depend entirely on diffusion from the choriocapillaris through Bruch's membrane. This is why any disruption to the RPE-Bruch's-choriocapillaris complex has such a profound impact on photoreceptor health.

Blood-Retinal Barrier

There are two components. The inner BRB is formed by tight junctions between retinal vascular endothelial cells. The outer BRB is formed by tight junctions between RPE cells (zonulae occludentes). In diabetes, breakdown of the inner BRB leads to leakage of fluid and lipid into the retina, producing the classic features of DME.

Foveal Avascular Zone

The fovea is the central 1.5 mm of the macula and contains the highest density of cones. The central 0.5 mm — the foveola — is entirely avascular, relying on diffusion from the choriocapillaris. This is why the FAZ is preserved in conditions that primarily affect the retinal circulation, but is damaged when the choroid is involved. On FFA, the FAZ appears as a dark ring surrounded by a capillary arcade.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Pathogenesis

Chronic hyperglycaemia triggers several interconnected pathways. The polyol pathway converts excess glucose to sorbitol via aldose reductase — sorbitol accumulates in pericytes, damaging them. Pericyte loss is the earliest histological change in diabetic retinopathy, and without pericyte support, capillary walls weaken and form microaneurysms. Simultaneously, the protein kinase C pathway is activated, increasing VEGF production. Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) further damage the basement membrane. Together these lead to capillary leakage, ischaemia, and eventually neovascularisation driven by VEGF.

Summary of diabetic retinopathy stages and their characteristic fundus and FFA features. IRMA and NVE are distinguished by their behaviour on FFA: IRMA does not leak, NVE leaks profusely.

ETDRS Classification

The ETDRS (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study) scale remains the gold standard for grading DR severity. For clinical and exam purposes, the simplified international scale is more commonly used: no DR, mild NPDR, moderate NPDR, severe NPDR, and PDR. The progression from severe NPDR to PDR within one year occurs in roughly 50% of patients, rising to 75% within two years — which is why severe NPDR triggers treatment regardless of whether PDR has developed.

FFA Findings in DR

Microaneurysms appear as hyperfluorescent dots in the early arteriovenous phase, persisting without fade into the late phase. Hard exudates block choroidal fluorescence and appear as hypofluorescent areas. NVD and NVE leak profusely — the frond of new vessels shows intense early fluorescence that then blooms into surrounding vitreous. IRMA, by contrast, shows the capillary channels without leakage. Areas of capillary non-perfusion appear as flat dark areas without any capillary filling — these are critical to identify because they predict neovascularisation risk.

OCT Findings in DME

The defining OCT feature of DME is increased central macular thickness (CMT), normally below 250 µm. Diffuse macular oedema appears as generalised retinal thickening with low intraretinal reflectivity. Cystoid macular oedema (CMO) shows characteristic hyporeflective cystic spaces within the retina, often in the outer nuclear and inner nuclear layers. A subretinal fluid component can also be present, appearing as a dark band beneath the photoreceptor layer. Hard exudates appear as hyperreflective deposits that cast a posterior shadow.

Treatment

Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) targets the peripheral ischaemic retina, reducing VEGF production and causing regression of new vessels. It is delivered as 1200–1600 burns of 200–500 µm spot size in 2–3 sessions. The ETDRS showed PRP reduces the risk of severe visual loss in PDR by 50%. The main side effects are peripheral field loss and exacerbation of macular oedema, so PRP should be preceded by treatment of DME when both are present.

For DME, intravitreal anti-VEGF injections have replaced macular laser as first-line treatment. Ranibizumab (Lucentis), bevacizumab (Avastin), and aflibercept (Eylea) have all shown equivalent functional outcomes in DRCR.net Protocol T. Aflibercept shows a marginal advantage in eyes with worse baseline vision. Intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide (IVTA) and the dexamethasone implant (Ozurdex) are alternatives for pseudophakic eyes or those who fail anti-VEGF, though cataract and IOP elevation are important side effects.

DRCR Protocol S (2015): Ranibizumab was non-inferior to PRP for PDR over 2 years. Eyes treated with ranibizumab had better visual acuity outcomes and lower rates of vitrectomy, at the cost of more frequent injections. Anti-VEGF for PDR is now an accepted alternative to PRP, particularly when DME coexists.

Vitrectomy is indicated for non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage (>3 months), tractional RD involving or threatening the macula, combined tractional-rhegmatogenous RD, and dense premacular haemorrhage. Pre-operative bevacizumab given 3–7 days before surgery reduces intraoperative bleeding and facilitates membrane dissection.

Systemic Risk Factors for DR Progression
Duration of diabetes is the single strongest predictor. After 20 years, almost all Type 1 and >60% of Type 2 diabetics have some DR. Other factors: poor glycaemic control (HbA1c >9%), hypertension, nephropathy, dyslipidaemia, anaemia, pregnancy, and puberty (accelerates in adolescence).

Drusen — The Starting Point

Drusen are deposits of extracellular material between the RPE and Bruch's membrane. Hard drusen are small (<63 µm), discrete, well-defined, and carry a low risk of progression. Soft drusen are larger (>125 µm), confluent, and poorly defined — their presence significantly increases the risk of progressing to geographic atrophy or CNV. Reticular pseudodrusen (subretinal drusenoid deposits) are located above the RPE and are an independent risk factor for late ARMD.

Geographic Atrophy

GA represents end-stage dry ARMD — progressive loss of RPE, photoreceptors, and choriocapillaris in a well-demarcated area. On fundus examination, it appears as a pale, sharply defined area of RPE loss through which choroidal vessels are clearly visible. On FAF (fundus autofluorescence), GA appears as a dark area of absent autofluorescence, with a hyperautofluorescent border indicating metabolically stressed RPE. There is no licensed treatment for GA at present, though complement inhibitors (pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol) have shown modest benefit in trials.

Choroidal Neovascularisation

CNV is the hallmark of wet ARMD. New blood vessels proliferate from the choriocapillaris through breaks in Bruch's membrane into the sub-RPE or subretinal space. On FFA, classic CNV shows a well-defined lacy or wheel-spoke network of vessels in the early phase with progressive leakage obscuring the margins in late phases. Occult CNV is less well-defined — it shows either a fibrovascular PED (irregular RPE elevation with late staining) or late leakage of undetermined source.

On OCT, key findings include subretinal fluid (SRF), intraretinal fluid (IRF), and pigment epithelial detachment (PED). The CNV membrane itself may be visible as a hyperreflective band beneath the RPE or between the RPE and Bruch's. Neovascular ARMD classified on OCT-A has revealed that CNVs are present even in some eyes without fluid — these quiescent CNVs may or may not require treatment and remain an area of active debate.

Anti-VEGF Agents

Four anti-VEGF agents are used for wet ARMD. Ranibizumab (0.5 mg) is a Fab fragment of a humanised monoclonal antibody — the first to be licensed and still widely used. Bevacizumab (1.25 mg) is an off-label full antibody with equivalent efficacy at a fraction of the cost (CATT trial, IVAN trial). Aflibercept (2 mg) is a fusion protein that binds VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and placental growth factor with higher affinity than ranibizumab — useful in eyes that respond poorly to ranibizumab. Brolucizumab (6 mg) is the newest agent, a single-chain antibody fragment with the highest VEGF-binding affinity, requiring less frequent dosing — but associated with a small risk of retinal vasculitis and occlusive retinal arteritis.

Treatment regimens include monthly dosing, PRN (treat-and-extend), and treat-and-extend (T&E). T&E is now the most widely used — after loading doses, the interval is extended by 2 weeks if dry, and reduced if fluid recurs. Most eyes can reach 12-weekly intervals with good control.

AREDS Supplements

The AREDS formula (zinc 80 mg + copper 2 mg + vitamin C 500 mg + vitamin E 400 IU + beta-carotene 15 mg) reduced the risk of progression from intermediate to advanced ARMD by 25% over 5 years. The AREDS2 formula replaces beta-carotene with lutein 10 mg and zeaxanthin 2 mg, because beta-carotene increased lung cancer risk in smokers. The AREDS2 formula is now preferred and should be recommended to patients with intermediate ARMD (large drusen or GA in one eye).

PDT with Verteporfin: Photodynamic therapy was the standard treatment before anti-VEGF. It remains useful in select cases: subfoveal classic CNV in patients who cannot receive injections, polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV, in combination with anti-VEGF — EVEREST protocol), and central serous chorioretinopathy (half-fluence PDT).

Retinal Detachment

▼

Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment

RRD is the most common type, occurring when a full-thickness break in the retina allows liquefied vitreous to pass into the subretinal space, separating the neurosensory retina from the RPE. The usual sequence is posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) — which occurs physiologically with age — followed by traction on a point of vitreoretinal adhesion, creating a horseshoe (flap) tear. Round atrophic holes are more common in lattice degeneration. Dialyses are circumferential breaks at the ora serrata, common after blunt trauma in young patients.

Symptoms are characteristic: photopsia (flashes) from vitreoretinal traction, floaters from the vitreous haemorrhage or Weiss ring of PVD, and then a dark shadow or curtain spreading from the periphery as the detachment advances. A macula-on detachment (fovea still attached) is a surgical emergency — results are significantly worse once the macula detaches.

On examination, Shafer's sign (tobacco dust — pigment cells in the anterior vitreous seen on slit lamp) is pathognomonic of a retinal break. The detached retina appears corrugated, elevated, and mobile. Subretinal fluid shifts with posture change (inferior detachments).

Tractional Retinal Detachment

TRD results from fibrovascular membranes on the retinal surface or within the vitreous that contract and pull the retina forward. The common causes are proliferative DR, sickle cell retinopathy, penetrating trauma, and advanced ROP. The detachment is concave, immobile, and does not extend to the ora serrata. There is no retinal break and no shifting fluid. The surface is smooth and taut. If a break develops in a TRD (combined tractional-rhegmatogenous RD), the fluid spreads rapidly and the urgency of surgery increases.

Exudative Retinal Detachment

ERD occurs when fluid accumulates in the subretinal space without a break or traction. The underlying cause is choroidal disease — posterior scleritis, VKH disease, uveal effusion, choroidal tumour (metastasis, haemangioma, melanoma), central serous chorioretinopathy, or Coats disease. The hallmark is shifting fluid: the subretinal fluid redistributes according to gravity, so the detachment changes shape when the patient changes posture. This distinguishes it from RRD on clinical examination.

Investigations

B-scan ultrasonography is essential when the view is obscured (dense vitreous haemorrhage, cataract). RRD shows a highly reflective membrane attached at the optic disc and ora serrata, mobile with eye movement. TRD shows membranes with reduced mobility. In ERD, look for choroidal thickening, a mass lesion, or T-sign (fluid in Tenon's space) in posterior scleritis.

Surgical Management of RRD

Three main surgical approaches are used. Scleral buckling (SB) is the traditional approach — an encircling or segmental silicone band is sutured to the sclera to indent the eye wall, closing the break and reducing vitreoretinal traction. It is particularly good for young phakic patients with inferior breaks and lattice degeneration without PVD. The success rate is over 90% for primary repair.

Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) involves removal of the vitreous gel, relief of all traction, drainage of SRF, laser retinopexy around all breaks, and intraocular tamponade. Tamponade options include SF6 gas (sulphur hexafluoride, absorbed in 2 weeks), C3F8 gas (perfluoropropane, lasts 6–8 weeks), and silicone oil (permanent until surgically removed, used when prolonged tamponade is needed or the patient cannot posture). The patient must posture to keep the gas bubble over the break.

Pneumatic retinopexy is an office-based procedure — an expansile gas bubble (SF6 or C3F8) is injected intravitreally, followed by cryotherapy or laser. It is suitable for superior breaks in phakic eyes, but has a higher re-detachment rate than PPV or SB (success around 70%).

Macula-on vs Macula-off: This is the single most important determinant of visual outcome. A macula-on RRD should be repaired within 24 hours — do not defer to next week's list. A macula-off detachment of less than 24 hours duration still warrants urgent surgery. Beyond 7 days of macula-off, visual recovery is limited even with anatomically successful surgery.

Central Retinal Vein Occlusion

CRVO occurs when the central retinal vein is occluded at or just behind the lamina cribrosa, where the arterial wall shares an adventitial sheath with the vein. The most common cause is arterial hypertension leading to arterial stiffening and compression of the vein. Other causes include hyperviscosity states (polycythaemia, Waldenström's), glaucoma (raised IOP compresses the vein at the lamina), and prothrombotic conditions.

The classic fundus appearance is "stormy sunset" or "blood and thunder fundus" — flame haemorrhages in all four quadrants, dilated tortuous veins, disc swelling, and multiple cotton wool spots. The key distinction is between ischaemic and non-ischaemic CRVO. Non-ischaemic (perfused) CRVO has <10 disc diameters of capillary non-perfusion on FFA and a normal or near-normal ERG. Ischaemic CRVO has >10 DD of non-perfusion, an abnormal b-wave:a-wave ratio on ERG, and a poor visual prognosis. About 33% of non-ischaemic cases convert to ischaemic over 3 years.

The dreaded complication of ischaemic CRVO is rubeosis iridis (neovascularisation of the iris) leading to neovascular glaucoma — this was historically called "100-day glaucoma" because it typically developed 3 months after the occlusion. All patients with CRVO require monthly monitoring for iris neovascularisation for at least 6 months.

Branch Retinal Vein Occlusion

BRVO occurs at arteriovenous crossings, where the artery shares an adventitial sheath with the vein. The superotemporal arcade is the most common site because there are more AV crossings here. The resulting occlusion produces sectoral haemorrhages, limited to the drainage territory of that branch vein. The upper temporal quadrant is most often affected, so macular oedema commonly develops. Vision loss in BRVO is usually less severe than CRVO and the prognosis is better.

Central Retinal Artery Occlusion

CRAO is an ophthalmological emergency. Sudden, painless, profound visual loss — typically counting fingers or worse — in an older patient with cardiovascular risk factors. The retina has a tolerance of roughly 90–100 minutes before irreversible ischaemic damage occurs. The fundus shows a milky white, oedematous retina with a cherry red spot at the fovea — the fovea appears red because its thin retina allows the underlying choroidal circulation to show through, while the thicker surrounding retina becomes oedematous and opaque. The optic disc is initially normal; pallor develops weeks later.

A cilioretinal artery (present in 20% of eyes) supplies a small area of the macula and arises from the posterior ciliary circulation. When a cilioretinal artery is present and CRAO occurs, this area is spared, preserving central vision despite total CRAO — an important exam point.

Acute management (within 4.5 hours): ocular massage, anterior chamber paracentesis, inhaled carbogen (5% CO2 + 95% O2), and IV acetazolamide — all aimed at lowering IOP and dislodging the embolus. Intra-arterial thrombolysis has been attempted but evidence remains limited. More importantly, CRAO must be treated as a TIA equivalent — urgent systemic workup for carotid disease, cardiac embolism, and coagulopathy is mandatory.

Branch Retinal Artery Occlusion

BRAO produces a wedge-shaped area of retinal whitening corresponding to the territory of the occluded arteriole. Visual acuity may be preserved if the fovea is spared, though an altitudinal or arcuate field defect is present. Fundus emboli (Hollenhorst plaques — cholesterol; white emboli — platelet-fibrin; calcific emboli — calcium from cardiac valves) may be visible. Systemic workup is identical to CRAO.

Retinopathy of Prematurity

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Background

Retinal vascularisation begins at 16 weeks from the optic disc and proceeds centrifugally, reaching the nasal ora at about 36 weeks and the temporal ora at 40 weeks. In a premature infant, the peripheral avascular retina is exposed to the relatively high oxygen environment of the NICU. When the infant is subsequently moved to room air, the avascular retina becomes hypoxic relative to its metabolic needs — VEGF surges, driving pathological neovascularisation at the junction between vascular and avascular retina.

ICROP3 Classification

Zones describe the location of the most peripheral retinal vascularisation. Zone I is the most posterior: a circle centred on the optic disc with a radius equal to twice the disc-to-fovea distance (roughly 30 degrees). Zone II extends from the Zone I margin to the nasal ora serrata (nasally) and around. Zone III is the remaining crescent of avascular temporal retina. Zone I disease is the most dangerous — it is close to the macula and carries a high risk of tractional RD.

Stages describe the severity of the disease at the vascular-avascular junction. Stage 1 is a flat demarcation line. Stage 2 is a ridge (the line gains height and width). Stage 3 is the ridge plus extraretinal fibrovascular proliferation extending into the vitreous. Stage 4 is partial retinal detachment (4A = fovea attached; 4B = fovea detached). Stage 5 is total retinal detachment, often funnel-shaped.

Plus disease is defined as vascular dilation and tortuosity in at least 2 quadrants of the posterior retina, meeting or exceeding a reference photograph. Pre-plus disease is dilation and tortuosity that does not meet the plus threshold but is abnormal. Aggressive posterior ROP (AP-ROP) is a rapidly progressive form in Zone I or posterior Zone II, with flat neovascularisation rather than a ridge — it does not follow the typical stage sequence and can progress directly to Stage 5.

Screening Criteria (India)

Screen all infants with birth weight below 2000 g or gestational age below 34 weeks. Also screen any infant above these thresholds who had a clinically unstable course (prolonged oxygen, sepsis, blood transfusion). First examination at 4 weeks postnatal age or 31 weeks post-menstrual age, whichever is later.

Treatment Indications

Treatment is indicated for Type 1 ROP: Zone I with any stage with plus disease; Zone I stage 3 without plus; Zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus disease. Type 2 ROP (Zone I stage 1 or 2 without plus; Zone II stage 3 without plus) is observed closely. The old concept of threshold disease has largely been replaced by the Type 1/Type 2 framework from the ETROP study.

Laser photocoagulation of the avascular peripheral retina remains the standard treatment. It ablates the source of VEGF and causes regression of neovascularisation. Intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB) has gained traction as an alternative, particularly for Zone I disease — it produces faster regression, does not damage peripheral retina, but requires longer follow-up because late recurrences can occur up to 65 weeks post-menstrual age. The RAINBOW trial demonstrated the safety and efficacy of ranibizumab for ROP as well.

Retinitis Pigmentosa

RP is the most common hereditary retinal dystrophy, affecting 1 in 4000 people. It is characterised by progressive rod photoreceptor degeneration followed by cone involvement. The inheritance can be autosomal dominant (best prognosis), autosomal recessive (most severe), or X-linked recessive (severe, males affected). Digenic and mitochondrial forms also exist. Over 70 genes have been implicated; RHO (rhodopsin) mutations account for 25% of autosomal dominant cases.

The cardinal symptom is nyctalopia (night blindness), appearing in the first or second decade. Concentric constriction of the visual field (tunnel vision) follows. Central vision is preserved until late in the disease. The classic fundus triad is: bone spicule pigmentation (pigment migration from the RPE into the inner retina along blood vessels), attenuated retinal arterioles, and waxy pallor of the optic disc. The macula may develop cystoid macular oedema in 20–30% of patients — treatable with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (oral acetazolamide or topical dorzolamide).

ERG is the most sensitive test — rod responses are markedly reduced or absent even before the fundus changes are visible. Full-field ERG shows reduced or non-recordable scotopic responses, and later, photopic responses.

Other Hereditary Dystrophies

Stargardt disease is the most common juvenile macular dystrophy, caused by mutations in ABCA4, encoding a photoreceptor-specific ABC transporter. Lipofuscin accumulates in the RPE. Fundus shows a beaten-bronze macular appearance with pisciform (fish-shaped) flecks in the posterior pole. FFA shows a "dark choroid" (silent choroid) in 80% of cases — the lipofuscin-laden RPE blocks background choroidal fluorescence. There is no established treatment, though gene therapy trials are ongoing.

Best vitelliform macular dystrophy is caused by mutations in BEST1 (bestrophin), encoding a chloride channel in the RPE. The characteristic lesion is a yolk-like (vitelliform) bright yellow subretinal deposit at the macula. The electro-oculogram (EOG) is severely abnormal (Arden ratio below 1.5) even in carriers with a normal-looking fundus — a unique feature that makes the EOG more diagnostically sensitive than the ERG in this condition.

Choroideremia is an X-linked recessive condition caused by mutations in CHM (encoding Rab escort protein 1). Progressive degeneration of the choroid, RPE, and photoreceptors from the periphery inward. Carriers have a salt-and-pepper fundus. Gene therapy (AAV-REP1) has shown sustained benefit in early trials.

Leber congenital amaurosis is the most severe hereditary retinal dystrophy, presenting at birth or in the first year of life with profound visual loss, nystagmus, and absent ERG responses. It is caused by mutations in over 25 genes; RPE65 mutations cause the most common form. Voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna) — an AAV2-based gene therapy — is approved for RPE65-associated LCA and represents the first licensed ocular gene therapy.

Central Serous Chorioretinopathy

CSCR predominantly affects young to middle-aged men with a type A personality and high corticosteroid exposure. Serous fluid accumulates beneath the neurosensory retina, causing a dome-shaped elevation at the macula. The patient notices a relative positive scotoma, micropsia, and metamorphopsia. On FFA, the classic "smokestack" pattern shows a hyperfluorescent leak that rises and then umbrellas as the dye spreads under the retina. On OCT, a dome of subretinal fluid is clearly visible, often with a small RPE detachment at the leakage point. Most acute episodes resolve spontaneously within 3–4 months. Chronic CSCR (>4 months) requires treatment: half-fluence PDT is the treatment of choice, with eplerenone (mineralocorticoid antagonist) as an oral alternative.

Eales Disease

Eales disease is an idiopathic obliterative vasculitis predominantly affecting the peripheral retina of young Indian men. The aetiology is strongly linked to hypersensitivity to Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens. Three phases are recognised: periphlebitis (inflammation of peripheral veins — perivascular sheathing), neovascularisation (peripheral new vessels develop in response to ischaemia), and complications (vitreous haemorrhage, tractional RD). Treatment includes systemic antitubercular therapy in tuberculin-positive cases, systemic steroids for the inflammatory phase, and laser photocoagulation for neovascularisation. Vitrectomy for non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage.

Coats Disease

Coats disease is a non-hereditary, unilateral condition predominantly affecting young boys. It is characterised by telangiectatic and aneurysmal retinal vessels with massive exudation into and beneath the retina. In severe cases, total exudative retinal detachment with leukocoria can mimic retinoblastoma. The distinction from retinoblastoma is critical: Coats disease has no calcification on CT (retinoblastoma does), negative family history, and the detachment is fluid rather than solid on B-scan. Treatment is laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy to the telangiectatic vessels, anti-VEGF as adjunct, and vitrectomy for retinal detachment in advanced cases.

Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma is the most common intraocular malignancy of childhood. It arises from retinal cells carrying mutations in both alleles of the RB1 tumour suppressor gene. The heritable form (40%) carries a germline mutation in one allele and is typically bilateral and multifocal; the non-heritable form (60%) arises from two somatic mutations and is unilateral. The classic presentation is leukocoria (white pupillary reflex), followed by strabismus, and less commonly, red painful eye (secondary glaucoma).

CT scan shows calcification within the tumour in over 90% of cases — this is the key distinguishing feature from Coats disease and other causes of leukocoria. Management depends on staging (IIRC/IRSS classification): intra-arterial chemotherapy (ophthalmic artery chemosurgery), intravitreal chemotherapy (melphalan), systemic chemoreduction, focal therapy (laser, cryotherapy), brachytherapy, and enucleation for advanced disease. Genetic counselling and family screening are mandatory for heritable cases.

Direct Ophthalmoscopy Bedside
1
Preparation

Dim the room lights and dilate the pupil if possible (tropicamide 1% + phenylephrine 2.5%). Set the ophthalmoscope aperture to the large white circle for dilated pupils, small circle for undilated. Start at +8 to +10 dioptres to examine the anterior segment and lens, then reduce the lens power as you move closer to the fundus.

2
Positioning

Stand at arm's length from the patient, same side as the eye you are examining (right eye with your right eye). Ask the patient to look at a distant target straight ahead, not at the light. Approach at about 15 degrees temporal to the patient's line of sight — this gives you the optic disc as your first landmark.

3
Examining the Disc

Focus on the optic disc first. Note the disc colour (pink normal, pale = optic atrophy, hyperaemic = disc swelling), margins (sharp or blurred), cup-to-disc ratio (normal <0.5), and any neovascularisation on or within 1 disc diameter of the disc (NVD in PDR). Note any disc swelling, haemorrhages, or pallor.

The disc-to-fovea distance is one disc diameter. The fovea is exactly 2 DD temporal to the disc — ask the patient to look directly at the light to bring the fovea into view, but do this briefly to avoid discomfort.
4
Examining the Vessels

Follow the four main arcades out from the disc. Note the arteriovenous ratio (normal 2:3), AV nipping (arteries kinking veins at crossings — hypertensive retinopathy), vessel tortuosity (CRVO), beading (severe NPDR), neovascularisation elsewhere (NVE — PDR), or silver/copper wiring (arteriosclerosis).

5
Examining the Macula

Ask the patient to look directly at the light. Observe the foveal reflex (bright central reflex — absent in macular disease), hard exudates (yellow, waxy), circinate exudate rings suggesting CMO, cherry red spot (CRAO), or retinal haemorrhages. Direct ophthalmoscopy gives a 15× magnified, monocular, non-stereoscopic view of a small area — its limitation is the lack of depth perception.

Direct ophthalmoscopy gives a 15× magnified view covering about 8 degrees. It is excellent for the disc and posterior pole but cannot adequately examine the peripheral retina.
Indirect Ophthalmoscopy with 20D Lens Clinic / OT
1
Setup

The binocular indirect ophthalmoscope (BIO) is worn on the head. Adjust the interpupillary distance dial. Set the light to a comfortable intensity. Maximally dilate the pupil (tropicamide + phenylephrine). The 20D condensing lens is held 5 cm from the patient's eye, perpendicular to the visual axis.

2
Image Characteristics

The image formed is real, inverted, and laterally reversed — both vertically and horizontally. A lesion at the top of the image is actually in the inferior fundus. A 20D lens gives approximately 3× magnification and a 45-degree field of view. A 28D lens gives a wider field (60 degrees) with less magnification — preferred for peripheral examination and ROP screening. A 30D lens is used for very poor pupils.

Remember: the image is inverted. When you draw the fundus diagram, invert what you see — inferior retina is at the top of what you observe, superior at the bottom.
3
Systematic Examination

Start at the disc, then examine each arcade. To examine the peripheral retina, ask the patient to look in the direction of the quadrant you want to see — looking up to examine the superior fundus, down for inferior. For the far periphery and ora serrata, scleral indentation with a scleral depressor is required (under topical anaesthesia).

4
Advantages over Direct

BIO provides a stereoscopic, wide-field view of the entire retina including the periphery. This makes it indispensable for examining retinal detachments, peripheral breaks, ROP (Zone II and III), and trauma. The bright light source also penetrates mild media opacities better than direct ophthalmoscopy.

Slit Lamp Biomicroscopy with 90D / 78D Lens Clinic
1
Lens Characteristics

The 90D lens gives higher magnification (~7×) but a narrower field (20–25 degrees). The 78D lens gives slightly lower magnification (~6.7×) but a wider, better-quality image with less aberration — preferred for detailed macular examination. A 66D lens is sometimes used for high-magnification disc assessment. These are non-contact lenses — no topical anaesthesia needed, but the patient must hold very still.

2
Technique

Dilate the pupil maximally. Ask the patient to rest their chin and forehead on the slit lamp. Hold the condensing lens about 8–10 mm from the patient's cornea using your thumb and forefinger, steadying your hand on the slit lamp frame. The image in the oculars is inverted and reversed, as with BIO. Use a narrow slit beam to assess retinal thickness and elevation in three dimensions — this is the key advantage over direct ophthalmoscopy.

The stereoscopic view through the slit lamp with a 90D lens gives you the best appreciation of macular contour and subretinal fluid. The optical section (narrow slit) lets you appreciate retinal thickness changes that would be invisible with direct ophthalmoscopy.
3
What to Look For

Disc: cup-to-disc ratio, disc pallor, NVD, disc oedema, haemorrhages. Macula: foveal reflex, retinal thickening (CMO, DME), subretinal fluid, hard exudates, macular hole (loss of foveal depression), epiretinal membrane (wrinkling of inner retinal surface). Posterior pole: haemorrhages (flame vs dot-blot vs pre-retinal), IRMA, NVE, cotton wool spots.

Reading a Fundus Fluorescein Angiography Report Investigations
1
Phases of FFA

After IV injection of sodium fluorescein, there are four phases: choroidal flush (arterial — choroidal filling, 10–12 seconds); arterial phase (CRA filling, 12–15 seconds); arteriovenous (capillary) phase (15–25 seconds) — this is when the FAZ and capillary detail are best seen; venous phase (25–60 seconds) — veins fill with laminar flow, then complete; and late phase (beyond 5 minutes) — normal fluorescence fades, but leakage persists.

2
Hyperfluorescence Patterns

Hyperfluorescence has four causes: window defect (RPE atrophy allows background choroidal fluorescence to show through — appears early, does not increase in size or intensity, fades with the choroidal phase); pooling (fluid space fills with dye that accumulates — increases in intensity but not area, e.g., PED, CSR dome); leakage (dye exits abnormal vessels — increases in both area and intensity with time, e.g., NVD, NVE, CNV, DME); and staining (dye enters non-fluid tissue — increases slowly, persists late, e.g., scar, disc).

The key exam question: does the hyperfluorescence increase in size? If yes, it is leakage or pooling. If no, it is a window defect or staining.
3
Hypofluorescence Patterns

Hypofluorescence has two causes: blocked fluorescence (something in front of the fluorescent tissue blocks the signal — e.g., blood, exudate, pigment, melanoma — appears dark throughout all phases); and filling defect (absence of normal fluorescence — e.g., capillary non-perfusion, CRAO, choroidal ischaemia — the tissue that should fluoresce simply does not fill).

4
Specific Disease Patterns

DR: microaneurysms as hyperfluorescent dots, NVD/NVE leaking profusely in late frames, IRMA as hyperfluorescent capillaries without leakage, capillary non-perfusion as dark areas. ARMD: classic CNV as early lacy hyperfluorescence with late leakage; window defects in GA. CRVO: delayed venous filling, capillary non-perfusion in ischaemic type, late disc staining. CSCR: smokestack or inkblot pattern of leakage from a single RPE defect.

Interpreting an OCT Macula Report Investigations
1
What OCT Measures

Spectral-domain OCT provides a near-histological cross-section of the retina. The central macular thickness (CMT) is automatically calculated — normal is 210–250 µm (varies by machine and normative database). The macular volume map (ETDRS grid, 9 sectors over 6 mm) provides thickness data across the central, inner, and outer zones. Always compare with the normative colour map — green is normal, red is thick, blue is thin.

2
Identifying Fluid

Intraretinal fluid (IRF) appears as hyporeflective (dark) spaces within the retinal layers — typically in the ONL or INL. It is the most ominous finding in neovascular ARMD and must be treated. Subretinal fluid (SRF) is a dark space between the photoreceptor layer and the RPE — it may indicate CNV or CSR. Sub-RPE fluid is below the RPE line, appearing as an RPE elevation — this is a pigment epithelial detachment (PED).

In clinical practice, IRF drives the treatment decision more than SRF. Many experts accept a small amount of chronic SRF in ARMD if the patient is otherwise stable, but any new IRF triggers re-treatment.
3
Key Structural Findings

Epiretinal membrane: hyperreflective line on the inner retinal surface causing wrinkling or tractional macular oedema. Macular hole: full-thickness discontinuity in the fovea — graded by minimum hole diameter and base diameter. External limiting membrane (ELM) and ellipsoid zone (EZ, inner-outer segment junction) integrity are the best OCT predictors of visual acuity — loss of EZ correlates with photoreceptor loss and predicts poor visual recovery.

4
DR-Specific OCT Findings

In DME: increased CMT, cystoid spaces (CMO), hyperreflective hard exudates with posterior shadowing, disorganisation of retinal inner layers (DRIL — a predictor of poor visual prognosis), and possible subretinal fluid. After anti-VEGF treatment, watch for reduction in CMT and resolution of cystoid spaces. Hard exudates may persist for months even when oedema resolves — they are not an active sign of treatment failure.

Classification of DR

Begin with the modified Airlie House / ETDRS classification: no DR, mild NPDR (microaneurysms only), moderate NPDR (additional lesions not meeting severe criteria), severe NPDR (4-2-1 rule — haemorrhages all 4 quadrants, venous beading 2+ quadrants, IRMA 1+ quadrant; any one criterion sufficient), and PDR (neovascularisation of disc or elsewhere, vitreous haemorrhage, tractional RD). Address CSME as a separate entity at any stage: retinal thickening within 500 µm of foveal centre, hard exudates within 500 µm with adjacent thickening, or thickening >1 DA within 1 DD of centre.

Pathogenesis (Brief)

Pericyte loss leads to microaneurysm formation. Capillary leakage results in hard exudates and oedema. Capillary non-perfusion drives ischaemia and VEGF upregulation. VEGF drives neovascularisation (NVD, NVE), which is fragile and bleeds into the vitreous. Fibrovascular membranes then contract, causing tractional RD.

Management of PDR

Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP): the standard treatment. 1200–1600 burns, 200–500 µm spot size, 0.1 s duration, in the mid and far periphery avoiding the posterior pole. Two to three sessions at 1-week intervals. Reduces severe visual loss by 50% (DRS). Mechanism: ablates ischaemic peripheral retina, reducing VEGF production, causing NVD/NVE regression. Side effects: peripheral field loss, nyctalopia worsening, exacerbation of DME.

Anti-VEGF (Protocol S): ranibizumab non-inferior to PRP at 2 years with better visual outcomes and less DME — an accepted alternative, especially when DME coexists. Requires frequent injections. High loss-to-follow-up risk in non-compliant patients (PRP provides more durable effect with fewer visits).

Vitrectomy indications: non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage (>3 months), tractional RD involving or threatening the macula, combined tractional-rhegmatogenous RD, dense premacular haemorrhage. Pre-op anti-VEGF (bevacizumab 3–7 days prior) to reduce intraoperative bleeding.

Systemic Control

Optimise HbA1c (target <7%), BP (<130/80), and lipids (statins). Fenofibrate (ACCORD-Lipid trial) shown to reduce DR progression independently of lipid-lowering.

2 Discuss the pathogenesis, classification, and treatment of age-related macular degeneration. 10 Marks
Pathogenesis

ARMD results from the interplay of ageing, genetic factors (CFH, ARMS2/HTRA1 polymorphisms — complement pathway dysregulation is central), and environmental factors (smoking, UV, diet). Drusen — lipid-rich deposits between RPE and Bruch's membrane — accumulate, reflecting impaired RPE phagocytic function. Thickening of Bruch's membrane impairs oxygen and nutrient diffusion to the outer retina. In wet ARMD, ischaemia drives VEGF upregulation, stimulating CNV growth through breaks in Bruch's membrane.

Classification

Early ARMD: small-medium drusen (<125 µm), no pigmentary changes. Intermediate ARMD: large drusen (>125 µm) or pigmentary changes. Late ARMD: geographic atrophy (dry, advanced) or CNV (wet, advanced). Describe drusen types (hard/soft/reticular), CNV types (classic/occult/Type 1/2/3 on OCT), and FFA/OCT patterns.

Treatment

Dry ARMD: AREDS2 supplements (zinc 80 mg + copper 2 mg + vitamins C, E + lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg) for intermediate ARMD; reduces progression to advanced by 25%. No treatment for GA currently licensed (complement inhibitors in trials — pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol).

Wet ARMD: intravitreal anti-VEGF — ranibizumab 0.5 mg, bevacizumab 1.25 mg, aflibercept 2 mg, or brolucizumab 6 mg. Loading 3 monthly doses then treat-and-extend. PDT with verteporfin for select cases (PCV combined with anti-VEGF, chronic CSR at half-fluence). Monitoring: OCT at each visit — treat if IRF present; debate regarding chronic SRF.

3 Describe the types of retinal detachment with their clinical features and surgical management of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. 10 Marks
Types of Retinal Detachment

Three types: Rhegmatogenous (break-related — most common; subretinal fluid from liquefied vitreous through full-thickness break; convex, corrugated, mobile; Shafer's sign). Tractional (fibrovascular membranes — diabetic, sickle cell, ROP; concave, immobile, no break; no shifting fluid). Exudative/serous (choroidal disease — tumour, scleritis, VKH, Coats; shifting fluid; no break; treat underlying cause).

Clinical Features of RRD

Symptoms: photopsia (flashes from vitreoretinal traction), floaters (PVD/vitreous haemorrhage), curtain/shadow (spreading as detachment advances). Signs: Shafer's sign (tobacco dust in vitreous on slit lamp), corrugated elevated retina, reduced IOP in the affected eye, demarcation line in chronic RD.

Surgical Management of RRD

Three techniques: (1) Scleral buckling — encircling or segmental silicone band sutured to sclera, indents eye wall to close break; cryotherapy to break; drain SRF if needed; good for young phakic patients, inferior breaks, lattice degeneration without PVD. (2) Pars plana vitrectomy — vitreous removal, traction relief, SRF drainage via break, laser retinopexy, gas/silicone tamponade. Preferred for posterior breaks, giant tears, PVR, vitreous haemorrhage. (3) Pneumatic retinopexy — expansile gas injection + laser/cryo; suitable for superior breaks in phakic eyes; success rate ~70%, lower than PPV/SB. Tamponade gases: SF6 (2–3 weeks), C3F8 (6–8 weeks); silicone oil for complex detachments requiring permanent tamponade.

Prognosis

Macula-on RD repaired within 24 hours: >80% achieve 6/12 or better. Macula-off detached >7 days: poor visual prognosis even with anatomical success. Primary reattachment rate: 90% for SB, 85–95% for PPV.

4 Write a short note on retinopathy of prematurity — classification, screening, and treatment. 10 Marks
Pathogenesis

Retinal vascularisation proceeds centrifugally from disc to periphery from 16 to 40 weeks gestation. In premature infants, the avascular peripheral retina is exposed to high NICU oxygen, suppressing VEGF. When the infant transitions to room air, relative hypoxia of the avascular retina triggers a VEGF surge — driving abnormal neovascularisation at the vascular-avascular junction.

ICROP3 Classification

Zones (location of avascular retina): I = innermost circle (radius = 2× disc-to-macula distance); II = from Zone I edge to nasal ora and around; III = remaining temporal crescent. Stages: 1 = demarcation line; 2 = ridge; 3 = ridge + extraretinal fibrovascular proliferation; 4A/B = partial RD (fovea on/off); 5 = total RD. Plus disease = vascular dilation and tortuosity in 2+ quadrants. AP-ROP = flat neovascularisation in Zone I/posterior Zone II, rapid progression.

Screening (India)

All infants: birth weight <2000 g or GA <34 weeks, or above with unstable clinical course. First exam at 4 weeks postnatal age or 31 weeks PMA, whichever later. Frequency determined by initial findings.

Treatment

Type 1 ROP (treat): Zone I any stage with plus; Zone I Stage 3 without plus; Zone II Stage 2 or 3 with plus. Laser photocoagulation: ablates avascular retina; peripheral field loss. Intravitreal bevacizumab: faster regression, retinal vessels complete development, no peripheral field loss, but higher late recurrence risk (follow to 65 weeks PMA). Stages 4–5: vitrectomy (lens-sparing vitrectomy for 4A, lensectomy for 4B/5).

5 Compare central retinal vein occlusion and branch retinal vein occlusion regarding aetiology, clinical features, and management. 10 Marks
Aetiology

CRVO: occlusion at or behind lamina cribrosa where artery and vein share adventitia. Main causes: arterial hypertension (most common), hyperviscosity states (polycythaemia, Waldenström's, cryoglobulinaemia), glaucoma (raised IOP compresses vein at lamina), oral contraceptives, thrombophilia. BRVO: occlusion at AV crossing where artery crosses over vein; arterial stiffening compresses vein. Superotemporal arcade most common. Risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, atherosclerosis.

Clinical Features

CRVO: sudden painless visual loss (unilateral), may be near-normal if non-ischaemic. Fundus: flame haemorrhages all 4 quadrants, dilated tortuous veins, multiple CWS, disc swelling — "blood and thunder" or "stormy sunset" fundus. BRVO: sectoral flame haemorrhages in one quadrant (superotemporal), may have CWS, no disc swelling. VA depends on macular involvement. Ischaemic vs non-ischaemic CRVO: >10 DD capillary non-perfusion, reduced ERG b:a ratio = ischaemic; risk of rubeosis and NVG.

Investigations

FFA: extent of capillary non-perfusion, macular oedema patterns. OCT: macular thickness, cystoid spaces, SRF. ERG (CRVO): b:a wave ratio to classify ischaemic vs non-ischaemic. Systemic workup: BP, lipids, FBC, coagulation, glucose, thrombophilia screen (especially in young patients).

Management

Macular oedema: intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept) monthly PRN — evidence from CRUISE (CRVO) and BRAVO (BRVO) trials. Dexamethasone implant (Ozurdex) for pseudophakic eyes or anti-VEGF non-responders. Grid laser macular photocoagulation: second-line for BRVO (BVOS trial). Neovascularisation: PRP if NVD/NVE/rubeosis develops. Systemic treatment of underlying cause. CRVO monitoring: monthly gonioscopy for rubeosis for 6 months.

NEET PG 2020 Cherry red spot at macula is NOT seen in: NEET PG
Answer: Diabetic retinopathy

Cherry red spot is seen in: CRAO (most classic), Tay-Sachs disease (GM2 gangliosidosis), Niemann-Pick disease (sphingomyelinase deficiency), Sandhoff disease, and methanol poisoning. It is NOT seen in diabetic retinopathy. DNB Dec 2018 The first histological change in diabetic retinopathy is: DNB
Answer: Loss of pericytes (pericyte dropout)

The earliest histological change in diabetic retinopathy is selective loss of intramural pericytes — the cells that provide structural support to the capillary wall and regulate autoregulation of retinal blood flow. Pericyte loss precedes all clinical signs. Without pericyte support, the capillary wall weakens, leading to microaneurysm formation (the earliest clinical sign on ophthalmoscopy). The earliest clinical sign is microaneurysm; the earliest histological sign is pericyte loss. Do not confuse the two.

AIIMS May 2017 In Best vitelliform macular dystrophy, which investigation is characteristically abnormal even in a carrier with a normal-looking fundus? AIIMS PG
Answer: Electro-oculogram (EOG) — Arden ratio below 1.85 (pathological below 1.5)

BEST1 (bestrophin) is a chloride channel in the RPE that is required for the light-evoked increase in RPE membrane potential — the basis of the EOG light rise. In Best disease, this light rise is absent or severely reduced, giving an abnormal Arden ratio (light peak:dark trough). The ERG is typically normal in Best disease. The EOG is abnormal in all carriers — even those with no visible fundus changes — making it the most sensitive diagnostic test for this condition and its autosomal dominant inheritance.

NEET PG 2022 Which of the following is NOT a criterion for clinically significant macular oedema (CSME) in diabetic retinopathy? NEET PG
Answer: Hard exudates within 1 disc diameter of the foveal centre without associated retinal thickening

The three ETDRS criteria for CSME are: (1) retinal thickening within 500 µm of foveal centre; (2) hard exudates within 500 µm of foveal centre WITH adjacent retinal thickening; (3) retinal thickening of one disc area or more, any part of which is within one disc diameter of the centre. Key point: hard exudates alone without adjacent thickening do NOT constitute CSME — the thickening must accompany the exudate. The threshold is 500 µm (not 1 DD) for proximity to foveal centre in criteria 1 and 2.

DNB June 2021 A 26-week premature infant has Zone I Stage 3 ROP without plus disease on examination at 4 weeks postnatal age. What is the management? DNB
Answer: Treat — this is Type 1 ROP

Zone I Stage 3 without plus disease meets the criteria for Type 1 ROP (ETROP study) and requires treatment. This is a common trap in exams — students may think that "without plus" means observation, but the Zone I Stage 3 criterion does NOT require plus disease to mandate treatment. The ETROP study showed that early treatment of Type 1 ROP (before plus disease fully develops) gives better visual outcomes than waiting for threshold disease. AIIMS Nov 2016 In rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, the subretinal fluid is situated between which layers? AIIMS PG
Answer: Between the photoreceptor layer (neurosensory retina) and the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)

The "subretinal space" in RRD is actually the potential space between the outer segment of the photoreceptors and the microvilli of the RPE — the plane of embryological separation between the inner and outer layers of the optic cup. This space does not normally exist because the photoreceptors and RPE are adherent. When fluid enters through a break, it accumulates in this plane, separating the neurosensory retina from the RPE and cutting off the photoreceptors from their choroidal oxygen supply. This is why rapid re-attachment is essential to prevent photoreceptor death.

NEET PG 2021 The smokestack pattern on FFA is characteristic of which condition? NEET PG
Answer: Central serous chorioretinopathy (CSR / CSCR)

The smokestack pattern on FFA is pathognomonic of CSR. It results from a focal RPE defect (pinpoint leak) through which dye enters the subretinal space, rises vertically under the dome of subretinal fluid (driven by hydrostatic currents), and then spreads horizontally as it approaches the roof of the dome — resembling smoke from a chimney. It is seen in about 10% of acute CSR cases; the more common FFA pattern is "inkblot" (centrifugal spread from the leak without upward deflection). Both patterns confirm active CSR but the smokestack is the classic exam answer.
