Phacoemulsification (Phaco)

UGPG
▼

Surgical Steps

  • Clear corneal incision: 2.8–3.0 mm, self-sealing
  • Capsulotomy (Capsulorhexis): Continuous circular tear in anterior lens capsule (4.5–5.5 mm diameter); allows controlled dissection
  • Hydrodissection: Inject balanced saline under capsule to separate nucleus from cortex
  • Nucleus fraction (Nucleofractis): Ultrasonic phacoemulsification breaks hard nucleus into fragments
  • Cortical aspiration: Aspirate residual cortical material after nucleus removal
  • IOL (Intraocular lens) implantation: Insert foldable IOL (acrylic, silicone) into capsular bag; power calculated pre-op (A-scan or optical biometry)

IOL Types

  • Monofocal: Single focus (distance most common); requires glasses for near
  • Multifocal: Multiple foci (distance + intermediate + near); halos, reduced contrast sensitivity
  • Toric: Corrects astigmatism
  • Phakic IOL: In phakic eye for high refractive errors (myopia, hyperopia)

Complications

  • Posterior capsule rupture (PCR): Most common intra-op complication (1–3%); requires vitrectomy; vision usually good if managed promptly
  • Dropped nucleus: PCR + nucleus falls into vitreous; PPV removal necessary
  • Descemet's detachment: Corneal endothelial touch; can cause corneal oedema; may need graft (DMEK/DSAEK)
  • Epithelial ingrowth: Epithelial cells grow into anterior chamber; corneal opacification
  • IOL decentration: Zonular damage; may need recenter or exchange
  • Endophthalmitis: Rare (~0.1%); managed with intravitreal antibiotics

Trabeculectomy (Glaucoma Surgery)

PG
▼
Fig 17.1 — Trabeculectomy: creating a filtration bleb

Surgical Technique

  • Full-thickness (GTT) vs Partial-thickness: Partial safer (reduced hypotony risk); full-thickness more effective
  • Sclerectomy: Remove full or partial thickness of sclera (3×3 mm) to allow aqueous into subconjunctival space
  • Peripheral iridectomy: Remove iris root tissue to maintain aqueous flow
  • Conjunctival closure: Watertight; good Tenon's dissection

Antimetabolites (Adjunctive)

  • Mitomycin C (MMC): 0.4–0.5 mg/mL for 2–3 min intra-op; MORE potent; better long-term IOP control; risk of hypotony, scarring
  • 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU): 5 mg/mL; less potent; used peri-op or post-op; better side-effect profile
  • Indicated in: young age, previous failed surgery, secondary glaucoma, aphakic glaucoma

Bleb Complications

  • Bleb leak: Aqueous egress from bleb edge; risk of flat anterior chamber, endophthalmitis; managed with: soft contact lens, aqueous suppressants, needle repositioning, or suture repair
  • Blebitis: Infection of bleb tissue (conjunctivitis-like); treated with topical antibiotics
  • Endophthalmitis: Bleb infection descends to globe; emergency intravitreal antibiotics, vitrectomy
  • Hypotony: IOP <5 mmHg; shallowed AC, vision loss; may need bleb needling or tube revision
  • Encapsulated bleb: Fibrotic response around bleb; can redevelop high IOP; may need 5-FU injection or revision

Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR)

UG
▼

Indications

  • Nasolacrimal duct (NLD) obstruction (congenital or acquired)
  • Chronic dacryocystitis unresponsive to probing/irrigation
  • NLD stricture from canaliculitis

Types

  • External DCR: Skin incision at medial canthus; traditional; good access; visible scar
  • Endoscopic DCR: Transnasal approach via nasal endoscope; no external scar; faster recovery; lower morbidity; preferred in many centers

Technique

  • Create osteotomy: Remove bone from medial orbital wall + nasal lateral wall (ethmoid)
  • Incise lacrimal sac + nasal mucosa: Create wide anastomosis between sac and nasal mucosa (middle meatus preferred)
  • Silicone intubation: Place bicanalicular silicone stent (Crawford tube) to keep duct patent during healing (4–6 weeks); prevents scarring

Success Rate

  • External DCR: 85–95% patent at 1 year
  • Endoscopic DCR: 70–90% (depends on technique, surgeon experience)
  • Failure may require revision or alternate modality (canalicular stent, Jones tube)

Vitreoretinal Surgery — Pars Plana Vitrectomy (PPV)

PG
▼

Indications

  • Vitreous haemorrhage (non-clearing)
  • Retinal detachment repair (RRD, tractional RD)
  • Epiretinal membrane (ERM) / macular pucker
  • Macular hole (idiopathic, traumatic)
  • Diabetic vitreous haemorrhage / tractional RD
  • Endophthalmitis (diagnostic + therapeutic)

Surgical Setup

  • 3-port 23/25G or 27G: Ports placed at pars plana (usually 3.5–4 mm from limbus)
  • Sclerotomy ports: Infusion (IOP stabilization), vitrector (cutting), light probe
  • Wide-angle viewing system: Direct or indirect lens; visualize entire retina

Tamponade (Internal Support)

  • C3F8 (perfluoropropane) gas: Expands 4×; lasts ~6 weeks; requires head positioning (gas bubble floats to apical area)
  • SF6 (sulfur hexafluoride): Expands 2×; lasts ~2 weeks; longer than C3F8 in some cases
  • Silicone oil: Permanent; used in complex cases; requires removal after 3+ months; associated with cataract, glaucoma, oil emulsification

Key Complications

  • Cataract: Post-op (phacoemulsification later)
  • Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR): Fibrotic contraction; risk of re-detachment
  • Silicone oil complications: Band keratopathy, emulsification, raised IOP, keratitis
  • Endophthalmitis: Rare but serious; treated with intravitreal antibiotics

Key Considerations

  • Donor selection: Cornea from deceased donor (eye bank); endothelial cell count important (normally >2000/mm²; minimum >400 for DMEK)
  • Rejection: Immune response targeting graft; risk increases with vascularization, ocular inflammation history, PKP (vs DMEK)
  • Management of rejection: High-dose topical + systemic steroids; may require regraft

Common Laser Procedures

  • Nd:YAG Posterior Capsulotomy: For PCO (posterior capsular opacification) post-cataract surgery; creates opening in opacified posterior capsule; restores visual axis
  • Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (PI): Nd:YAG or argon; creates small opening in iris for PACG; equalizes anterior-posterior chamber pressures
  • Panretinal Photocoagulation (PRP): Argon or diode; PDR treatment; multiple burns across retina → destroys ischemic retina → reduces VEGF, prevents neovascularization
  • Focal/Grid Laser: Argon for CSME; direct leaking microaneurysms + grid pattern over macular oedema area
  • SLT: Non-destructive; selective treatment of pigmented trabecular cells; IOP reduction 20–30%; repeatable

Strabismus Surgery & Other Procedures

UG
▼

Muscle Surgery Types

  • Recession: Weaken muscle; reattach insertion posteriorly (toward insertion site); used for overacting muscles
  • Resection: Strengthen muscle; remove section of muscle and reattach more anteriorly; used for underacting muscles
  • Adjustable sutures: Allow fine-tuning of muscle strength post-op (revision without re-surgery)
  • Faden procedure: Place suture behind muscle insertion; reduces action over time; used for restrictive strabismus

Lateral Tarsal Strip

  • For lower lid ectropion or entropion (involutional, paralytic)
  • Surgical technique: Remove lateral third of tarsal plate, shorten, reattach; tightens lower lid

MCQ Bank — Ophthalmic Operations

10 questions · ~25 min
Q1 UG
Which anaesthesia is MOST commonly used for phacoemulsification?
ATopical (lidocaine drops)
BRetrobulbar
CGeneral anaesthesia
DPeribulbar
Correct Answer: A — Topical
Topical anaesthesia (lidocaine 4% drops + gel) is most common for phaco: safe, rapid onset, minimal systemic effects. Peribulbar/retrobulbar used for longer/complex cases. General reserved for pediatric or uncooperative patients.
Q2 UG
Posterior capsule rupture during phaco occurs in ~1–3% of cases. What is the immediate management?
AConvert to ECCE and complete phaco later
BInject viscoelastic, perform anterior vitrectomy, complete surgery and IOL implantation
CAbandon surgery and refer to vitreoretinal surgeon
DLeave the capsule as is; no intervention needed
Correct Answer: B — Inject viscoelastic, perform anterior vitrectomy, complete surgery
PCR is NOT an emergency to abandon. Manage in-situ: inject viscoelastic to seal defect, perform small anterior vitrectomy at wound/anterior chamber, complete nucleus removal and cortical aspiration, and implant IOL (in bag if possible, or sulcus). Vitrectomy only if nucleus falls into vitreous.
Q3 PG
Which antimetabolite is MORE potent in trabeculectomy — MMC or 5-FU?
A5-FU (stronger)
BMitomycin C (stronger)
CBoth equally potent
DDepends on concentration used
Correct Answer: B — Mitomycin C (stronger)
MMC is more potent than 5-FU; achieves better long-term IOP control. However, MMC carries higher risk of hypotony and late scarring. 5-FU is safer with fewer side effects but less effective. Choice depends on indication and risk factors.
Q4 UG
Bleb infection that descends to the globe is called:
ABlebitis only
BConjunctivitis
CBleb leak
DEndophthalmitis
Correct Answer: D — Endophthalmitis
Blebitis = infection of bleb tissue (conjunctivitis-like); treated with topical antibiotics. If infection descends through bleb into anterior chamber/vitreous → endophthalmitis: emergency intravitreal antibiotics + vitrectomy.
Q5 PG
Which corneal transplant type has the LOWEST rejection rate?
APKP (penetrating keratoplasty)
BDSAEK
CDMEK (Descemet membrane endothelial)
DDALK (deep anterior lamellar)
Correct Answer: C — DMEK
DMEK (ultra-thin posterior transplant) has <5% rejection rate at 5 years — lowest of all keratoplasty types. DALK <1%; PKP 10–25%; DSAEK 5–10%. DMEK also gives best visual outcomes but has steepest learning curve.
Q6 UG
C3F8 gas expands how many times in the eye?
A4×; lasts ~6 weeks
B2×; lasts ~2 weeks
C6×; lasts ~10 weeks
DDoesn't expand; permanent
Correct Answer: A — 4×; lasts ~6 weeks
C3F8 (perfluoropropane) expands 4× its initial volume; duration ~6 weeks. SF6 expands 2×; duration ~2 weeks. Silicone oil is permanent (requires removal after 3+ months). Head positioning critical to keep bubble apical during reattachment.
Q7 PG
SLT (Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty) targets which structure in glaucoma?
ACiliary body (non-selective)
BPigmented trabecular meshwork cells
CSchlemm's canal directly
DIris tissue
Correct Answer: B — Pigmented trabecular meshwork cells
SLT is "selective": targets pigmented TM cells preferentially (Q-switched Nd:YAG, 532 nm green). Non-destructive; IOP reduction ~20–30%; POAG, PACG (adjunct); repeatable. Argon laser (non-selective) destroys full TM thickness.
Q8 UG
The most common cause of dimished vision post-phaco is:
AIOL decentration
BEndophthalmitis
CPosterior capsule opacification (PCO)
DAstigmatism from surgical wound
Correct Answer: C — PCO (Posterior Capsule Opacification)
PCO develops in 20–50% within 1–2 years post-phaco (LEC migration, fibrosis). Treated with Nd:YAG posterior capsulotomy. Other common post-op issues: refractive astigmatism, dry eye, but PCO is leading cause of visual complaint requiring intervention.
Q9 UG
In trabeculectomy, recession weakens a muscle; resection strengthens. Which procedure is used in strabismus surgery for underacting muscle?
AResection (strengthen)
BRecession (weaken)
CTenotomy
DMyectomy
Correct Answer: A — Resection (strengthen)
Resection = remove section of muscle, reattach more anteriorly → strengthens action. Used for underacting (weak) muscles. Recession = reattach insertion posteriorly → weakens action. Used for overacting (tight) muscles.
Q10 PG
Which laser procedure requires head positioning to keep the bubble apical during retinal reattachment?
ANd:YAG posterior capsulotomy
BPRP (pan-retinal photocoagulation)
CPars plana vitrectomy with C3F8/SF6 tamponade
DSLT for glaucoma
Correct Answer: C — PPV with gas tamponade
PPV with expandable gas (C3F8, SF6) requires strict head positioning to keep the gas bubble against the detachment site (apical retina) for adequate reattachment support. Gas bubble floats due to buoyancy; proper positioning ensures apical placement.

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Describe the steps of phacoemulsification and list major intra-operative complications. (8 marks)
Steps: CCI → capsulorhexis → hydrodissection → phacoemulsification → cortical aspiration → IOL implantation. Complications: PCR, dropped nucleus, Descemet's detachment, IOL decentration, epithelial ingrowth, endophthalmitis.
University Final MBBS
A 60-year-old with glaucoma is scheduled for trabeculectomy. Discuss indications, surgical technique, and bleb-related complications. (10 marks)
Indications: POAG uncontrolled on meds. Technique: sclerectomy + peripheral iridectomy + conjunctival closure. Complications: bleb leak, blebitis, endophthalmitis, hypotony, encapsulated bleb. Management stratified by severity.

Anaesthesia at a Glance

  • Topical: Immediate; phaco (most common)
  • Sub-Tenon's: 3–5 min; good akinesia; safe
  • Peribulbar: 10–15 min; excellent akinesia
  • Retrobulbar: 5–10 min; most complete akinesia; highest risk (perforation, brainstem anaesthesia)
  • General: Pediatric, emergency, uncooperative

Phaco Key Steps

  • CCI → Capsulorhexis → Hydrodissection
  • Nucleofractis (phacoemulsification)
  • Cortical aspiration → IOL implantation
  • PCR rate: 1–3% (manage in-situ with vitrectomy)
  • Most common post-op issue: PCO (20–50%)

Keratoplasty Quick Ref

  • PKP: Full thickness; 10–25% rejection
  • DALK: Anterior; preserves endothelium; <1% rejection
  • DSAEK: Posterior; 5–10% rejection
  • DMEK: Ultra-thin posterior; <5% rejection; best visual

Gas Tamponade

  • C3F8: 4× expansion; ~6 weeks
  • SF6: 2× expansion; ~2 weeks
  • Silicone oil: Permanent; remove after 3+ months
  • Head positioning critical for apical support
