Chapter 7

Glaucoma

Optic neuropathy with progressive disc and visual field changes. Classification (POAG, PACG, NTG, secondary, congenital), diagnosis, and management.

UG Level PG Level High-Yield Critical

Definition & Pathophysiology

UGPG
▼
Definition: Glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy characterized by a triad: (1) IOP >21 mmHg, (2) optic disc changes, (3) visual field defects. NOT all patients with elevated IOP have glaucoma; NOT all glaucoma patients have elevated IOP.

Aqueous Dynamics (Pathophysiology)

  • Production: Ciliary epithelium secretes ~2 µL/min; primarily active secretion (Na-K ATPase)
  • Drainage:
    • Trabecular (conventional) pathway (85%): Via trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal → collector channels → episcleral veins
    • Uveoscleral (unconventional) pathway (15%): Via ciliary muscle bundles → suprachoroidal space → exit through sclera
  • IOP = (Aqueous production − Aqueous drainage) + Episcleral venous pressure
  • Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg; diurnal variation up to 5 mmHg
IOP Dynamics Mnemonic
85:15 rule = Trabecular 85% : Uveoscleral 15% aqueous drainage

Primary Open Angle Glaucoma (POAG)

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼
Most common type (60–70% of glaucoma). Insidious onset; patients often unaware until advanced.

Clinical Features

  • Painless vision loss (no acute symptoms)
  • Bilateral but asymmetrical
  • Risk factors: Age, family Hx, African descent, myopia, elevated IOP, thin cornea, low perfusion pressure

Optic Disc Changes (Progressive)

  • Cup-to-disc (C:D) ratio ≥0.6 (suspect glaucoma; normal ≤0.3–0.4)
  • Asymmetry >0.2 between eyes is significant
  • Vertical elongation of cup (not horizontal)
  • Disc haemorrhages at margin (Drance haemorrhages)
  • RNFL (retinal nerve fibre layer) loss — thinning/vertical pallor
  • Baring of circumlinear vessel — innermost rim vessel exposed at disc edge
  • Notching at superior/inferior poles (glaucomatous notch)
  • ISNT rule: Inferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal rim thickness. Violation = suspect glaucoma

Visual Field Defects (Progression Pattern)

  • Early: Nasal step (peripheral nasal loss), paracentral scotoma
  • Mid: Bjerrum scotoma (arcuate defect in Bjerrum area)
  • Arcuate scotoma from superior/inferior pole → follows RNFL path
  • Ring scotoma — complete ring around fixation
  • Central island spared in advanced disease (rod vision preserved)
  • Total VF loss — end-stage
️ Exam Pearl:Bjerrum scotoma (arcuate defect in Bjerrum area, 10–20 degrees from fixation) is PATHOGNOMONIC for POAG. Most commonly asked VF defect.

Primary Angle Closure Glaucoma (PACG) — Acute Attack

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼
Ophthalmic Emergency. IOP >50 mmHg; permanent vision loss without urgent treatment.

Risk Factors (Anatomical)

  • Hyperopic eye (shorter axial length) — most important
  • Shallow anterior chamber (A-V distance <2.5 mm)
  • Thick lens (age, nuclear sclerosis)
  • Forward iris bowing (pupil block)
  • Female > male (2–4:1)
  • Age >50 years

Treatment Sequence (Emergency Protocol)

  1. IV Acetazolamide 500 mg IV (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor; reduces aqueous production)
  2. Topical Beta-blocker: Timolol 0.5% TDS (reduces aqueous production; also increases uveoscleral drainage)
  3. Topical Alpha-2 agonist: Brimonidine TDS (reduces aqueous production; avoid in acute attack if systemically unwell)
  4. Topical Prostaglandin analogue: Latanoprost 0.005% at night (increases uveoscleral drainage)
  5. Topical Steroid: Dexamethasone/prednisolone 1% QID (reduce inflammation)
  6. Miotics (Pilocarpine 2% TID): Constrict pupil, deepen AC; use AFTER IOP <40 mmHg and cornea clear (earlier use worsens angle closure by forward lens iris diaphragm)
  7. Definitive: Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (once IOP controlled and cornea clear; prevents recurrence)
️ Pitfall: Do NOT use pilocarpine first in acute PACG. Use it ONLY after IOP controlled and cornea clear. Early pilocarpine worsens attack by forward lens iris diaphragm.

Normal Tension Glaucoma (NTG)

PG
▼
Definition: Optic disc and visual field changes consistent with glaucoma but IOP ≤21 mmHg (untreated). Accounts for 5–10% of glaucoma; more common in Asian populations.

Pathophysiology

  • Primary mechanism: Vascular insufficiency (ischaemia) rather than mechanical IOP-related damage
  • Vasospastic dysfunction: Poor autoregulation of optic nerve head perfusion; exaggerated response to IOP or systemic BP changes
  • Flammer syndrome: Association with cold extremities, low perfusion pressure, migraine
  • Risk factors: Female, Japanese/Asian descent, migraine, Raynaud's phenomenon, low systemic BP

Clinical Features

  • IOP always ≤21 mmHg (multiple readings needed to exclude high-tension glaucoma with diurnal variation)
  • Optic disc changes similar to POAG (but often more focal, deeper cupping)
  • VF defects: Often nasal steps or paracentral scotomas; central defects more common than in POAG
  • May progress despite IOP control (due to vascular component)

Management

  • Systemic BP control: Avoid aggressive BP lowering (preserve perfusion pressure)
  • IOP reduction: Even modest reduction (25%) may slow progression; use prostaglandin analogues (increase uveoscleral flow)
  • Optimize vascular perfusion: Address migraine, vasospasm; avoid vasoconstrictors

Congenital Glaucoma (Buphthalmos)

UG
▼
Definition: Developmental abnormality of the trabecular meshwork/Schwalbe's line. Presents in infancy (<3 years usually).

Classic Triad

  • Photophobia (light sensitivity; infant squints, irritable in bright light)
  • Epiphora (excessive tearing; not from blocked nasolacrimal duct but pain response)
  • Blepharospasm (eye closure from pain)

Clinical Signs

  • Large cornea (>12 mm) — eye stretches due to elevated IOP in infancy (sclera is elastic)
  • Haab's striae — horizontal breaks in Descemet's membrane from corneal stretching
  • Horizontal corneal striae (may appear as linear scars)
  • Deep optic cupping — disc enlargement and deepening of cup
  • Elevated IOP — often >30 mmHg

Management

  • Goniotomy (angle surgery): Incise abnormal Schwalbe's line; success rate ~50–80%; first-line surgical treatment
  • Trabeculotomy: Ab-externo approach; success ~70–80%; often combined with goniotomy
  • Trabeculectomy: If goniotomy/trabeculotomy fail; more invasive
  • Medical management: Topical prostaglandin analogues, beta-blockers, CAI as bridge to surgery
  • Early intervention crucial: Prevent permanent optic atrophy and blindness

Treatment Algorithm

  • First-line (monotherapy): Prostaglandin analogue (latanoprost — best efficacy, once-daily dosing)
  • Dual therapy: Prostaglandin + Beta-blocker (timolol) or Prostaglandin + CAI
  • Triple therapy: Prostaglandin + Beta-blocker + CAI (or Alpha-2 agonist)
  • Laser/Surgery: If medical therapy fails or IOP target not met

Surgical & Laser Management

PG
▼

Laser Procedures

  • Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI): Nd:YAG or argon; creates opening in iris periphery → deepens AC angle → relieved angle-closure. Gold standard for acute/chronic PACG.
  • Laser Trabeculoplasty: Argon (ALT) or Selective (SLT); stimulates TM cells → ↑ aqueous outflow by ~20%; can be repeated.
  • Cyclophotocoagulation: Destroys ciliary body epithelium → ↓ aqueous production; palliative for advanced glaucoma or phthisical eyes.

Surgical Procedures

  • Trabeculectomy: Gold standard for POAG; creates fistula from AC to subconjunctival space (filtering bleb). Success ~70–80% at 5 years. Enhanced with Mitomycin C (MMC) or 5-FU to prevent scarring.
  • MIGS (Minimally Invasive Glaucoma Surgery): iStent (trabecular micro-bypass), Hydrus (supraciliary device), XEN (gel implant). Lower IOP reduction than trabeculectomy but fewer complications; good for mild-moderate glaucoma.
  • Cyclodiode/Cyclodestructive procedures: For refractory glaucoma (phthisical eyes, neovascular glaucoma); last resort.

Diagnostic Investigations & Monitoring

UGPG
▼
  • Tonometry: Goldmann applanation (gold standard); normal 10–21 mmHg. Also Rebound (NCT), Pneumotonometry.
  • Gonioscopy: Grade angle opening (Shaffer classification); essential to differentiate open-angle from angle-closure.
  • Optic Disc Assessment: C:D ratio, disc photography, ISNT rule, disc haemorrhages, RNFL thinning.
  • Visual Field (Perimetry): Standard Automated Perimetry (SAP); detects scotomas (nasal step, Bjerrum, arcuate, ring); monitors progression.
  • OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography): Quantifies RNFL thickness, macular volume; excellent for monitoring; may detect changes before visual field.
  • Fundus Photography: Serial imaging to detect disc changes, haemorrhages, pallor.
  • Corneal Pachymetry: Measure corneal thickness; thin cornea (<555 µm) = risk for glaucoma (same IOP causes more damage).

MCQ Bank — Glaucoma

14 questions · ~28 min
Q1 UG
What percentage of aqueous humour drains through the trabecular (conventional) pathway?
A15%
B85%
C50%
D100%
Correct Answer: B — 85%
85% drains via trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal → collector channels → episcleral veins. 15% via uveoscleral (unconventional) pathway through ciliary muscle bundles. This is the 85:15 rule.
Q2 UG
Which is the most common type of glaucoma?
APrimary angle-closure glaucoma
BNormal tension glaucoma
CCongenital glaucoma
DPrimary open-angle glaucoma
Correct Answer: D — POAG
POAG accounts for 60–70% of all glaucoma cases. It is insidious, painless, and bilateral but asymmetrical. Patients often present late with advanced disease.
Q3 PG
Which finding on optic disc examination is PATHOGNOMONIC for primary open-angle glaucoma?
AOptic disc pallor
BDisc hyperaemia
CBjerrum scotoma on visual field
DPapilloedema
Correct Answer: C — Bjerrum scotoma
Bjerrum scotoma (arcuate defect in Bjerrum area, 10–20 degrees from fixation) is PATHOGNOMONIC for POAG. It follows the RNFL distribution and is characteristic of glaucomatous VF loss. This is a high-yield exam finding.
Q4 UG
In primary angle-closure glaucoma, what is the typical IOP during acute attack?
A>50 mmHg (often 40–80 mmHg)
B21–30 mmHg
C30–40 mmHg
D<10 mmHg
Correct Answer: A — >50 mmHg
In acute PACG, IOP can exceed 50 mmHg (often 40–80 mmHg). This extreme elevation causes corneal oedema ("frosted glass"), fixed mid-dilated pupil, and ischemic iris changes. Requires urgent treatment.
Q5 PG
What is the first-line systemic agent in acute angle-closure glaucoma?
AMannitol IV
BAcetazolamide 500 mg IV
CPilocarpine 2% eye drops
DDexamethasone 8 mg IV
Correct Answer: B — Acetazolamide 500 mg IV
IV Acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) is the first systemic agent; reduces aqueous production rapidly. Followed by topical beta-blockers, alpha-2 agonists, and prostaglandins. Pilocarpine is used ONLY after IOP is controlled and cornea is clear.
Q6 UG
The ISNT rule in glaucoma refers to:
AInferior chamber angle opening
BIntraocular pressure temporal change
CIris sphincter thickness
DInferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal rim thickness
Correct Answer: D — ISNT rule (rim thickness order)
ISNT rule states normal neuroretinal rim thickness follows: Inferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal. Violation of this rule (e.g., Temporal ≥ Inferior) is suspicious for glaucoma. Used to screen for early POAG.
Q7 PG
Which drug is the first-line monotherapy for primary open-angle glaucoma?
ALatanoprost (prostaglandin analogue)
BTimolol (beta-blocker)
CDorzolamide (CAI)
DPilocarpine (miotic)
Correct Answer: A — Latanoprost
Prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost, bimatoprost, travoprost) are first-line monotherapy. They are the most effective (25–35% IOP reduction), well-tolerated, and dosed once daily (at night). Beta-blockers are second-line if PG contraindicated.
Q8 UG
The classic triad of congenital glaucoma (buphthalmos) includes all EXCEPT:
APhotophobia
BEpiphora
CMydriasis (pupil dilation)
DBlepharospasm
Correct Answer: C — Mydriasis
Classic triad: Photophobia, Epiphora, Blepharospasm (from corneal pain due to high IOP). NOT mydriasis. Also present: large cornea (>12 mm), Haab's striae (corneal stretching marks), deep cupping, elevated IOP.
Q9 PG
In normal tension glaucoma, which mechanism is MOST important?
AVascular insufficiency (ischaemia) and vasospasm
BElevated intraocular pressure
CTrabecular meshwork obstruction
DAngle closure
Correct Answer: A — Vascular insufficiency
NTG is driven by vascular insufficiency and poor perfusion (ischaemia), NOT elevated IOP. Vasospastic dysfunction is key. Associated with Flammer syndrome, migraine, low BP. Management focuses on improving perfusion and modest IOP reduction.
Q10 UG
What is the first-line surgical procedure for primary open-angle glaucoma?
ALaser trabeculoplasty
BTrabeculectomy
CCyclophotocoagulation
DPeripheral iridotomy
Correct Answer: B — Trabeculectomy
Trabeculectomy is the gold standard surgical procedure for POAG. It creates a filtering bleb from AC to subconjunctival space; ~70–80% success rate at 5 years. Can be enhanced with MMC or 5-FU. MIGS procedures are newer alternatives with lower IOP reduction but fewer complications.
Q11 PG
In acute angle-closure glaucoma, when should pilocarpine be used?
AAs first-line agent to constrict pupil immediately
BBefore starting IV acetazolamide
CONLY after IOP <40 mmHg and cornea becomes clear
DNever; it worsens angle closure
Correct Answer: C — After IOP controlled and cornea clear
CRITICAL EXAM POINT: Do NOT use pilocarpine early in acute PACG. High IOP causes corneal oedema; early pilocarpine worsens angle closure by forward lens-iris diaphragm displacement. Use ONLY after IV acetazolamide, topical beta-blockers, and alpha-2 agonists have controlled IOP and cleared cornea.
Q12 PG
Which of the following is a contraindication to beta-blockers in glaucoma?
ADiabetes mellitus
BHypertension
CCoronary artery disease
DAsthma or COPD (especially non-selective beta-blockers)
Correct Answer: D — Asthma/COPD
Non-selective beta-blockers (timolol, levobunolol) can cause bronchospasm in asthma/COPD. Beta-1 selective agents (betaxolol) are safer. Avoid beta-blockers also in heart failure, bradycardia. Check systemic contraindications before prescribing topical beta-blockers.
Q13 PG
Which prostaglandin analogue side effect may limit patient compliance?
AIris darkening and periocular skin darkening (hypertrichosis)
BCorneal clouding
CAcute uveitis
DCataract formation
Correct Answer: A — Iris darkening & hypertrichosis
Prostaglandin analogues can cause iris darkening (cosmetic concern, especially in light-colored irises), increased eyelash growth (hypertrichosis), and periocular skin darkening. These cosmetic side effects can reduce patient compliance. Inform patients beforehand.
Q14 PG
Which medication has the risk of developing tachyphylaxis (tolerance) in glaucoma treatment?
ALatanoprost
BBrimonidine (alpha-2 agonist)
CDorzolamide
DTimolol
Correct Answer: B — Brimonidine
Brimonidine has significant risk of tachyphylaxis (tolerance develops over weeks to months), requiring dose escalation or drug substitution. Due to this limitation and systemic side effects (drowsiness), it's usually not first-line monotherapy but is useful in combination therapy.

University Practicals
Discuss the acute attack of angle-closure glaucoma. What is the emergency management sequence? (8 marks)
Correct Answer: Acute PACG presents with severe pain, red eye, fixed pupil, hazy cornea, IOP>50. Emergency treatment: IV Acetazolamide → topical beta-blocker/alpha-2 agonist → [pilocarpine after control] → laser PI. Sequence is critical; pilocarpine early worsens attack.

Aqueous Dynamics

  • Production: ~2 µL/min by ciliary epithelium
  • Drainage: 85% trabecular, 15% uveoscleral
  • Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg
  • Diurnal variation: up to 5 mmHg

Glaucoma Classification

  • POAG (60–70%): Open angle, insidious, painless
  • PACG: Closed angle, acute pain, IOP >50 mmHg
  • NTG: IOP ≤21 mmHg but disc + VF changes (vasospasm)
  • Congenital (Buphthalmos): Large cornea, Haab's striae, photophobia

POAG Disc Changes

  • C:D ≥0.6 (normal ≤0.3–0.4)
  • ISNT rule: Inferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal
  • Disc notching, haemorrhage, RNFL loss
  • Baring of circumlinear vessel

VF Defects (POAG)

  • Pathognomonic: Bjerrum scotoma (arcuate, 10–20° from fixation)
  • Nasal step, arcuate, ring scotoma, central island
  • Follow RNFL distribution pattern

PACG Acute Attack

  • Severe pain, red eye, hazy cornea, IOP >50 mmHg
  • Mid-dilated FIXED pupil, ischemic iris
  • Emergency: IV Acetazolamide → Timolol → (Pilocarpine after control) → Laser PI
  • NEVER pilocarpine first!

First-Line Drugs

  • Monotherapy: Latanoprost (PG analogue; 25–35% IOP ↓)
  • Dual: PG + Timolol or PG + CAI
  • Triple: PG + Beta-blocker + CAI
  • Beta-blockers: avoid in asthma/COPD

Congenital Glaucoma

  • Classic triad: Photophobia, Epiphora, Blepharospasm
  • Large cornea (>12 mm), Haab's striae, deep cupping
  • Treatment: Goniotomy (1st-line) or trabeculotomy

Surgical Options

  • Trabeculectomy: Gold standard; ~70–80% success with MMC/5-FU
  • MIGS: iStent, Hydrus, XEN; lower IOP reduction, fewer complications
  • Laser: PI (PACG), trabeculoplasty (POAG), cyclophotocoagulation (refractory)
