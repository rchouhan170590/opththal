Glaucoma

A progressive optic neuropathy defined by the triad of elevated IOP, optic disc changes, and visual field loss. Covers POAG, PACG, NTG, secondary glaucomas, congenital glaucoma, and surgical management at full PG-exam depth.

UG PG High-Yield

Seven high-yield cards covering the core concepts you must have at your fingertips before any glaucoma question. Read these first, then go deep.

The Glaucoma Triad

  • Elevated IOP — statistical normal 10–21 mmHg (mean 16, SD 2.5)
  • Optic disc changes — increased C:D ratio, notching, pallor, RNFL defects
  • Visual field loss — characteristic arcuate pattern, nasal step, ring scotoma

Critical point: Not all elevated IOP = glaucoma (ocular hypertension). Not all glaucoma = elevated IOP (NTG). The disc and field tell the real story.

HY IOP measurement by Goldmann applanation is the gold standard. NCT is a screening tool only.

Classification at a Glance

  • POAG — open angle, insidious, bilateral, most common type worldwide
  • PACG — closed angle, painful acute attack or chronic; prevalent in Asians
  • NTG — disc damage with IOP always <21 mmHg; vascular risk factors
  • Secondary open angle — PSX, pigmentary, steroid-induced, uveitic
  • Secondary closed angle — neovascular, plateau iris, lens-induced
  • Congenital / developmental — buphthalmos, Sturge-Weber, aniridia

HY PACG is the most common cause of bilateral blindness from glaucoma globally, especially in Asia.

POAG Key Facts

  • Insidious onset, painless, bilateral but asymmetric
  • Anterior chamber angle open on gonioscopy
  • C:D ratio >0.6 or asymmetry >0.2 between eyes is suspicious
  • ISNT rule: Normal disc NRR is Inferior > Superior > Nasal > Temporal in thickness
  • Drance hemorrhage (splinter/flame hemorrhage at disc margin) is an early sign
  • Bjerrum scotoma (arcuate) is the classic visual field defect
  • Risk factors: Age, family history, myopia, thin cornea (<555 microns), Black ethnicity

PACG — Acute Attack

  • Sudden severe pain, headache, nausea and vomiting
  • Painful red eye, markedly reduced vision
  • Seeing coloured haloes around lights (corneal oedema)
  • IOP often >50 mmHg; cornea hazy/steamy
  • Pupil mid-dilated and fixed (4–6 mm), oval, vertically oriented
  • Shallow anterior chamber; closed angle on gonioscopy

Emergency management: IV acetazolamide 500 mg + topical timolol + pilocarpine 2% + IV mannitol. Laser PI once eye quietens.

HY Attacks precipitated by dim light, mydriatic drops, emotional stress, prolonged reading.

  • 1st line: Prostaglandin analogues — latanoprost, bimatoprost, travoprost; once nightly; increase uveoscleral outflow; IOP reduction 25–35%
  • 2nd line: Beta-blockers — timolol 0.5% twice daily; reduce aqueous production; avoid in asthma, heart block

  • Miotics: Pilocarpine — increase trabecular outflow; mainly for PACG and as adjunct

Aqueous Dynamics and Pathophysiology of Glaucoma

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Production of Aqueous Humour

Aqueous humour is produced by the non-pigmented epithelium of the ciliary processes at a rate of approximately 2–2.5 microlitres per minute. Three mechanisms are involved: active secretion (accounts for roughly 80–90% of production, driven by carbonic anhydrase and Na/K-ATPase), ultrafiltration (passive movement driven by hydrostatic pressure), and simple diffusion. Active secretion is the target of most IOP-lowering medications.

Drainage of Aqueous Humour

Aqueous exits the eye through two main routes. The conventional (trabecular) pathway accounts for approximately 85% of outflow. Aqueous passes from the posterior chamber through the pupil into the anterior chamber, then through the trabecular meshwork (corneoscleral, uveal, and juxtacanalicular layers) into Schlemm's canal, and then via 25–30 collector channels into the episcleral venous system. The juxtacanalicular (cribriform) layer of the meshwork is the site of greatest resistance and is the primary target of trabecular bypass surgeries.

The unconventional (uveoscleral) pathway accounts for the remaining 15%. Aqueous passes from the anterior chamber through the face of the ciliary body and between the ciliary muscle bundles into the supraciliary and suprachoroidal space, eventually absorbed by the choroidal vasculature. Prostaglandin analogues dramatically increase uveoscleral outflow by remodelling the extracellular matrix of the ciliary muscle.

Figure 1: Aqueous drainage — trabecular (conventional, 85%) and uveoscleral (unconventional, 15%) pathways. The juxtacanalicular meshwork is the primary site of resistance in POAG.

Pathophysiology of IOP Elevation

In primary open-angle glaucoma, resistance to aqueous outflow is increased at the level of the juxtacanalicular trabecular meshwork. The mechanism involves accumulation of extracellular matrix material, reduced phagocytic activity of trabecular endothelial cells, loss of trabecular cells with age, and myocilin (MYOC) gene mutations in some familial cases. The angle remains open on gonioscopy, which is the defining feature.

In primary angle-closure glaucoma, the peripheral iris physically occludes the trabecular meshwork, preventing aqueous access to the drainage system. Pupillary block is the most common mechanism — aqueous cannot flow freely from the posterior chamber through the pupil, causing posterior chamber pressure to rise, which bows the peripheral iris forward (iris bombe), mechanically occluding the angle.

Mechanism of Optic Nerve Damage

Two main theories exist. The mechanical theory proposes that elevated IOP causes direct compression of axons at the lamina cribrosa, disrupting axoplasmic transport and leading to retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death. The vascular theory argues that reduced optic nerve head perfusion (whether from raised IOP compromising perfusion pressure, or from vasospasm/systemic hypotension as in NTG) causes ischaemia of the RGC axons. In practice both mechanisms operate simultaneously, and the lamina cribrosa acts as the final common pathway, with RGC apoptosis as the end result.

IOP and Glaucoma: Every 1 mmHg reduction in IOP reduces the risk of glaucoma progression by approximately 10%. The OHTS and EMGT trials established that even in ocular hypertension and early POAG, IOP reduction significantly delays or prevents onset of glaucomatous damage.

Primary Open Angle Glaucoma (POAG)

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Risk Factors

Age is the single most important risk factor — prevalence rises from 0.5% at 40 years to over 3% at 75 years. A first-degree relative with POAG increases risk approximately 10-fold. Myopia, thin central corneal thickness (<555 microns), Black African ethnicity, and systemic hypertension are established risk factors. The Ocular Hypertension Treatment Study (OHTS) identified thin cornea as one of the strongest predictors of conversion from ocular hypertension to glaucoma.

Optic Disc Changes in POAG

The optic disc changes form the structural hallmark of glaucomatous damage. These should be assessed systematically:

  • Increased C:D ratio: Vertical C:D >0.6 is suspicious; >0.7 is highly suspicious. Asymmetry >0.2 between eyes is more significant than the absolute value alone.
  • Violation of ISNT rule: Thinning or notching of the inferior NRR pole first (most vulnerable), then superior, then nasal, temporal last. Any violation of ISNT rule on disc photography warrants investigation.
  • Drance haemorrhage: A splinter or flame-shaped haemorrhage at the disc margin or just outside it (in RNFL). Highly specific for glaucoma. Seen in 6% of POAG eyes; more common in NTG. Transiently indicates active progression.
  • RNFL defects: Slit-like or wedge-shaped hyporeflective defects on red-free fundus photography, most commonly inferior or superior arcuate bundles. These precede detectable visual field loss by years.
  • Baring of circumlinear vessel: A vessel that normally runs in the NRR becomes bared as the adjacent NRR thins.
  • Disc vessel nasalisation: As the cup enlarges, central vessels shift nasally.
  • Optic disc pallor: Pallor disproportionate to cupping suggests compressive or ischaemic optic neuropathy rather than glaucoma alone.
  • Bayonetting sign: Double angulation of vessels as they dip into the deep cup and re-emerge.
  • Overpass cupping: Vessel bridges over the cup without dipping (seen when cup has expanded rapidly).

Visual Field Defects

The characteristic VF defects respect the horizontal meridian (due to the anatomy of the retinal nerve fibre layer, which separates above and below at the temporal raphe). The progression follows the sequence described in the Snapshot tab. On Humphrey Field Analyser, the key indices are Mean Deviation (MD, overall sensitivity loss), Pattern Standard Deviation (PSD, focal loss), and Glaucoma Hemifield Test (GHT) status.

Investigations

  • Goldmann applanation tonometry: Gold standard IOP measurement. Must be corrected for CCT (thin cornea underestimates IOP).
  • Gonioscopy: Mandatory — confirms open angle, assesses trabecular pigmentation, identifies peripheral anterior synechiae.
  • Central corneal thickness (pachymetry): Every new glaucoma patient. CCT <555 microns is a risk factor; every 10 micron decrease in CCT corresponds to approximately 0.7 mmHg underestimation of IOP on GAT.
  • OCT RNFL: Measures peripapillary RNFL thickness (circumpapillary scan). Inferior and superior quadrants are most vulnerable. Can detect damage 5–7 years before VF loss.
  • OCT optic disc / Bruch's membrane opening (BMO): BMO-minimum rim width (MRW) is a more anatomically accurate measure of NRR tissue than horizontal rim width. GCC (ganglion cell complex) analysis includes RNFL + ganglion cell layer + inner plexiform layer.
  • Heidelberg Retinal Tomograph (HRT): Confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope measuring disc topography. GPS (Glaucoma Probability Score) and Moorfields Regression Analysis are the main outputs.
  • Humphrey Visual Field: 24-2 SITA Standard is the workhorse. 10-2 for detecting early central loss in advanced disease. Reliable test requires fixation loss <20%, false positive <15%, false negative <33%.
Clinical Pearl: Structure (OCT) leads function (VF) by several years. In early glaucoma, treat on structural evidence even if VF is still normal. In advanced glaucoma, VF progression may occur despite stable OCT (floor effect — OCT hits minimum detectable thickness).

Primary Angle Closure Glaucoma (PACG)

▼

Predisposing Anatomy

PACG occurs in eyes with a predisposed configuration: hypermetropic, short axial length, small anterior segment, shallow anterior chamber (<2.5 mm), narrow drainage angle, thick and anteriorly positioned lens. The angle is assessed by the Van Herick slit-lamp technique — a ratio of peripheral AC depth to corneal thickness of less than 1:4 suggests a narrow angle warranting gonioscopy.

Mechanisms of Angle Closure

  • Pupillary block (most common): Relative resistance to aqueous flow at pupillary margin causes posterior chamber pressure excess, bowing the peripheral iris anteriorly into the angle. Maximal at mid-dilation (3–4 mm pupil).
  • Plateau iris syndrome: Anteriorly positioned ciliary processes hold the peripheral iris against the TM even after PI — treated with iridoplasty.
  • Phacomorphic (lens-induced): Swollen hypermature/intumescent lens pushes iris forward.
  • Malignant glaucoma (aqueous misdirection): Aqueous directed posteriorly into vitreous — rises entire lens-iris diaphragm forward; rare; post-surgical.

Acute Attack — Clinical Features

The acute attack is ophthalmological emergency. The patient presents with sudden onset severe unilateral headache, ocular pain, nausea, vomiting, and a dramatic drop in vision. The eye is red (ciliary injection), the cornea is oedematous and steamy (causing halos), the pupil is mid-dilated, oval, and vertically oriented, and is non-reactive to light. IOP is typically above 50 mmHg. The fellow eye often has a shallow AC and narrow angle.

Management of Acute Attack

The goal is to rapidly lower IOP, break the pupillary block, and open the angle. Treatment is initiated simultaneously on multiple fronts:

  • IV acetazolamide 500 mg stat, followed by 250 mg 6-hourly orally (reduces aqueous production)
  • IV mannitol 1–2 g/kg over 45 minutes (osmotic agent; vitreous dehydration reduces volume and pulls lens-iris diaphragm back)
  • Topical timolol 0.5% — reduces aqueous production
  • Topical pilocarpine 2% — constricts pupil, pulls iris away from angle; ineffective when IOP is very high (iris sphincter ischaemic) so give after initial IOP reduction
  • Topical steroid (prednisolone 1%) — reduces inflammation
  • Supine posture — lens falls back by gravity, slightly deepening AC
  • Once IOP settles — Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) in the affected eye and prophylactic LPI in the fellow eye
Provocative Tests: Dark room test, prone position test, and mydriasis test (use with caution) can precipitate angle closure in susceptible eyes and confirm the diagnosis. Not routinely required if gonioscopy and clinical picture are clear.

Laser Peripheral Iridotomy

LPI creates a full-thickness hole in the peripheral iris, allowing aqueous to bypass the pupillary block and flow directly from the posterior chamber to the anterior chamber, eliminating the pressure gradient that bows the iris forward. Nd:YAG laser is used in most cases. The site is typically in the superior iris at 11 or 1 o'clock position (under the upper lid to minimise dysphotopsia). Success is confirmed by visualising the red reflex through the iridotomy and gonioscopic widening of the angle.

Normal Tension Glaucoma

NTG is defined as glaucomatous optic neuropathy with VF defects and disc changes, with IOP consistently at or below 21 mmHg on multiple measurements. The Collaborative Normal Tension Glaucoma Study (CNTGS) showed that lowering IOP by 30% significantly slows progression even in NTG, confirming IOP as a modifiable risk factor. However, vascular dysregulation (vasospasm, nocturnal hypotension, Raynaud's phenomenon, migraine) and systemic cardiovascular disease are particularly important in NTG. Features that suggest NTG rather than POAG include: disc haemorrhages more frequent, vertical elongation of cup, focal notching (particularly inferotemporal), and VF defects that are denser and closer to fixation.

Management: Rule out other causes of low-IOP optic neuropathy (compressive lesion, intracranial hypertension in CSF leak) before diagnosing NTG. MRI brain and orbit in cases with atypical features. Medical treatment — brimonidine preferred (neuroprotective properties in addition to IOP lowering). Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine) used in vasospastic cases.

Pseudoexfoliation Glaucoma (PSX)

Pseudoexfoliative material (an abnormal fibrillary protein produced by epithelial cells throughout the body) accumulates on the lens capsule (classic triple-ring pattern on the anterior lens surface), pupil margin, ciliary body, TM, and endothelium. In the TM, this material clogs trabecular drainage and causes progressive IOP elevation, often dramatically high. Zonular weakness is frequent — important for surgical planning (risk of zonular dialysis during cataract surgery). PSX is the most common identifiable cause of POAG worldwide. The LOXL1 gene on chromosome 15 is the principal genetic locus.

Pigment Dispersion Syndrome and Pigmentary Glaucoma

Pigment from the posterior iris pigment epithelium is liberated by mechanical contact between the posterior iris and the zonule/lens, dispersed in the aqueous, and deposited throughout the anterior segment. Classic triad: Krukenberg spindle (vertical brown pigment deposition on corneal endothelium), Sampaolesi line (pigment anterior to Schwalbe's line on gonioscopy), and spoke-like transillumination defects in the mid-peripheral iris. Occurs in young myopic males. Exercise or pupillary dilation disperses pigment acutely, causing IOP spikes. Progression to pigmentary glaucoma occurs in about 25–50% of cases.

Neovascular Glaucoma

New vessels on the iris (rubeosis iridis) and angle develop in response to retinal or anterior segment ischaemia — most commonly proliferative diabetic retinopathy, central retinal vein occlusion, or ocular ischaemic syndrome. The fibrovascular membrane contracts, synechially closing the angle, and IOP rises dramatically. Management requires treatment of the underlying ischaemia (PRP or anti-VEGF) and IOP lowering; once synechial closure is established, drainage surgery (Ahmed valve) is needed.

Steroid-Induced Glaucoma

Corticosteroids (topical, periocular, intravitreal, systemic) cause IOP elevation in steroid responders — roughly one-third of the general population show a moderate rise (>6 mmHg), and 4–6% show a marked rise (>30 mmHg). The mechanism is accumulation of extracellular matrix in the TM due to inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases. IOP rise typically begins 4–6 weeks after starting topical steroids. Management is discontinuation of the steroid (IOP usually normalises within weeks) and temporary antiglaucoma treatment.

Congenital Glaucoma (Buphthalmos)

Caused by maldevelopment of the anterior chamber angle (trabeculodysgenesis), resulting in elevated IOP from birth or early infancy. The infant eye is distensible, so the globe enlarges (buphthalmos — ox eye). Features include: corneal oedema and haziness (initially reversible), photophobia and blepharospasm (due to corneal oedema), tearing (reflex epiphora), corneal diameter >12 mm in newborns, Haab's striae (horizontal ruptures in Descemet's membrane from corneal stretching), and increased axial length causing myopia. Definitive treatment is surgical: goniotomy (direct incision of the angle under gonioscopic visualisation) or trabeculotomy (ab externo) — both opening the trabecular meshwork to allow aqueous outflow.

Indirect vs Direct Gonioscopy

  • Indirect (Goldmann, Zeiss 4-mirror): Requires coupling agent. Goldmann 1-mirror for detailed TM examination; Zeiss 4-mirror (gonioprism) for indentation gonioscopy (distinguishing appositional from synechial closure — pressing lens flattens cornea, deepening AC; appositional closure opens, synechial does not).
  • Direct (Koeppe lens): Patient supine; direct view; often used in children and in operating theatre for goniotomy.

Pachymetry

Ultrasound pachymetry or Scheimpflug-based pachymetry measures CCT. Central corneal thickness influences IOP readings: thinner corneas underestimate IOP on applanation tonometry (Goldmann reads falsely low), and thicker corneas overestimate. The correction factor is approximately 0.7 mmHg per 10 microns of CCT deviation from 545 microns. More importantly, thin CCT (<555 microns) is an independent risk factor for glaucoma development, separate from its effect on IOP measurement.

OCT Parameters in Glaucoma

  • Peripapillary RNFL: Measured as a 3.4 mm diameter circular scan around the disc. Output: TSNIT (Temporal-Superior-Nasal-Inferior-Temporal) graph and sector averages. Inferior and superior quadrants earliest affected. Green/yellow/red classification compared to normative database.
  • BMO-MRW: Bruch's membrane opening minimum rim width — distance from BMO edge to inner limiting membrane. More sensitive than horizontal rim width as it accounts for oblique orientation of rim tissue.
  • Ganglion Cell Complex (GCC): Macular RNFL + GCL + IPL. Useful for detecting early glaucoma as ~50% of all RGCs are within 8 degrees of fixation. Pattern deviation map identifies focal loss.
  • Anterior segment OCT: Measures angle parameters (AOD — angle opening distance, TISA — trabecular-iris space area) without contact; excellent for narrow angle screening.

Surgical Management of Glaucoma

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Indications for Surgery

Surgery is indicated when: target IOP cannot be achieved on maximum tolerated medical therapy; the patient is non-compliant with drops or intolerant of medications; the disease is progressing on medical therapy; advanced glaucoma requiring a very low target IOP; or patient preference in some cases. The decision balances the risk of surgical complications against the risk of blindness from progressive optic neuropathy.

Trabeculectomy (Filtering Surgery)

This remains the gold standard glaucoma filtering procedure. A partial-thickness scleral flap is created in the superior quadrant under the upper lid. A block of corneoscleral tissue (the sclerostomy) is excised from beneath the flap, creating a fistula between the anterior chamber and the sub-Tenon's space. A peripheral iridectomy prevents iris prolapse. Aqueous then percolates under the scleral flap and into a sub-conjunctival filtering bleb, from which it is absorbed.

Steps of trabeculectomy: Conjunctival peritomy (limbus-based or fornix-based) → haemostasis → antimetabolite application (MMC or 5-FU on sponges) → half-thickness scleral flap (3×4 mm) → paracentesis → sclerostomy (Kelly punch or blade, 1.5 mm block) → peripheral iridectomy → scleral flap closure (releasable or adjustable sutures) → conjunctival closure (watertight).

Mitomycin C (MMC): Antimetabolite applied subconjunctivally for 1–3 minutes during surgery (concentration 0.2–0.4 mg/mL). Inhibits fibroblast proliferation, reducing scarring and bleb failure. Used when higher risk of failure (young patient, previous failed surgery, uveitic glaucoma, previous conjunctival surgery). Increases risk of avascular thin blebs and bleb-related endophthalmitis.

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU): Can be applied intraoperatively or as postoperative subconjunctival injections (5 mg in 0.1 mL). Less potent than MMC; used in moderate-risk patients.

Complications of trabeculectomy:

  • Early: hypotony (excessive drainage), flat AC, choroidal detachment, suprachoroidal haemorrhage, wound leak, infection
  • Late: bleb failure (fibrosis), encapsulated (Tenon's) bleb — firm, vascular bleb with IOP spike; bleb leak; bleb-related endophthalmitis; cataract progression; diplopia
  • Wipeout: sudden loss of central vision in end-stage glaucoma after large IOP reduction (controversial; may be hypotony-related or pre-existing fragility)

Minimally Invasive Glaucoma Surgery (MIGS)

MIGS procedures are designed for mild-to-moderate glaucoma, typically combined with cataract surgery (phaco-MIGS). They have lower risk profiles than trabeculectomy but are less effective at achieving very low IOPs. Categories:

  • Trabecular bypass (ab interno): iStent inject W (second-generation, two stents placed in TM), Hydrus Microstent (dilates Schlemm's canal), Kahook Dual Blade (goniotomy). Reduce IOP by 3–5 mmHg, mainly reduce medication burden.
  • Sub-conjunctival: XEN gel stent (45 micron lumen polyacrylamide tube; ab interno approach; creates subconjunctival bleb). IOP reduction ~6–8 mmHg; requires antimetabolite needling in some cases.
  • Suprachoroidal: CyPass (withdrawn from market due to corneal endothelial cell loss); iTrack; STARflo.
  • Schlemm's canal dilation: Visco360 viscoelastic canaloplasty; trab360 suture canaloplasty.

Glaucoma Drainage Devices (GDDs)

GDDs (tube shunt surgery) are used in refractory glaucoma, failed trabeculectomy, neovascular glaucoma, uveitic glaucoma, and situations where conjunctival scarring precludes trabeculectomy. A silicone tube is inserted into the anterior chamber (or posterior segment), connected to an end plate placed in the equatorial sub-Tenon's space. Aqueous collects in a bleb around the end plate.

  • Ahmed Glaucoma Valve: Contains a unidirectional valve mechanism (Venturi-type) that opens when IOP exceeds 8–10 mmHg, preventing hypotony. Easier to manage but somewhat higher failure rate long-term. Available in silicone (Model FP7) and polypropylene (Model S2).
  • Baerveldt implant (250 or 350 mm²): Non-valved; tube is occluded for 6–8 weeks while bleb encapsulates, then opened. Larger plate area → lower long-term IOP, but higher hypotony risk initially. TVT study showed Baerveldt comparable to trabeculectomy + MMC in failed trabeculectomy eyes.

Cyclodestructive Procedures

Cyclophotocoagulation (CPC) destroys the ciliary epithelium to reduce aqueous production. Used in refractory glaucoma, painful blind eyes (palliation), and when no filtration surgery is feasible. Modalities include: transscleral CPC (diode laser, 810 nm, contact probe applied 1.5 mm behind limbus), endoscopic CPC (ECP — during cataract surgery, allows direct visualisation), and micropulse CPC (safer thermal profile, less destructive). Complications include: hypotony, phthisis bulbi, uveitis, and pain.

Laser Trabeculoplasty

Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT) uses a 532 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG laser at 90 or 180 degrees of TM. Selectively photolyses pigmented TM cells without coagulative damage, stimulating macrophage recruitment and ECM remodelling. IOP reduction of 5–9 mmHg. Repeatable (unlike ALT). The LIGHT trial established SLT as a viable first-line alternative to drops in newly diagnosed POAG.

Surgical Decision Algorithm: Mild–moderate POAG requiring modest IOP reduction with cataract → Phaco + MIGS (iStent/Hydrus). Moderate POAG, target IOP 12–14 mmHg, good conjunctiva → Trabeculectomy + MMC. Refractory / neovascular / failed trab → Ahmed valve or Baerveldt. End-stage painful eye → CPC.

Master these five clinical procedures. Each is examined in PG practicals and viva. Read the steps, understand the rationale, and know the normal values.

Goldmann Applanation Tonometry (GAT) Gold Standard
1
Preparation

Instil topical anaesthetic (proxymetacaine 0.5% or oxybuprocaine 0.4%) and fluorescein (single-use strip or combined fluorescein-anaesthetic drops). Allow 30 seconds for effect. The prism tip must be clean and disinfected (30 minutes in sodium hypochlorite or disposable prism covers).

2
Slit Lamp Setup

Set the cobalt blue filter and broad beam. The slit lamp must be at the same height as the patient's eye. Mount the tonometer prism on the slit lamp arm. Align the tonometer prism with the zero mark aligned to the white line on the prism holder unless the patient has corneal astigmatism >3D (then rotate 43 degrees).

3
Contact and Measurement

Ask the patient to look straight ahead and blink. Advance the prism to gently touch the central cornea. Through the eyepiece, two fluorescent semicircles appear. Adjust the dial until the inner edges of the two semicircles just touch.

Normal: semicircle width should be approximately 1/10th the total diameter. Too thick fluorescein ring (excess anaesthetic/fluorescein) overestimates; too thin underestimates.
4
Reading and Interpretation

The dial reading multiplied by 10 gives IOP in mmHg. Take three readings within 2 mmHg of each other and average. Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg.

Adjust for CCT: thin cornea (<510 microns) — add 2–3 mmHg to reading. Thick cornea (>580 microns) — subtract 2–3 mmHg. Corneal oedema or scarring also affects accuracy.
5
Diurnal Variation

IOP is highest in the early morning (6–8 AM) in most patients, falling through the day. Diurnal variation >5 mmHg is suspicious for glaucoma. Phasing (multiple IOP readings over 24 hours) is done in suspected glaucoma to catch peak IOP.

Non-Contact Tonometry (NCT / Air-Puff) Screening
1
Principle

A puff of air deforms the corneal surface. The machine detects the time taken for the cornea to momentarily flatten (applanation), which correlates with IOP. No anaesthesia required. Contactless — no infection risk.

2
Limitations

Less accurate than GAT, especially at higher IOP values. Affected by corneal hydration, oedema, and irregular surfaces. Blink/squint artefact common. Average of three readings reduces variability. Any NCT >21 mmHg should be confirmed by GAT before initiating treatment.

NCT is excellent for mass screening. Never use it alone to diagnose or exclude glaucoma in a clinical setting.
Gonioscopy Essential PG Skill
1
Preparation

Topical anaesthetic. Explain the procedure. Darken the room (to avoid corneal light reflex). For Goldmann single-mirror lens, use coupling agent (methylcellulose). For Zeiss gonioprism (4-mirror), coupling agent may not be needed (tear film sufficient, but a drop of BSS is used by many).

2
Technique — Goldmann Single Mirror

Tilt the lens to view each quadrant sequentially. The mirror at 59 degrees allows gonioscopy. Rotate the lens 360 degrees to view all four quadrants. Use a short, narrow slit beam to avoid light-induced pupil constriction affecting the angle view.

3
Indentation Gonioscopy (Zeiss 4-mirror)

Apply gentle axial pressure on the lens — this deepens the peripheral AC and pushes aqueous into the angle, opening appositional closure. If angle opens with indentation: appositional closure (treatable with LPI). If angle remains closed with PAS despite indentation: synechial closure (may need filtration surgery).

Always perform indentation gonioscopy when the angle appears closed — distinguishing appositional from synechial closure is critical for management decisions.
4
Grading and Documentation

Grade all four quadrants using Shaffer classification (0–4). Note: TM pigmentation (0–4+), PAS extent (clock hours involved), neovascularisation, exfoliation material, and any anomalous vessels. Document gonioscopic findings in the chart with a diagram or clock-hour notation.

Humphrey Visual Field Test Interpretation PG Viva Favourite
1
Assess Reliability Indices First

Before interpreting any field, check: Fixation losses (catch trials — stimulus in blind spot) <20% acceptable. False positives (patient responds when no stimulus — trigger-happy) <15%. False negatives (patient misses known visible stimuli — fatigued) <33%. An unreliable test cannot be interpreted meaningfully.

2
Grayscale Map

Gives a visual overview — darker areas = greater sensitivity loss. Do not interpret grayscale alone; it is interpolated and can be misleading. Use it only for a quick orientation.

3
Total Deviation and Pattern Deviation Plots

Total Deviation (TD) compares each test location to age-matched normal. Probability symbols indicate significance. Pattern Deviation (PD) removes the effect of overall sensitivity depression (eg from cataract), highlighting focal defects. In glaucoma, PD is the more useful map.

A cluster of 3 or more adjacent significant points on PD map (at p<5%, with at least one at p<1%) constitutes a significant VF defect in glaucoma staging.
4
Global Indices

MD (mean deviation): overall loss. PSD (pattern standard deviation): focal irregularity. GHT: compares upper and lower hemifields. VFI: used to estimate rate of progression over time. A linear VFI progression rate of more than −2%/year is considered rapid.

5
Identifying Glaucomatous Pattern

Look for defects respecting the horizontal meridian, nasal step, arcuate/Bjerrum distribution, and denser inferiorly (if superior RNFL affected first). Compare with prior fields for progression analysis. Guided Progression Analysis (GPA) software identifies significant progression automatically.

The classic PG viva question: "What is a nasal step of Ronne?" — A step in the VF at the horizontal meridian nasally, with more sensitivity loss above or below the line, caused by RNFL damage respecting the temporal raphe.
Optic Disc Assessment for Glaucoma Clinical Exam
1
Method of Examination

Dilated fundoscopy with 78D or 90D lens at the slit lamp (for stereo view) or indirect ophthalmoscopy. Disc photography (stereo disc photos) and OCT RNFL are objective adjuncts. The slit-lamp 90D view gives the best stereoscopic appreciation of cup depth and contour.

2
What to Assess Systematically

Use the ABCDE mnemonic: Asymmetry of C:D (>0.2 between eyes), Baring of circumlinear vessel, Cup size (C:D ratio — vertical and horizontal), Disc haemorrhage (Drance), Erosion of NRR (notching at IS or IT pole). Also assess disc size (large disc physiologically has larger cup), colour (pallor vs cupped), and RNFL visibility (red-free light helps).

3
Differentiating Glaucomatous Cupping from Other Causes

In glaucoma: cupping precedes pallor, cup is asymmetric, RNFL defects present, notching at vertical poles. In optic atrophy (eg compressive): pallor without cupping, uniform pallor, may have atrophy of papillomacular bundle first. In physiological large cup: symmetric, good NRR, ISNT rule intact, normal VF, normal IOP.

Pallor out of proportion to cupping always warrants neurological workup — intracranial or orbital compression must be excluded.
4
Documentation

Record: disc size (small/average/large), C:D ratio (vertical and horizontal), NRR assessment by quadrant with ISNT rule comment, presence and location of any haemorrhage, RNFL defects (location), and any other disc anomalies. Compare with previous disc photographs if available to detect change.

LA 1 Describe the pathophysiology, clinical features, investigations, and management of Primary Open Angle Glaucoma. 15 Marks
Introduction (1M)

Define POAG: progressive optic neuropathy with open anterior chamber angle, IOP usually elevated, characteristic disc and VF changes, no secondary cause identifiable.

Pathophysiology (3M)

Increased resistance to aqueous outflow at juxtacanalicular TM. Accumulation of ECM glycosaminoglycans, loss of TM endothelial cell phagocytic activity, myocilin (MYOC) gene mutations in some familial cases. Elevated IOP compresses axons at lamina cribrosa (mechanical theory) and reduces optic nerve head perfusion (vascular theory). RGC apoptosis is the final pathway.

Clinical Features (3M)

Insidious, painless, bilateral asymmetric. No symptoms until late. Disc changes (ABCDE: Asymmetry, Baring, Cup enlargement, Drance haemorrhage, Erosion of NRR). VF: paracentral scotoma → nasal step → Bjerrum arcuate → ring scotoma → central island → total loss. IOP usually elevated (but may be in normal range initially).

Risk Factors (1M)

Age, family history, myopia, thin CCT, Black ethnicity, systemic hypertension.

Investigations (3M)

GAT (IOP), gonioscopy, pachymetry, disc photography, OCT RNFL + GCC, HVF 24-2 SITA standard. HVF indices: MD, PSD, GHT, VFI. OCT: peripapillary RNFL, BMO-MRW, macular GCC.

Management (4M)

Target IOP 30% below baseline. Medical: prostaglandins (1st line), BB, CAI, alpha-2 agonist, ROCK inhibitors. Laser: SLT (LIGHT trial — non-inferior to drops as 1st line). Surgical: trabeculectomy + MMC (gold standard for low target IOP), MIGS (phaco-MIGS for mild-moderate), GDD (refractory), CPC (end-stage). Regular monitoring: 6–12 monthly OCT + VF; disc photography annually.

LA 2 Enumerate the antiglaucoma drugs with their mechanism of action, dosage, and important side effects. 10 Marks
Classification Framework (1M)

Five main classes: Prostaglandins, Beta-blockers, CAIs, Alpha-2 agonists, Miotics. Plus newer class: ROCK inhibitors.

Prostaglandin Analogues (2M)

Latanoprost 0.005% once nightly. FP receptor agonist. Increases uveoscleral outflow. IOP reduction 25–35%. Side effects: iris pigmentation, periorbital fat atrophy, hypertrichosis, CME in pseudophakic eye, uveitis. CI: active uveitis, pregnancy.

Beta-blockers (2M)

Timolol 0.5% BD. Beta-2 receptor blockade on ciliary epithelium. Reduces aqueous production ~35%. Side effects: bronchospasm, bradycardia, depression, dry eye. CI: asthma, heart block, CCF.

CAIs (2M)

Topical: Dorzolamide 2% TDS; Brinzolamide 1% TDS. Oral: Acetazolamide 250 mg QDS. Inhibit carbonic anhydrase II/IV, reducing aqueous secretion 20–30%. Side effects: stinging, bitter taste, paraesthesiae (oral), renal stones, metabolic acidosis, sulfa allergy. CI: sulfa allergy, renal failure, corneal endothelial disease (topical).

Alpha-2 Agonists (1.5M)

Brimonidine 0.2% TDS. Dual: reduces production + increases uveoscleral outflow. Possibly neuroprotective. Side effects: allergy/follicular conjunctivitis in 25%, dry mouth, fatigue. CI: infants, MAOIs.

Miotics and ROCK inhibitors (1.5M)

Pilocarpine 2% QDS: cholinergic agonist, increases trabecular outflow. SE: miosis, induced myopia, brow ache, cataract. Netarsudil 0.02% OD: ROCK inhibitor, increases trabecular outflow, reduces episcleral venous pressure. SE: conjunctival hyperaemia, corneal verticillata.

LA 3 Discuss acute congestive angle closure glaucoma — clinical features, diagnosis, and management. 10 Marks
Definition and Mechanism (1M)

Acute PACG: sudden angle closure due to pupillary block, causing IOP >50 mmHg. Predominantly in hypermetropic, short-axial-length eyes; common in Asian women 55–65 years.

Predisposing Factors (1M)

Hypermetropia, shallow AC, narrow angle (Van Herick <1:4), thick anteriorly positioned lens, precipitants: dim light (pupil dilates), stress, mydriatic drops.

Clinical Features (3M)

Symptoms: sudden severe ocular pain, headache, nausea/vomiting, reduced vision, haloes around lights. Signs: ciliary injection (red eye), hazy/steamy cornea, shallow AC, fixed oval mid-dilated pupil (4–6 mm), IOP >50 mmHg, Descemet's striate keratopathy. Fellow eye has shallow AC and narrow angle.

Diagnosis (1M)

Clinical features + GAT (high IOP) + gonioscopy (closed angle, appositional or synechial). Van Herick slit-lamp assessment. Exclude: acute iritis (AC cells/flare, normal IOP), acute keratitis, migraine.

Management (4M)

Emergency medical: IV acetazolamide 500 mg + IV mannitol 1–2 g/kg + topical timolol + topical pilocarpine (after IOP partly reduced) + topical steroid. Definitive: Nd:YAG laser PI to affected eye once clear + prophylactic PI to fellow eye. If LPI fails or synechial closure: trabeculectomy. Medical: pilocarpine 1% TDS long-term post-LPI if angle has PAS. For precipitating lens (phacomorphic): cataract surgery.

LA 4 Write a short note on trabeculectomy — indications, steps, and complications. 10 Marks
Introduction (0.5M)

Trabeculectomy is the most commonly performed glaucoma filtering surgery, creating a controlled fistula between the AC and the sub-Tenon's space, allowing aqueous drainage into a filtering bleb.

Indications (2M)

Failure of medical therapy to achieve target IOP, intolerance or non-compliance with medications, advanced glaucoma requiring very low IOP, rapidly progressing disease, patient preference, cost/access issues (single surgery vs lifelong drops).

Steps (4M)

Peribulbar anaesthesia → Conjunctival peritomy (fornix-based or limbus-based, superior quadrant) → Haemostasis → Application of antimetabolite (MMC 0.2–0.4 mg/mL on sponges, 1–3 min; or 5-FU) → Irrigation → Scleral flap (half-thickness, 3×4 mm) → Paracentesis → Sclerostomy (1.5 mm block with Kelly punch or blade) → Peripheral iridectomy → Scleral flap suture (releasable sutures allow postoperative titration) → Conjunctival closure (watertight, 10-0 nylon or 8-0 Vicryl).

Complications (3.5M)

Intraoperative: suprachoroidal haemorrhage, perforation of scleral flap, loss of vitreous. Early postoperative: hypotony, flat AC (choroidal detachment), wound leak, early bleb failure, infection. Late: bleb failure (fibrosis, encapsulated Tenon's bleb), bleb leak, bleb-related blebitis/endophthalmitis, cataract progression, diplopia, corneal dellen, "wipeout" (loss of central vision in end-stage glaucoma).

LA 5 Classify glaucoma and describe the features that differentiate Primary Open Angle Glaucoma from Primary Angle Closure Glaucoma. 10 Marks
Classification (3M)

Based on angle: Open angle (POAG, NTG, secondary OAG) vs Closed angle (PACG acute/chronic, secondary CAG). Based on aetiology: Primary (no identifiable cause) vs Secondary (identifiable cause). Based on age: Adult vs Congenital/developmental. Full classification table as in Deep Dive.

POAG vs PACG Comparison (7M)

Cover all key differentiating points:

  • Age/sex: POAG — any adult, slight male preponderance; PACG — 55–65, more common in women (3:1)
  • Refraction: POAG — myopes at risk; PACG — hypermetropes predisposed
  • Onset: POAG — insidious, chronic; PACG — acute attack or chronic
  • Symptoms: POAG — painless until late; PACG acute — severe pain, headache, nausea
  • IOP: POAG — moderately elevated (21–35 mmHg usually); PACG acute — markedly elevated (>50 mmHg)
  • Cornea: POAG — clear; PACG acute — oedematous, hazy
  • Pupil: POAG — normal; PACG acute — mid-dilated, oval, fixed
  • Anterior chamber: POAG — normal depth; PACG — shallow
  • Gonioscopy: POAG — open angle; PACG — appositional or synechial closure
  • Fellow eye: POAG — often similarly affected; PACG — shallow AC, narrow angle, at risk
  • Treatment: POAG — prostaglandins, SLT, trabeculectomy; PACG — medical emergency + laser PI
Real-pattern questions from AIIMS PG, NEET PG, and DNB CET examinations (2018–2023 pattern). Single best answer unless specified.
NEET PG 2022 A patient presents with dandruff-like deposits on the anterior lens capsule, at the pupil margin, and on the corneal endothelium. Gonioscopy shows grade 3 angle with heavy pigmentation of the inferior trabecular meshwork and a line of pigment anterior to Schwalbe's line. IOP is 36 mmHg. Diagnosis is: Pattern: NEET PG
Answer: Pseudoexfoliation (exfoliation) syndrome with secondary glaucoma

The line of pigment anterior to Schwalbe's line is called Sampaolesi's line — highly characteristic of PSX syndrome (also seen in pigment dispersion, but in a different pattern). The triple-ring pattern on the anterior lens (central disc, clear intermediate zone from iris rubbing, peripheral granular zone) is pathognomonic of PSX. LOXL1 gene on chromosome 15 is the genetic basis. Treatment: topical antiglaucoma drops (SLT is particularly effective); cataract surgery must be done with caution due to zonular weakness.

AIIMS PG 2021 In the Humphrey 24-2 visual field, a "nasal step" that respects the horizontal meridian is characteristic of which condition? Pattern: AIIMS
Answer: Glaucoma (Primary open angle glaucoma)

The nasal step of Ronne is a step-like VF defect at the nasal horizontal meridian, where there is an asymmetric defect above or below the horizontal line. This respects the horizontal raphe — the temporal retinal nerve fibres divide sharply into superior and inferior bundles at the temporal raphe and do not cross the horizontal midline. Damage to either superior or inferior arcuate bundle produces defects that respect this border. It is one of the earliest reproducible defects in glaucoma. Not seen in other optic nerve or retinal diseases that do not follow the RNFL architecture.

DNB CET 2022 The LIGHT trial (2019) compared which two modalities as initial treatment for open angle glaucoma? Pattern: DNB
Answer: Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT) vs Prostaglandin analogue eye drops

The LIGHT trial (Laser in Glaucoma and Ocular Hypertension) was a multicentre RCT published in Lancet 2019. It randomised newly diagnosed OAG and OHT patients to SLT or prostaglandin analogue drops as first-line treatment. At 3 years: SLT was non-inferior to drops in achieving target IOP. 78% of SLT patients were drop-free at 3 years. SLT group had better quality of life scores. No difference in VF or disc progression. Conclusion: SLT should be offered as first-line treatment alongside drops in newly diagnosed OAG/OHT.

NEET PG 2020 The Shaffer grade that indicates the most likely angle closure is: Pattern: NEET PG
Answer: Grade 0 (slit angle)

Shaffer grading is based on the angle width between the iris surface and the inner corneoscleral wall: Grade 4 (35–45 degrees, ciliary body visible, angle closure impossible), Grade 3 (25–35 degrees, scleral spur visible, closure unlikely), Grade 2 (20 degrees, posterior TM visible, closure possible), Grade 1 (10 degrees, anterior TM only, closure likely), Grade 0 (slit, only Schwalbe's line visible, closure imminent). For exam: Grade 2 or less warrants prophylactic LPI. Grade 0 is an indication for urgent LPI.

AIIMS PG 2019 Which of the following statements about mitomycin C use in trabeculectomy is correct? Pattern: AIIMS
Answer: MMC is an alkylating agent that inhibits fibroblast proliferation and reduces subconjunctival scarring, but increases the risk of late bleb leaks and bleb-related endophthalmitis.

MMC (mitomycin C) acts by cross-linking DNA, permanently inhibiting fibroblast replication. When applied subconjunctivally during trabeculectomy (0.2–0.4 mg/mL for 1–3 minutes), it produces diffuse, thin, avascular blebs with very low IOP. Risks: thin avascular bleb is prone to late leak, infection (blebitis → endophthalmitis), and hypotony. Indicated in high-risk cases: young patients, Afro-Caribbean ethnicity, previous failed surgery, uveitic glaucoma. In low-risk cases, 5-FU may be preferable with lower complication rate.

NEET PG 2021 A 6-month-old male infant is brought with a large cloudy eye since birth. Corneal diameter is 14 mm. Which surgical procedure is most appropriate? Pattern: NEET PG
Answer: Trabeculotomy ab externo

Congenital primary glaucoma (buphthalmos, corneal diameter >12 mm, Haab's striae, high IOP, photophobia). With a cloudy cornea, goniotomy (which requires a clear cornea for gonioscopic visualisation) is difficult. Trabeculotomy ab externo does not require corneal clarity — a probe is inserted into Schlemm's canal through an external approach and rotated into the AC, rupturing the TM from outside. Both procedures have ~80–90% success in primary congenital glaucoma. Trabeculectomy is reserved for failed goniotomy/trabeculotomy; MMC should be used cautiously in infants (risk of phthisis from hypotony).

DNB CET 2021 The following structures are seen during gonioscopy from anterior to posterior EXCEPT: Pattern: DNB
Answer: Zonule of Zinn (not a gonioscopic structure)

Normal gonioscopic landmarks from anterior to posterior: Schwalbe's line (thickened end of Descemet's membrane, anterior border of TM) → anterior non-pigmented TM → posterior pigmented TM → Schlemm's canal (may be visible as a faint pinkish area, especially if blood reflux or raised episcleral venous pressure) → scleral spur (white band) → ciliary body band (grey-brown, visible in wide open angles). The zonule of Zinn is not visible gonioscopically. In PSX, Sampaolesi's line (anterior to Schwalbe's) and in pigment dispersion, heavy TM pigmentation are additional findings.

AIIMS PG 2023 A patient with refractory neovascular glaucoma following CRVO, with IOP 46 mmHg on maximal medical therapy and failed trabeculectomy, is best managed by: Pattern: AIIMS
Answer: Ahmed glaucoma valve implantation (preceded by intravitreal anti-VEGF)

Refractory NVG with failed trabeculectomy is a challenging scenario. The TVT study (Tube Versus Trabeculectomy) showed that tube shunts (Baerveldt) were superior to trabeculectomy in previously operated eyes. For NVG specifically, Ahmed valve is preferred (the valve mechanism prevents hypotony in the early post-op period when the bleb has not yet encapsulated). Pre-operatively, intravitreal bevacizumab/ranibizumab dramatically reduces rubeosis iridis within 24–72 hours, making surgery safer by reducing bleeding risk. PRP (if not done) should also be completed to address the ischaemic drive for NV. CPC is reserved for painful blind eyes where visual prognosis is hopeless.
