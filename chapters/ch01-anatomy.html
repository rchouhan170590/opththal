Chapter 1

Anatomy & Physiology of the Eye

The structural and functional foundation of clinical ophthalmology. Understanding the anatomy of the eyeball, visual pathway, aqueous dynamics, and nerve supply is essential before approaching any disease.

UGPG

Six high-yield cards covering what you need to recall in the first thirty seconds of any anatomy question. These are the anchors — everything else in the chapter hangs off them.

Layers of the Eyeball

The adult eyeball is roughly 24 mm in anteroposterior diameter and is built in three concentric coats. The outer fibrous coat consists of the sclera posteriorly and the transparent cornea anteriorly. Inside that sits the middle uveal coat, which is the vascular layer made up of the choroid, ciliary body, and iris. The innermost coat is the retina, which is where all the photochemistry happens.

  • Outer coat: Sclera (posterior 5/6) + Cornea (anterior 1/6)
  • Middle coat (uveal tract): Choroid + Ciliary body + Iris
  • Inner coat: Retina (neural + pigmented layers)
  • Three chambers: Anterior (aqueous), Posterior (aqueous), Vitreous
  • Axial length: 24 mm — every 1 mm increase equals roughly 3 D of myopia

Visual Pathway

Light hits the photoreceptors, signals travel inward through the bipolar and ganglion cells, exit as the optic nerve, partially cross at the chiasm, relay through the lateral geniculate nucleus, and finally reach the primary visual cortex. The crossing at the chiasm is the detail that gives every visual field defect its diagnostic character.

  • Retina → Optic nerve (CN II) → Optic chiasm (nasal fibres cross)
  • Optic tract → Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN) of thalamus
  • Optic radiation → Primary visual cortex (V1, calcarine fissure, Area 17)
  • Meyer's loop: Inferior fibres loop through the temporal lobe; lesion here = contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")
  • Baum's loop: Superior fibres via parietal lobe; lesion = inferior quadrantanopia

Aqueous Dynamics

Aqueous humor is produced continuously by the ciliary epithelium, circulates through the anterior and posterior chambers, and drains through two separate routes. When production and drainage fall out of balance, intraocular pressure rises — and that is the fundamental mechanism behind glaucoma.

  • Production: Ciliary epithelium, ~2 μL/min (active transport predominantly)
  • Trabecular route: ~85% → canal of Schlemm → episcleral veins
  • Uveoscleral route: ~15% → ciliary muscle → suprachoroidal space
  • Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg (Goldmann tonometer)
  • Diurnal variation: highest in the morning, lowest at midnight

Blood Supply

The retina has a dual blood supply, which is why different disease processes affect different layers. The inner retina is supplied by the central retinal artery, which is an end artery — block it and the inner layers die within minutes. The outer retina, including the photoreceptors and RPE, depends entirely on the choroidal circulation.

  • Ophthalmic artery: First intracranial branch of the internal carotid artery
  • Central retinal artery (CRA): Supplies inner 6 layers of the retina; true end artery
  • Short posterior ciliary arteries: Supply outer retina, RPE, and choriocapillaris
  • Circle of Zinn-Haller: Supplies optic nerve head; ischaemia here = AION
  • Dual supply creates a watershed zone at the optic disc margin

Visual Field Defects by Site

The location of a lesion in the visual pathway produces a predictable field defect. Get this table into your head and you can answer almost any visual pathway question by working backwards from the defect.

  • Retina / optic nerve: Monocular loss (one eye only)
  • Optic chiasm (central compression): Bitemporal hemianopia
  • Optic tract / LGN / cortex: Homonymous hemianopia (contralateral)
  • Meyer's loop (temporal lobe): Contralateral superior quadrantanopia — "pie in the sky"
  • Baum's loop (parietal lobe): Contralateral inferior quadrantanopia — "pie on the floor"
  • Occipital cortex: Congruent homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing
The Rule
Before the chiasm = one eye. At the chiasm = bitemporal. After the chiasm = homonymous (both eyes, same side).

Nerve Supply of the Eye

Six cranial nerves are involved in the eye and orbit. Mixing them up in an exam is avoidable. The mnemonic LR6 SO4 handles the extraocular muscles; everything else follows from anatomy.

  • Optic nerve (CN II): Vision — all afferent visual information
  • Oculomotor (CN III): MR, SR, IR, IO muscles; levator palpebrae; parasympathetic to sphincter pupillae (constriction) and ciliary muscle (accommodation)
  • Trochlear (CN IV): Superior oblique only; the only cranial nerve to exit dorsally
  • Abducens (CN VI): Lateral rectus only
  • Nasociliary (branch of CN V1): Corneal sensation; also supplies the ciliary ganglion
  • Facial nerve (CN VII): Orbicularis oculi for lid closure; lesion = lagophthalmos
  • Mnemonic: LR6 SO4 rest CN3 — Lateral Rectus: VI, Superior Oblique: IV, all others: III

Outer Coat — Sclera and Cornea

UGPG
▼

The Sclera

The sclera forms the posterior five-sixths of the outer coat and gives the eyeball its shape and mechanical rigidity. It is white and opaque, composed of collagen bundles running in multiple directions — which is exactly why it is opaque while the cornea is not. Structurally it has five layers: episclera (superficial vascular), scleral stroma (dense collagen), lamina fusca (transitional zone with uvea), and the scleral spur at the corneoscleral junction. The extraocular muscles insert into sclera, which is why the Spiral of Tillaux matters surgically.

Emissary canals are the small passages through the sclera that transmit nerves and vessels. These same canals provide a route for intraocular tumours to spread extraocularly — something to keep in mind when staging choroidal melanoma.

The Cornea

Five layers, from anterior to posterior. The epithelium is stratified squamous, five to six cells deep, and regenerates remarkably fast because limbal stem cells at the corneoscleral junction are constantly replenishing it. Bowman's layer lies beneath — acellular, cannot regenerate, so a scar forms permanently when it is damaged. The stroma makes up about ninety percent of the corneal thickness and is collagen type I arranged in lamellae with remarkable regularity — the reason light passes through without scattering. Descemet's membrane is the basement membrane of the endothelium and actually thickens with age. The endothelium is a single layer of hexagonal cells that run a constant pump: sodium-potassium ATPase actively moves fluid out of the stroma to keep it in the dehydrated, transparent state. Normal density is around 2500 cells per square millimetre and there is essentially no regeneration in humans — when cells are lost, the remaining ones enlarge and migrate to fill the gaps.

E B S D E — Epithelium, Bowman's, Stroma, Descemet's, Endothelium

Transparency of the Cornea

Three things keep the cornea transparent. The stroma is avascular. The collagen lamellae are arranged in a regular lattice with spacing smaller than the wavelength of visible light, which prevents scattering. The endothelial pump maintains relative dehydration of the stroma — hydration disrupts the lattice and causes oedema and cloudiness, which is exactly what you see in bullous keratopathy or Fuchs endothelial dystrophy.

The Limbus

The corneoscleral junction, the limbus, is where most surgical incisions for cataract surgery are placed. It also houses the palisades of Vogt where corneal epithelial stem cells reside. Loss of limbal stem cells — from Stevens-Johnson syndrome, chemical burns, or aniridia — leads to conjunctivalisation of the cornea and opacity that cannot be fixed with a routine corneal graft.

Numbers worth knowing: Corneal diameter 12 mm horizontal, 11 mm vertical. Central thickness 0.52–0.55 mm. Refractive power +43 D — the most powerful refracting surface of the eye.

Uveal Tract — Choroid, Ciliary Body, and Iris

▼

The Choroid

The choroid is the most vascular tissue in the body by weight — it carries about eighty percent of total ocular blood flow. Its job is to nourish the outer retina, particularly the photoreceptors and RPE. Structurally it runs from Bruch's membrane on its inner surface outward through the choriocapillaris, Sattler's layer of medium vessels, and Haller's layer of larger vessels, to the suprachoroidal space. Bruch's membrane is a five-layered structure and its integrity is critical: breaks in Bruch's allow choroidal neovascularisation to grow through — the mechanism in wet AMD.

The Ciliary Body

The ciliary body extends from the scleral spur anteriorly to the ora serrata posteriorly and has two parts. The pars plicata (anterior 2 mm) contains the ciliary processes, which are responsible for aqueous secretion. The pars plana (posterior 3.5 mm) is flat, relatively avascular, and the surgeon's preferred entry point for vitreoretinal surgery — 3.5–4 mm from the limbus in phakic eyes. The ciliary muscle controls accommodation by changing the tension on the zonular fibres, which alters the shape of the crystalline lens. When the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonules relax, the lens rounds up, and near focus is achieved. This is Helmholtz's theory of accommodation.

The Iris

The iris is a diaphragm with a central aperture, the pupil. Sphincter pupillae encircles the pupil and is innervated by parasympathetic fibres from the ciliary ganglion via short ciliary nerves — constricting the pupil in response to light or near fixation. Dilator pupillae radiates from the pupil margin to the ciliary body and is innervated by sympathetic fibres. The colour of the iris is determined by the density of melanin in the anterior stroma; blue eyes have less anterior stromal pigment, brown eyes have more. The posterior pigment epithelium is constant regardless of eye colour.

The angle of the anterior chamber is where iris, cornea, and trabecular meshwork meet. Gonioscopy visualises this angle directly. Narrow or closed angles obstruct aqueous outflow and raise IOP — angle-closure glaucoma.

Retina — Ten Layers, Photoreceptors, and Special Zones

UGPG
▼

The Ten Layers

Working from inside (vitreous side) outward to the choroid, the retina has ten named layers. Inner limiting membrane, nerve fibre layer (containing ganglion cell axons), ganglion cell layer, inner plexiform layer, inner nuclear layer (bipolar, amacrine, and Muller cells), outer plexiform layer (Henle's fibre layer at the macula), outer nuclear layer, external limiting membrane, photoreceptor layer (inner and outer segments), and the RPE. The outer four layers — outer nuclear, outer plexiform, photoreceptors, and RPE — depend on the choroidal circulation for their oxygen and nutrients. The inner six depend on the central retinal artery. This division is what makes central retinal artery occlusion and choroidal ischaemia produce such different clinical pictures.

Rods and Cones

There are about 120 million rods and 6 million cones in the human retina. Rods dominate the peripheral retina and are exquisitely sensitive in dim light (scotopic vision). They contain rhodopsin, a photopigment made of opsin plus 11-cis retinal. Cones are concentrated at the fovea and are responsible for colour discrimination and high-acuity vision in bright light (photopic). The three cone types — short, medium, and long wavelength — underlie trichromatic colour vision. At the fovea there are roughly 199,000 cones per square millimetre and essentially no rods.

Macula, Fovea, and Foveola

The macula is the central 5 mm region of the retina responsible for all your detailed vision. Within it, the fovea is a 1.5 mm central depression where the retina is very thin because the inner layers are swept aside laterally (this lateral displacement of neurons is what creates Henle's fibre layer). The foveola, the central 0.35 mm zone, contains only cone inner segments with no overlying neurons, no blood vessels, and no inner layers at all. Nutrients reach the foveola entirely by diffusion from the underlying choriocapillaris. This is why the foveola appears dark on red-free photographs and why a cherry-red spot appears there in central retinal artery occlusion — the surrounding pale ischaemic retina contrasts with the visible choroidal circulation beneath the thin foveola.

Optic Disc and Physiological Blind Spot

The optic disc is 1.5 mm in diameter and located about 4 mm nasal to the fovea. It has no photoreceptors and corresponds to the physiological blind spot. The disc has a central cup (the physiological cup) and a surrounding pink rim (the neuroretinal rim). Cup-to-disc ratio is normally less than 0.5 and the rim should be present throughout. In glaucoma the rim thins and the cup enlarges. A ratio above 0.5 warrants further investigation, though it is not pathological by itself.

Exam trap: The GCL is 8–10 layers thick at the macula (not just 1 layer like elsewhere). At the foveola it is absent entirely. Rods are absent from the central fovea — this is why trying to see a faint star directly makes it disappear (you need to look slightly to the side, using the rod-rich parafovea).

Extraocular Muscles

UGPG
▼

Origins and Basic Anatomy

All four rectus muscles and the superior oblique arise from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex. The inferior oblique is the exception — it originates from the anteromedial floor of the orbit, which is why it has no involvement in the annulus. Knowing this matters because orbital apex syndromes (which affect structures passing through the annulus) will spare inferior oblique function.

Actions

The medial rectus adducts and the lateral rectus abducts. The story gets more nuanced with the obliques and vertical recti because the eye in primary position sits 23 degrees off the axis of these muscles. The superior rectus, when the eye is in the standard position, primarily elevates but also intorts and adducts. The inferior rectus depresses, extorts, and adducts. Superior oblique primarily intorts, and its secondary actions are depression (most effective when the eye is adducted) and abduction. Inferior oblique is the antagonist — extorsion, elevation, and abduction. Clinically the pure actions are tested in specific gaze positions to isolate each muscle.

Spiral of Tillaux and Surgical Relevance

The rectus muscles insert into the sclera at varying distances from the limbus, forming a spiral pattern. Medial rectus inserts closest at 5.5 mm, inferior rectus at 6.5 mm, lateral rectus at 6.9 mm, and superior rectus farthest at 7.7 mm. This matters when planning strabismus surgery — the amount of recession or resection needed depends on where you start from.

Yoke Muscles and Hering's Law

Yoke muscles are the pairs of muscles that work together for conjugate gaze in each direction. Hering's law of equal innervation says that both muscles of a yoke pair receive equal neural impulses. The yoke pairs are: right lateral rectus with left medial rectus (for right gaze), right superior rectus with left inferior oblique (for right elevation), and right inferior rectus with left superior oblique (for right depression). Understanding this is the key to interpreting the Parks three-step test for superior oblique palsies.

Visual Pathway in Detail

From Retina to Cortex

Each retinal quadrant maps to a specific part of the pathway and cortex. Nasal fibres of each retina cross at the chiasm to join the opposite optic tract. Temporal fibres of each retina do not cross and stay ipsilateral. The result is that the left optic tract carries information from the right visual field of both eyes. This is why lesions after the chiasm produce contralateral (homonymous) defects affecting the same half of the visual field in both eyes.

Fig 1.1 — Visual pathway from retina to primary cortex showing key lesion sites and corresponding visual field defects

Lateral Geniculate Nucleus

The LGN has six layers. Layers 1 and 2 are magnocellular — large cells processing motion and low spatial frequency. Layers 3 through 6 are parvocellular — smaller cells handling fine detail and colour. Layers 1, 4, and 6 receive input from the ipsilateral eye; layers 2, 3, and 5 from the contralateral eye. Knowing this level of detail is PG territory but it comes up in AIIMS questions about LGN anatomy.

Pupillary Light Reflex Pathway

Light enters the eye, activates photoreceptors, and the signal reaches the pretectal nucleus of the midbrain via the optic tract. From the pretectal nucleus, fibres project bilaterally to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei (the parasympathetic component of CN III). From there the pathway goes via the ciliary ganglion in the orbit to the sphincter pupillae through the short ciliary nerves. The bilateral pretectal connections explain the consensual reflex — light in one eye constricts both pupils.

Near Reflex Triad

When you shift fixation from a distant to a near object, three things happen simultaneously: accommodation (ciliary muscle contracts, lens rounds up), convergence (both medial recti contract), and miosis (sphincter pupillae constricts). This is the near reflex triad. In Argyll Robertson pupils, the near reflex is intact but the light reflex is lost — a classic syphilitic finding that is the reverse of a normal situation.

Aqueous Humor Dynamics

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Production

Aqueous is produced by the epithelium of the ciliary processes at a rate of about 2 microlitres per minute. The non-pigmented inner ciliary epithelium does the actual secreting. The mechanism is predominantly active transport using sodium-potassium ATPase, accounting for around seventy percent of production. Ultrafiltration contributes about twenty percent and simple diffusion the remainder. Aqueous fills the posterior chamber, passes through the pupil into the anterior chamber, and then drains. The composition is similar to plasma but with much lower protein content.

Trabecular Drainage

About eighty-five percent of aqueous drains through the trabecular meshwork, which sits in the angle between the cornea and iris. The meshwork is a fenestrated tissue; aqueous filters through it by bulk flow into the canal of Schlemm, which runs circumferentially around the anterior chamber angle. From Schlemm's canal it exits through collector channels into aqueous veins and ultimately into the episcleral venous plexus. The episcleral venous pressure (roughly 8 mmHg) sets the baseline below which IOP cannot fall, no matter how good drainage becomes.

Uveoscleral Drainage

The remaining fifteen percent takes a different route entirely, flowing between the ciliary muscle fibres into the suprachoroidal space and then out through the scleral pores. This pathway is pressure-independent, which is why prostaglandin analogues (which increase uveoscleral outflow by relaxing the ciliary muscle) are so effective at lowering IOP even in patients with normal or low-normal IOP.

IOP and Its Determinants

IOP is determined by the balance between aqueous production and drainage, modified by episcleral venous pressure. The formula is essentially: IOP = (production / outflow facility) + episcleral venous pressure. Diurnal variation is significant — IOP peaks in the early morning hours (often between 6 and 8 AM) when cortisol-related factors increase aqueous production and drainage is slower. This is why single IOP readings can be misleading and phasing (multiple readings over a day) is sometimes necessary in glaucoma workup.

Goldmann's equation: IOP = F/C + Pe, where F = aqueous flow rate, C = outflow coefficient (facility), Pe = episcleral venous pressure (normal ~8 mmHg). Any drug that lowers IOP must work by reducing F or increasing C — there is no pharmacological way to lower Pe reliably.

Pupillary Pathways and Reflexes

PGHigh-Yield
▼

Sympathetic Pathway (Dilator Pupillae and Muller's Muscle)

The sympathetic supply to the eye runs as a three-neuron arc. The first-order neuron descends from the hypothalamus through the brainstem and cervical spinal cord to the ciliospinal centre of Budge at C8-T1. The second-order neuron exits through the T1 root, travels over the apex of the lung and subclavian artery, loops around the carotid to the superior cervical ganglion at the carotid bifurcation. The third-order neuron then follows the internal carotid artery through the cavernous sinus, joins the nasociliary branch of V1, and enters the orbit to supply the dilator pupillae, Muller's muscle of the upper lid, and the lower lid retractors. Interruption anywhere along this arc produces Horner's syndrome: miosis, partial ptosis, and anhidrosis (depending on which part is affected).

Parasympathetic Pathway (Sphincter Pupillae)

Parasympathetic fibres travel with CN III from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus to the ciliary ganglion just posterior to the globe. Post-ganglionic fibres then travel through the short ciliary nerves to reach the sphincter pupillae and the ciliary muscle. These fibres run on the outside of the CN III nerve trunk, which is why a posterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing CN III from outside will produce an early, dilated pupil — the parasympathetic fibres are hit first. By contrast, ischaemic CN III palsy (from diabetes) affects the central microvascular supply, sparing the outer parasympathetics — hence pupil-sparing CN III palsy in diabetics.

Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (RAPD)

The swinging flashlight test detects asymmetry in the afferent pathway. You swing a bright light from one eye to the other in a darkened room. Normally both pupils constrict when the light is in either eye. If there is a defect in the afferent pathway of one eye (optic nerve disease, severe retinal disease), shining the light in that eye produces less constriction than shining it in the normal eye. When you swing to the abnormal eye, both pupils dilate slightly. This paradoxical dilation is the RAPD. It is the single most objective sign of optic nerve asymmetry you can find without equipment.

RAPD is not caused by cataract or media opacities alone. Dense vitreous haemorrhage can occasionally produce a mild RAPD, but any significant RAPD should prompt evaluation of the optic nerve and retina.

Four fundamental examination skills that every ophthalmology student must master before entering the wards. These are not just exam techniques — they are the clinical anchors that let you localise disease and monitor progression.

Testing Visual Acuity Basic Examination
1
Preparation

Set up the Snellen chart at 6 metres (or use a mirror system at 3 metres with a reversed chart). Ensure adequate room illumination. Test each eye separately — always cover the other eye with an occluder, not the patient's own fingers, which can part to peek.

2
Recording the Result

Acuity is recorded as a fraction: distance tested over the distance at which a normal eye should read that line. So 6/6 means the patient reads at 6 metres what a normal eye reads at 6 metres. 6/60 means they can only read what a normal eye reads at 60 metres. In the USA this is in feet (20/20). Always record the best corrected acuity with the patient's own glasses if they wear them.

If the patient cannot read the top letter (6/60), move closer: 3/60 or 1/60. Below that, test counting fingers (CF), hand movements (HM), perception of light (PL), or no perception of light (NPL).
3
Pinhole Test

Hold a pinhole over the eye and repeat the chart reading. A pinhole eliminates most refractive error by limiting the light cone to the paraxial rays. If acuity improves significantly with the pinhole, the cause of blur is likely refractive — glasses will help. If there is no improvement, the cause is structural — media opacity, retinal disease, or optic nerve pathology. This is one of the most useful quick tests you have.

A pinhole does not improve vision if the cornea or media is severely irregular, because even paraxial rays are distorted.
4
Near Vision with Jaeger Chart

Hold the Jaeger chart at 33 cm in good lighting. N6 or J1 is the standard for normal near vision. Near vision testing matters separately because a patient can have normal distance acuity and significantly impaired near vision (early presbyopia, or accommodation palsy). Always record near acuity with the patient's reading glasses if they use them.

Confrontation Visual Field Testing Neurological Localisation
1
Setup

Sit directly opposite the patient at arm's length. Cover one of your own eyes and ask the patient to cover the corresponding eye on their side (your right, their left). Both of you now fixate on each other's uncovered eye. Your own visual field serves as the reference.

2
Finger Counting in Each Quadrant

Bring your hand into each of the four quadrants midway between you and the patient. Present one, two, or five fingers and ask the patient to count them. Compare each quadrant between eyes. A patient who cannot count fingers in a quadrant where you can has a field defect in that quadrant.

For subtle defects, use the red-desaturation test — show a bright red target in each quadrant and ask if it appears the same brightness. The affected side will appear dimmer or pinkish rather than red.
3
Interpreting the Pattern

Superior field loss in one eye with an altitudinal pattern suggests ischaemic optic neuropathy. Bitemporal loss affecting both eyes points to the chiasm — ask about headaches and check for pituitary signs. A homonymous defect (same half-field in both eyes) means the lesion is post-chiasmal. A superior quadrantanopia in the contralateral field of a patient with a stroke history should make you think of the temporal lobe and Meyer's loop.

Pupillary Reflex Examination Afferent and Efferent Pathway
1
Direct and Consensual Light Reflex

Dim the room. Shine a bright torch into one eye from slightly below and lateral so the patient is not trying to accommodate to a near target. A normal direct reflex is prompt constriction of the illuminated pupil. The consensual reflex is simultaneous constriction of the opposite pupil. Record whether each reflex is present, sluggish, or absent, and note any anisocoria (unequal pupils).

2
Swinging Flashlight Test for RAPD

This is the key test for optic nerve asymmetry. In a dim room, swing a bright light from one eye to the other, spending about 3 seconds on each side. Watch the pupil you are illuminating. Normally it stays constricted as you move from eye to eye. If one eye has an afferent defect, when you swing the light to that eye both pupils dilate instead of remaining constricted — because the signal from that eye is weaker. The dilation is graded 1 to 4+. Even a subtle RAPD suggests a significant afferent defect.

Make sure you are not testing the near reflex accidentally. Keep the target far and the light positioned from below.
3
Near Reflex

Ask the patient to look at a distant object and then quickly at your finger held about 30 cm away. Both pupils should constrict as part of the near triad. Preserved near reflex with absent light reflex is the hallmark of Argyll Robertson pupils. The reverse (light reflex intact, near reflex absent or poor) is not a well-characterised clinical syndrome.

Colour Vision Testing with Ishihara Plates Optic Nerve and Cone Function
1
Technique

Test each eye separately in good daylight or matched artificial lighting. Hold the plate at 75 cm. Give the patient a maximum of three seconds per plate. The full Ishihara set has 38 plates; the screening set has 24. A normal person reads all or nearly all plates correctly. Reading fewer than 13 of 17 test plates correctly suggests a deficiency.

2
Interpreting Errors

Protan defects (protanopia or protanomaly) indicate reduced sensitivity to red. Deutan defects (deuteranopia or deuteranomaly) indicate reduced sensitivity to green. Protan and deutan defects together account for about 8% of males and 0.5% of females. The Ishihara plates do not reliably distinguish protan from deutan — for that you need the Farnsworth D-15 or Nagel anomaloscope. Acquired colour vision defects (from optic nerve disease) tend to involve red-green discrimination predominantly, and they are monocular or asymmetric, unlike congenital defects which are always bilateral and symmetric.

A patient with a subtle optic neuritis may have normal Snellen acuity but clearly impaired colour vision. The red-desaturation test ("does this red target look as bright and saturated as the same target in the other eye?") is a quick bedside screen for optic nerve disease.
Twelve undergraduate-level MCQs covering the factual anatomy and physiology that appear in MBBS theory and practical exams.
Q1

Which of the following accurately describes the three coats of the eyeball from outside inward?

UG
A Uveal, fibrous, retinal
B Fibrous, vascular (uveal), nervous (retinal)
C Scleral, choroidal, photoreceptor
D Corneal, ciliary, optic
Correct: B

The three coats are fibrous (sclera + cornea), vascular/uveal (choroid + ciliary body + iris), and nervous (retina). This classification is the starting point for all ocular anatomy.

Q2

Nasal fibres of the retina cross at which structure in the visual pathway?

UG
A Lateral geniculate nucleus
B Optic tract
C Optic chiasm
D Primary visual cortex
Correct: C

Nasal fibres from each retina cross at the optic chiasm to join the contralateral optic tract. Temporal fibres stay ipsilateral. This crossing is why chiasmal lesions produce bitemporal hemianopia.

Q3

Aqueous humor drains primarily (85%) through which route?

UG
A Trabecular meshwork to canal of Schlemm
B Uveoscleral pathway
C Ciliary body directly to bloodstream
D Through the lens capsule
Correct: A

The trabecular route accounts for 85% of aqueous drainage. Aqueous passes through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm and exits through collector channels to episcleral veins. The uveoscleral pathway handles the remaining 15%.

Q4

The normal cup-to-disc (C:D) ratio of the optic disc is considered within normal limits if it is less than:

UG
A 0.3
B 0.5
C 0.7
D 0.9
Correct: B

A C:D ratio of less than 0.5 is generally considered normal. Ratios of 0.6 or greater warrant careful evaluation for glaucoma, and asymmetry of more than 0.2 between the two eyes is suspicious regardless of the absolute value.

Q5

A lesion in Meyer's loop (inferior fibres of the optic radiation in the temporal lobe) causes which visual field defect?

UGHigh-Yield
A Bitemporal hemianopia
B Contralateral inferior quadrantanopia
C Contralateral superior quadrantanopia
D Ipsilateral monocular blindness
Correct: C

Meyer's loop carries inferior retinal fibres, which represent the superior visual field. A temporal lobe lesion here produces a contralateral superior quadrantanopia — classically called "pie in the sky." Parietal lobe (Baum's loop) lesions produce the opposite: inferior quadrantanopia or "pie on the floor."

Q6

The binocular visual field extends approximately how many degrees in horizontal diameter?

UG
A 60 degrees
B 90 degrees
C 150 degrees
D 180 degrees
Correct: D

The total monocular visual field extends about 150–160 degrees horizontally. The binocular overlap zone is about 120 degrees. Including the monocular temporal crescents (about 30 degrees each side), the combined binocular field spans close to 180 degrees horizontally.

Q7

Which corneal layer, once damaged, cannot regenerate and instead forms a permanent scar?

UG
A Bowman's layer
B Epithelium
C Stroma
D Descemet's membrane
Correct: A

Bowman's layer is acellular and cannot regenerate. Injury results in permanent scar formation. The epithelium regenerates readily from limbal stem cells. The stroma partially regenerates. Descemet's membrane is regenerated by endothelial cells.

Q8

Which of the following extraocular muscles is supplied by the trochlear nerve (CN IV)?

UG
A Inferior oblique
B Superior rectus
C Superior oblique
D Lateral rectus
Correct: C

CN IV (trochlear nerve) exclusively supplies the superior oblique. The mnemonic LR6 SO4 makes this unforgettable: Lateral Rectus = CN VI, Superior Oblique = CN IV, everything else = CN III.

Q9

The sympathetic nerve supply to the dilator pupillae travels via which pathway?

UG
A Short ciliary nerves from ciliary ganglion
B Three-neuron arc from hypothalamus through ciliospinal centre to superior cervical ganglion
C Directly from CN III (oculomotor) nucleus
D Via the pterygopalatine ganglion
Correct: B

The sympathetic supply runs as a three-neuron arc: first neuron from hypothalamus to ciliospinal centre (C8-T1), second neuron up through the chest and neck to the superior cervical ganglion, third neuron along the ICA into the orbit. Interruption anywhere causes Horner's syndrome.

Q10

Rods are most densely concentrated in which area of the retina?

UG
A Peripheral retina, with maximum density at about 20 degrees eccentricity
B Central fovea
C Optic disc
D Nasal retina only
Correct: A

Rods are absent from the central fovea (which has cones only) and peak in density at about 20 degrees from the fovea in a ring-like distribution. There are no photoreceptors at the optic disc. This peripheral rod distribution explains why night vision is best when you look slightly to the side of an object rather than directly at it.

Q11

In the lateral geniculate nucleus, which layers receive input from the contralateral eye?

UGPG
A Layers 1, 4, and 6
B Layers 2, 3, and 5
C Layers 1, 2, and 3
D Layers 4, 5, and 6
Correct: B

LGN layers 2, 3, and 5 receive input from the contralateral eye. Layers 1, 4, and 6 receive ipsilateral eye input. The magnocellular layers are 1 and 2 (motion, low spatial frequency); parvocellular layers are 3 through 6 (colour and fine detail).

Q12

The near reflex triad consists of which three responses?

UG
A Accommodation, divergence, and mydriasis
B Convergence, accommodation, and pupillary dilation
C Accommodation, convergence, and miosis
D Miosis, divergence, and relaxation of ciliary muscle
Correct: C

The near reflex triad is accommodation (ciliary muscle contracts, zonules relax, lens rounds up), convergence (both medial recti contract), and miosis (sphincter pupillae constricts). In Argyll Robertson pupil, the light reflex is absent but the near reflex — including miosis — is intact.

Twelve clinical scenario-based MCQs at NEET PG / DNB level. These test anatomical correlates in clinical situations, not just recall.
Q1

A 45-year-old woman presents with loss of peripheral vision in both temporal fields. MRI shows a lesion compressing the optic chiasm from above. What is the most likely diagnosis?

PGClinical
A Meningioma of the sphenoid wing
B Craniopharyngioma (typically affects chiasm from above in children)
C Pituitary adenoma
D Optic glioma
Correct: C

Pituitary adenomas compress the chiasm from below, but a large macroadenoma superiorly extending can compress it from below-central. The classic bitemporal hemianopia from a pituitary adenoma compresses the crossing nasal fibres centrally. Craniopharyngiomas typically arise above the chiasm in children. The clinical and imaging context here points to pituitary disease in a middle-aged woman — check for prolactin, GH, and cortisol axis.

Q2

A patient develops sudden loss of vision in one eye with a pale, oedematous retina and a cherry-red spot at the fovea. The fovea appears red because:

PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A The thin foveola allows choroidal circulation to show through against the surrounding pale ischaemic retina
B Haemorrhage accumulates selectively at the fovea
C The CRA sends a branch specifically to the fovea
D Cone pigments absorb more light at the fovea
Correct: A

In CRAO, the inner retinal layers become oedematous and white because they are ischaemic. At the foveola, the retina is extremely thin with no overlying inner layers, so the underlying choroidal circulation remains visible as red-orange. The contrast between the surrounding pale retina and the visible choroid at the foveola creates the classic cherry-red spot.

Q3

During vitreoretinal surgery, the pars plana entry point in a phakic patient is placed how far from the limbus?

PGClinical
A 1–2 mm
B 2–3 mm
C 5–6 mm
D 3.5–4 mm
Correct: D

The pars plana (safe zone between the ciliary processes and ora serrata) is entered at 3.5–4 mm from the limbus in phakic patients. In pseudophakic or aphakic eyes, 3–3.5 mm is used. Too anterior risks hitting the lens; too posterior risks retinal damage.

Q4

A 60-year-old man with well-controlled diabetes presents with a painless, complete CN III palsy with pupil sparing. The most likely cause is:

PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A Posterior communicating artery aneurysm
B Ischaemic microvascular CN III palsy
C Cavernous sinus thrombosis
D Uncal herniation
Correct: B

Ischaemic microvascular CN III palsy (from diabetes or hypertension) characteristically spares the pupil because the parasympathetic fibres on the outer surface of the nerve are preserved when the central microvasculature is affected. A posterior communicating aneurysm compresses from outside and hits the outer pupillomotor fibres first, producing a painful CN III palsy with early pupil involvement. Pupil-sparing + painless + vascular risk factors = ischaemic in origin until proven otherwise.

Q5

The swinging flashlight test reveals a Marcus Gunn pupil (RAPD) in the right eye. This indicates a defect in the:

PGClinical
A Efferent pathway of the right eye
B Sympathetic supply to the right iris
C Afferent pathway of the right eye (retina or optic nerve)
D Ciliary ganglion on the right side
Correct: C

RAPD indicates an afferent pathway defect. The afferent limb carries visual information from the retina and optic nerve to the pretectal nucleus. When this is compromised on one side, swinging the light to that eye produces less constriction (because less signal reaches the pretectum), and both pupils dilate. The efferent pathway (CN III) controls the motor response; if that were damaged, you would see a fixed dilated pupil, not an RAPD.

Q6

A patient has vision preserved in a central strip despite complete central retinal artery occlusion. The most likely anatomical explanation is:

PGClinical
A Presence of a cilioretinal artery supplying the papillomacular bundle
B Dual blood supply to the inner retina at the fovea
C Collateral supply from the choroidal vasculature to inner retina
D Recanalization of the CRA within minutes
Correct: A

A cilioretinal artery, present in about 20% of people, arises from the posterior ciliary circulation rather than the CRA. It enters the disc nasally and supplies the papillomacular bundle. If CRAO occurs and a cilioretinal artery is present, central vision is preserved even as the peripheral vision is lost. This is an important anatomical variant to know.

Q7

Prostaglandin analogues (e.g., latanoprost) lower IOP primarily by:

PGHigh-Yield
A Reducing aqueous production from the ciliary epithelium
B Increasing trabecular outflow facility
C Reducing episcleral venous pressure
D Increasing uveoscleral outflow by relaxing the ciliary muscle
Correct: D

Prostaglandin analogues increase uveoscleral outflow. They act on prostaglandin FP receptors in the ciliary muscle, causing remodelling of the extracellular matrix and relaxation of the muscle, which widens the spaces between ciliary muscle fibres and allows more aqueous to exit through the suprachoroidal route. Beta-blockers reduce aqueous production; carbonic anhydrase inhibitors also reduce production; pilocarpine increases trabecular outflow.

Q8

The IOP measurement by Goldmann applanation tonometry is unreliable when:

PGClinical
A The patient is lying supine
B Central corneal thickness (CCT) is abnormally thin or thick
C There is significant cataract
D The patient is above 60 years of age
Correct: B

Goldmann applanation is calibrated for a CCT of 520 microns. A thicker cornea gives a falsely high reading; a thinner cornea gives a falsely low reading. Post-LASIK eyes have thin corneas and may have their true IOP underestimated. This is why pachymetry is an essential part of glaucoma evaluation.

Q9

The Spiral of Tillaux refers to:

PG
A The spiral pattern formed by the varying distances of rectus muscle insertions from the limbus (MR 5.5 mm, IR 6.5 mm, LR 6.9 mm, SR 7.7 mm)
B The spiral course of the optic nerve in the orbit
C The helical structure of collagen in the corneal stroma
D The pattern of retinal nerve fibre layer defects in glaucoma
Correct: A

The Spiral of Tillaux describes the distances of rectus muscle insertions from the limbus: MR 5.5 mm, IR 6.5 mm, LR 6.9 mm, SR 7.7 mm. These distances increase progressively, forming a spiral. Surgically, the lateral rectus insertion is the most posterior and the medial rectus the most anterior.

Q10

Which structure forms the inner surface of the choroid and acts as a barrier between the choroidal vasculature and the RPE?

PG
A Internal limiting membrane
B Choriocapillaris
C Bruch's membrane
D External limiting membrane
Correct: C

Bruch's membrane is a five-layered structure (RPE basement membrane, inner collagenous zone, elastic layer, outer collagenous zone, choriocapillaris basement membrane) that separates the RPE from the choroidal circulation. Breaks in Bruch's membrane allow choroidal new vessel growth into the subretinal space — this is the basis of choroidal neovascularisation in wet AMD.

Q11

A 30-year-old man has ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis of the right face after a right-sided pancoast tumour. The second-order neuron of the sympathetic supply is affected. This neuron travels via:

PGClinicalHigh-Yield
A Hypothalamus to ciliospinal centre
B Ciliospinal centre (C8-T1) over the apex of the lung to the superior cervical ganglion
C Superior cervical ganglion along the ICA into the orbit
D Through the ciliary ganglion to the iris
Correct: B

The second-order sympathetic neuron exits C8-T1, hooks over the apex of the lung and the subclavian artery, and ascends to the superior cervical ganglion at the bifurcation of the carotid. A Pancoast tumour at the lung apex compresses this neuron, producing Horner's syndrome with anhidrosis of the face (because the sweating fibres are also disrupted at this level). Third-order neuron lesions typically produce Horner's without anhidrosis.

Q12

The episcleral venous pressure is approximately 8 mmHg. This value represents:

PG
A The driving pressure for aqueous production
B The maximum IOP achievable in a normal eye
C The pressure at which the trabecular meshwork collapses
D The minimum IOP achievable regardless of how much drainage is increased
Correct: D

Episcleral venous pressure sets the baseline below which IOP cannot fall. Even with perfect drainage, aqueous cannot flow "uphill" against venous pressure. This is why trabeculectomy or drainage tubes cannot reduce IOP below the episcleral venous pressure of approximately 8 mmHg. Elevated episcleral venous pressure (from carotid-cavernous fistula or Sturge-Weber) raises IOP independent of aqueous dynamics.

Four long-answer questions at university and PG examination level. Expand each to see a structured answer outline.
LA 1

Describe the layers of the retina with their functions and blood supply.

10 Marks
Introduction

Briefly state that the retina is the innermost coat of the eye, approximately 0.5 mm thick peripherally and 0.1 mm at the fovea, and that it consists of ten histological layers arranged between the vitreous and the choroid.

Ten Layers (inside outward)

Name and describe each: Inner Limiting Membrane (basal lamina of Muller cells), Nerve Fibre Layer (ganglion cell axons forming the optic nerve), Ganglion Cell Layer (2nd-order neurons; 8–10 layers thick at macula), Inner Plexiform Layer (synapse between bipolar and ganglion cells), Inner Nuclear Layer (bipolar, amacrine, horizontal, and Muller cells), Outer Plexiform Layer (Henle's fibre layer at macula; synapses between photoreceptors and bipolars), Outer Nuclear Layer (nuclei of photoreceptors), External Limiting Membrane (junctional complexes of Muller cells), Photoreceptor Layer (inner and outer segments of rods and cones), Retinal Pigment Epithelium (single layer of hexagonal cells, metabolically active, phagocytoses shed outer segments, part of blood-retinal barrier).

Rods and Cones

120 million rods (peripheral, scotopic, rhodopsin) vs 6 million cones (central, photopic, 3 types). Macula: 5 mm; fovea: 1.5 mm; foveola: 0.35 mm — cones only, avascular.

Blood Supply

Inner retina (inner 6 layers): central retinal artery, branch of ophthalmic artery, true end artery. Outer retina (outer 4 layers including photoreceptors and RPE): choriocapillaris from short posterior ciliary arteries. The two systems meet at a watershed zone. Clinical correlation: CRA occlusion = inner retinal ischaemia; choroidal ischaemia = outer retinal failure.

Clinical Correlations

Detachment separates neurosensory retina from RPE at the potential space between layers 9 and 10. Macular oedema = fluid in outer plexiform layer (Henle's). Glaucoma damages the nerve fibre layer first.

LA 2

Draw and describe the visual pathway. Add the effects of lesions at each level.

15 Marks
Pathway Description

Photoreceptors (rods and cones) transduce light to electrical signal. Bipolar cells (1st order) synapse with ganglion cells (2nd order) whose axons form the nerve fibre layer and exit as the optic nerve at the optic disc. The two optic nerves join at the optic chiasm where nasal fibres from each retina cross. The optic tracts carry fibres from the contralateral visual hemifield to the LGN (thalamus). From LGN, optic radiation fibres pass to the primary visual cortex (Area 17, calcarine fissure). Inferior fibres loop through the temporal lobe (Meyer's loop). Superior fibres pass through the parietal lobe (Baum's loop).

Diagram

Draw the schematic with both eyes, chiasm, tracts, LGN, radiation loops, and cortex. Label all structures.

Lesion Effects

Optic nerve: monocular blindness on that side. Chiasm (central): bitemporal hemianopia. Optic tract: incongruent contralateral homonymous hemianopia. LGN: similar to tract but rare. Meyer's loop (temporal): contralateral superior quadrantanopia "pie in sky." Baum's loop (parietal): contralateral inferior quadrantanopia "pie on floor." Occipital cortex: congruent homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing (because the macular cortex has dual blood supply from MCA and PCA).

LGN Detail (PG)

6 layers: 1,2 magnocellular; 3-6 parvocellular. Ipsilateral input: 1,4,6. Contralateral: 2,3,5.

LA 3

Describe aqueous humor — its formation, composition, circulation and drainage pathways.

10 Marks
Formation

Secreted by non-pigmented ciliary epithelium of the pars plicata. Rate: ~2 μL/min. Mechanism: active secretion (Na-K ATPase, 70%), ultrafiltration (20%), diffusion (10%). Diurnal variation: maximum in early morning.

Composition

Ultrafiltrate of plasma but protein-free (maintains optical clarity and reduced immune privilege). Contains ascorbic acid (antioxidant), glucose (nutrition for avascular structures), amino acids, low molecular weight proteins, and ions. pH approximately 7.2.

Circulation

Produced in posterior chamber, flows through pupil into anterior chamber (convection currents: warm aqueous rises anteriorly, cools at cornea, falls temporally). This convective flow explains why cells in uveitis settle as keratic precipitates in the inferior cornea.

Drainage

Trabecular (85%): trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal → collector channels → aqueous veins → episcleral veins. Pressure-dependent. Uveoscleral (15%): between ciliary muscle fibres → suprachoroidal space → scleral pores. Pressure-independent; enhanced by prostaglandins. IOP = F/C + Pe.

Clinical Relevance

Glaucoma = imbalance (usually reduced outflow). Aqueous flare = protein leak in uveitis. Hyphaema = blood in anterior chamber. Drugs that lower IOP work by reducing production (beta-blockers, CAIs) or increasing outflow (prostaglandins, pilocarpine).

LA 4

Enumerate the extraocular muscles. Describe their nerve supply, actions, and clinical significance.

10 Marks
The Six Muscles

Four recti (superior, inferior, medial, lateral) and two obliques (superior, inferior). All originate from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex except the inferior oblique, which arises from the anteromedial orbital floor.

Actions

Medial rectus: adduction (CN III). Lateral rectus: abduction (CN VI). Superior rectus: elevation, intorsion, adduction (CN III). Inferior rectus: depression, extorsion, adduction (CN III). Superior oblique: intorsion, depression in adduction, abduction (CN IV). Inferior oblique: extorsion, elevation in adduction, abduction (CN III).

Spiral of Tillaux

Insertion distances from limbus: MR 5.5 mm, IR 6.5 mm, LR 6.9 mm, SR 7.7 mm — the spiral matters for strabismus surgery.

Hering's Law and Yoke Muscles

Equal innervation to yoke pairs: right LR and left MR (right gaze); right SR and left IO (right elevation); right IR and left SO (right depression). Overaction of IO with underaction of SO in CN IV palsy illustrates this.

Clinical Significance

CN III palsy: ptosis, eye down and out, dilated pupil (surgical) or pupil-sparing (ischaemic). CN IV palsy: superior oblique palsy, vertical diplopia, head tilt to opposite side (Parks three-step test). CN VI palsy: convergent squint, failure to abduct, diplopia on lateral gaze. Thyroid eye disease affects IO most commonly. Blowout fracture traps IR. Duane's syndrome involves co-innervation of MR and LR.

Six questions drawn from AIIMS, NEET PG, and DNB papers. Expand each for the answer and approach.
AIIMS 2019

Bowman's membrane is absent in which of the following structures?

AIIMS PG

Answer: Conjunctiva. Bowman's layer is unique to the cornea. It is absent in the conjunctiva, sclera, lens, and retina. The question is a reminder to associate Bowman's specifically with the corneal structure and not generalise it to the entire anterior segment.

NEET PG 2022

A patient presents with bitemporal hemianopia. The most common cause at the optic chiasm is:

NEET PG

Answer: Pituitary adenoma. The pituitary gland sits below the optic chiasm in the sella turcica. A growing adenoma compresses the crossing nasal fibres from below, cutting off temporal field information from both eyes and producing classic bitemporal hemianopia. Craniopharyngiomas can do the same, typically from above in children. Always check prolactin, GH, and cortisol when you find this field defect.

DNB 2021

The fovea centralis is characterised by all of the following EXCEPT:

DNB CET

Answer: Presence of rods. The fovea centralis is characterised by cones only (no rods), avascularity, high visual acuity, and lateral displacement of inner retinal layers. The foveola (central 0.35 mm) is the thinnest part of the retina. Rods are most dense at about 20 degrees of eccentricity from the fovea, not within it.

AIIMS 2018

Meyer's loop lesion in the temporal lobe causes which field defect?

AIIMS PG

Answer: Contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky"). Meyer's loop carries inferior retinal fibres representing the superior visual field. Temporal lobe pathology (tumour, infarct, epilepsy surgery) affecting this loop produces a defect in the contralateral superior quadrant of both eyes. The key is to remember that inferior retina = superior field, and the loop goes into the temporal lobe.

NEET PG 2020

Which of the following best describes the aqueous humor drainage via the uveoscleral route?

NEET PG

Answer: Pressure-independent drainage through ciliary muscle spaces to suprachoroidal space. About 15% of aqueous drains this way. Because it is independent of IOP, this route remains functional even when IOP is low. Prostaglandin analogues target this route by remodelling the ciliary muscle extracellular matrix. This is distinct from the trabecular route, which is pressure-dependent and accounts for 85% of drainage.

University Final MBBS

Enumerate the layers of the cornea. Write a note on the clinical significance of the corneal endothelium. (5 marks)

University Exam

Layers: Epithelium (5–6 layers, regenerates from limbal stem cells), Bowman's layer (acellular, no regeneration, scars permanently), Stroma (90% of thickness, Type I collagen lamellae, regular spacing for transparency), Descemet's membrane (basement membrane of endothelium, thickens with age), Endothelium (single hexagonal cell layer, Na-K ATPase pump, no regeneration in humans).

Clinical significance of endothelium: Normal density ~2500 cells/mm2. Below ~500 cells/mm2, the pump fails, stroma swells, cornea clouds — bullous keratopathy. Causes include surgical trauma (phacoemulsification), Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, and trauma. Treatment is endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK or DMEK). Pre-operative endothelial cell count (specular microscopy) is mandatory before any intraocular surgery.
