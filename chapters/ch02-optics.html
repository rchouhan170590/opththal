Optics & Refraction

From the physics of lenses and the refracting surfaces of the eye to the clinical management of myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism, and presbyopia — plus contact lenses and refractive surgery.

Errors of Refraction

In emmetropia, parallel rays from infinity focus exactly on the retina with the eye at rest. Any deviation from this is a refractive error.

  • Myopia: Far point lies in front of the retina. Image focused before it reaches the retina. Corrected with a concave (diverging, minus) lens.
  • Hypermetropia: Far point lies behind the retina. Eye needs to accommodate even for distant objects. Corrected with a convex (converging, plus) lens.
  • Astigmatism: Two principal meridians have different curvatures, so no single focal point exists — you get a conoid of Sturm instead. Corrected with cylindrical lenses.
  • Presbyopia: Loss of accommodative amplitude after the age of 40. The near point recedes beyond comfortable reading distance. Managed with a reading addition (convex add).

Accommodation

Accommodation is the eye's ability to increase its refractive power in order to focus on near objects. The mechanism follows Helmholtz's theory.

  • Ciliary muscle contracts, reducing the tension on the zonular fibres.
  • With zonules relaxed, the elastic lens capsule moulds the lens into a more convex shape.
  • A more convex lens has higher dioptric power — near objects come into focus.
  • Amplitude of accommodation is greatest in childhood (around 14D) and declines steadily with age. By 40 years, enough amplitude has been lost to make near work symptomatic — this is presbyopia.
  • The near point is the closest distance at which the eye can maintain clear focus. As amplitude falls, the near point moves farther away.

Myopia — Classification

Most myopia is axial — the eyeball is simply too long. The normal axial length is around 23.5 mm, and every additional millimetre adds roughly 3 dioptres of myopia.

  • Axial myopia: Most common. Axial length exceeds 23.5 mm.
  • Curvature myopia: Steeper than normal corneal or lens curvature.
  • Index myopia: Increased refractive index of the lens (seen in early nuclear cataract — the “second sight” phenomenon).
  • Positional myopia: Anterior displacement of the lens.

By degree: low (<3D), moderate (3–6D), high (>6D). Pathological myopia exceeds 6D and is accompanied by degenerative changes: posterior staphyloma, lattice degeneration, Fuchs’ spot (a pigmented scar from CNV at the macula), lacquer cracks in Bruch’s membrane, and peripapillary atrophy.

Refractive Surgery Options

  • LASIK: A hinged corneal flap (110–130 μm) is created, excimer laser ablates the underlying stroma, flap is repositioned. Fastest visual recovery. Best for myopia up to −10D.
  • PRK (photorefractive keratectomy): Surface ablation after epithelium removal. No flap. Slower healing, risk of subepithelial haze. Better for thin corneas or contact sports.
  • SMILE: Femtosecond laser cuts a lenticule within the stroma; it is removed through a small 2–4 mm arc incision. No flap at all. Best biomechanical stability.
  • Phakic IOL / ICL: A Visian ICL (implantable collamer lens) is placed behind the iris in front of the crystalline lens. Preferred for high myopia (>10D) where corneal ablation would be unsafe.

IOL Power Calculation

Before cataract surgery, you need two measurements: the corneal curvature (by keratometry) and the axial length (by A-scan ultrasonography or optical biometry with IOLMaster). These feed into a formula along with an IOL-specific A-constant.

  • SRK-T formula: The most widely used third-generation formula. Works well for eyes of average length.
  • Haigis formula: Preferred for very short or very long eyes, as it uses three constants and accounts for anterior chamber depth.
  • Target refraction: Aim for emmetropia in most patients. A slight myopic target (−0.5D) is acceptable and helps near vision without glasses post-operatively.

Physical Optics — Light, Lenses, and the Reduced Eye

UGPG
▼

The Behaviour of Light

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform medium, but bends when it crosses the interface between two media of different optical densities — a process called refraction. The optical density of a medium is described by its refractive index (n), defined as the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to its speed in that medium. Water has a refractive index of 1.33, glass around 1.5, and the cornea approximately 1.376. The higher the refractive index, the more the light bends when it enters that medium.

Snell’s Law

Snell’s law states that n1 sinθ1 = n2 sinθ2, where θ is measured from the normal (perpendicular) to the interface. This simple relationship governs everything about how lenses and the eye focus light. When light moves from a less dense to a more dense medium (say, air into cornea), it bends toward the normal — converging. Moving the other way, it bends away — diverging.

Vergence and Lens Power

Vergence describes the degree of convergence or divergence of a bundle of rays and is measured in dioptres (D). Rays from a point at infinity are parallel and have zero vergence. Rays from a point 1 metre away have a vergence of −1D (diverging). A converging lens adds positive vergence. The power of a thin lens is simply P = 1/f, where f is the focal length in metres. So a lens that focuses parallel light at 25 cm has a power of +4D.

The Reduced Eye

The human eye has multiple refracting surfaces — the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, the anterior and posterior lens surfaces — each with slightly different radii of curvature and refractive indices. For most calculations, we simplify this to Listing’s reduced eye: a single refracting surface with a refractive index of 1.336, located 5.6 mm behind the anterior cornea (the nodal point approximation). The total power of the eye in this model is about 60D — the cornea contributing approximately 43D and the lens contributing the remaining 17D. The cornea is the dominant refracting structure. This is why corneal disease and corneal surgery have such a large effect on refraction.

Cardinal Points

Every optical system has six cardinal points: two principal planes (H and H′), two nodal points (N and N′), and two focal points (F and F′). For clinical purposes, the nodal point is the most useful — it is the point through which rays pass without changing their angle of incidence. In the human eye the anterior nodal point is about 7.2 mm behind the corneal apex and the posterior nodal point 7.5 mm behind. Angular subtense calculations for visual acuity use the nodal point as their reference.

Total power of the eye: Cornea contributes ~43D and the crystalline lens ~17D at rest, giving a total of ~60D. During maximum accommodation, the lens adds another 14D (in a young person), taking the total to around 74D.

Emmetropia and Ametropia

UGPG
▼

Defining Emmetropia

An emmetropic eye is one in which parallel rays from a distant object are brought to focus exactly on the retina when the eye is at rest — meaning the ciliary muscle is fully relaxed and the lens is in its flattest state. Emmetropia is the ideal, but it is not the most common state. Most people are mildly hypermetropic at birth and become progressively more emmetropic through childhood (emmetropisation).

Ametropia and Its Types

Ametropia simply means a refractive error exists. The far point — the point conjugate with the retina when the eye is relaxed — is displaced from optical infinity. In myopia the far point is at a finite distance in front of the eye. In hypermetropia the far point is virtual, lying behind the eye. In astigmatism there is no single far point; instead there are two focal lines at different distances (Sturm’s conoid).

The Spectacle Correction and Vertex Distance

The correcting lens is placed at the spectacle plane, conventionally 12 mm in front of the cornea (the vertex distance). Think of it this way: the correcting lens must create a virtual image of infinity at the patient’s far point. For a myope whose far point is at 50 cm, a concave lens of −2D placed at the spectacle plane will diverge parallel rays so they appear to come from 50 cm — exactly where the far point sits.

Vertex distance matters enormously in high prescriptions. A −10D lens at 12 mm from the cornea is not equivalent to a −10D lens held against the cornea. The effective power changes with distance. The formula is F′ = F / (1 − d·F), where d is the vertex distance in metres. For prescriptions above ±4D, always adjust for vertex distance when converting from trial frame to spectacle order.

Exam point: Vertex distance adjustment is a favourite numerical question. If a patient’s trial frame correction is −12D at 12 mm from the cornea, the equivalent contact lens power (at zero vertex distance) will be less minus — approximately −10.71D. Moving a lens closer to the eye reduces the effective minus power.

Myopia — From Classification to Pathological Changes

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Classification by Cause

The most common cause is axial elongation. An axial length greater than 23.5 mm in an adult means the image plane falls in front of the retina. Curvature myopia occurs when the anterior corneal surface is steeper than normal — you see this in keratoconus. Index myopia develops when the refractive index of the crystalline lens increases, something that happens in early nuclear sclerosis. This is the so-called “second sight” of incipient cataract, where patients who previously needed reading glasses suddenly find they can read without them. Positional myopia results from anterior displacement of the lens, as can occur after trauma.

Classification by Degree and Clinical Features

Low myopia is under 3D, moderate is 3–6D, and high myopia exceeds 6D. What you will see clinically in a straightforward myope: blurred distance vision, clear near vision, and often a tendency to hold objects very close. The eye itself appears larger because of the increased axial length. The anterior chamber is deeper. There may be a slight divergent tendency (exophoria or even esotropia in very young children, though myopia more classically associates with divergent squint than convergent).

Pathological Myopia

When myopia exceeds 6D with evidence of progressive axial elongation and degenerative fundal changes, it becomes pathological myopia — a distinct disease entity, not merely a strong glasses prescription. The sclera stretches, the choroid thins, and Bruch’s membrane develops cracks (lacquer cracks). The fundal changes include:

  • Posterior staphyloma — an outpouching of the posterior pole where the thinned sclera cannot maintain its shape
  • Peripapillary atrophy (a crescent of depigmentation around the optic disc)
  • Lattice degeneration with or without retinal breaks — important because it predisposes to retinal detachment
  • Choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) breaking through lacquer cracks, which leaves a pigmented scar at the macula called Fuchs’ spot
  • Vitreous degeneration and posterior vitreous detachment at an earlier age than in emmetropes

The term Foster-Fuchs’ spot refers specifically to the end-stage pigmented scar of regressed CNV at the macula. Do not confuse it with the active CNV lesion itself.

Management

Optical correction with concave lenses remains the standard. Contact lenses are an alternative and, by sitting closer to the cornea, actually require slightly less minus power than spectacles (vertex distance effect). Refractive surgery is an option once the refraction has been stable for at least two years. For pathological myopia, the concern is retinal complications — these patients need annual fundus examination and should be warned about symptoms of retinal detachment.

Myopia control has become an active area. Low-dose atropine 0.01% eye drops slow axial elongation in children with minimal side effects. Orthokeratology (wearing rigid moulding lenses overnight) and specially designed soft multifocal lenses also show meaningful control effects.

Hypermetropia — The Eye That Works Too Hard

UGPG
▼

Classification

Like myopia, hypermetropia can be axial (short eye), curvature-based (flatter cornea), index-related, or positional. Axial hypermetropia is most common. A hypermetropic eye is one where the power is insufficient for the length — parallel rays would focus behind the retina if nothing intervened.

Latent, Manifest, and Total Hypermetropia

This distinction is one of the most tested areas in refraction. The young hypermetrope has abundant accommodation and can compensate for some or all of the hypermetropia by continuously accommodating. The part that is masked this way is called latent hypermetropia. The part that remains after maximum voluntary effort (and is detectable with non-cycloplegic refraction) is manifest hypermetropia. Total hypermetropia is the full amount, only revealed by cycloplegic refraction — which is why you should never prescribe for hypermetropia in children without first doing cycloplegic refraction with atropine or cyclopentolate.

Clinical Presentation

A young person with mild hypermetropia may have no symptoms at all because they accommodate without difficulty. What you will see is asthenopia — eye strain, headaches behind the eyes, difficulty sustaining near work. As accommodation falters in the late thirties and forties, distance blur emerges as well. The classic association is with convergent squint (esotropia) in children, because the excessive accommodative effort to clear vision also drives excess convergence through the accommodative convergence reflex.

Amblyopia Risk

Uncorrected hypermetropia in childhood carries a real risk of amblyopia, particularly if it is asymmetric (anisometropia). The blurred retinal image in the more hypermetropic eye fails to provide the visual stimulation needed for normal cortical development. Full cycloplegic correction should be prescribed early, even if the child appears asymptomatic, specifically to protect visual development.

Clinical rule: Always use cycloplegic refraction (atropine 1% for three days, or cyclopentolate 1% for children older than 1 year) before prescribing for hypermetropia in any child under 12. Missing latent hypermetropia means under-correcting, which perpetuates amblyopia risk.

Astigmatism — Two Focal Lines, One Blurred Image

UGPG
▼

The Optics of Astigmatism

In astigmatism, the two principal meridians (usually roughly 90 degrees apart) have different refractive powers. Light entering through the steeper meridian comes to focus closer than light through the flatter meridian. The result is not a point focus but a three-dimensional shape called Sturm’s conoid — two focal lines separated by a circle of least confusion (the interval of Sturm). The circle of least confusion is the point of best compromise focus.

Regular Astigmatism

Regular astigmatism has perpendicular principal meridians and can be corrected with cylindrical lenses. It is further classified by the orientation of the steeper meridian:

  • With-the-rule (WTR): The vertical meridian (around 90 degrees) is steeper. Common in children and young adults. The eye has more power vertically, which happens to be useful because most scenes have more horizontal detail — the vertical power helps resolve horizontal edges.
  • Against-the-rule (ATR): The horizontal meridian is steeper. Becomes more prevalent with age as the cornea flattens vertically. A minus cylinder at axis 180 degrees corrects it.
  • Oblique: Principal meridians lie somewhere between 30–60 degrees and 120–150 degrees.

Irregular Astigmatism

Irregular astigmatism occurs when the principal meridians are not perpendicular, or when the curvature varies across the optical zone in an unpredictable way. The classical cause is keratoconus — a progressive ectasia where the corneal stroma thins and the apex protrudes. Corneal scarring after trauma or infection also produces irregular astigmatism. Spectacles and soft contact lenses cannot correct irregular astigmatism adequately because they present a smooth curved surface to the eye but cannot compensate for local irregularity. Rigid gas-permeable contact lenses are the key: they create a tear lens between their smooth back surface and the irregular cornea, neutralising the irregular optics of the cornea itself.

Detection and Correction

The stenopeic slit is a narrow aperture that, when rotated in front of the eye, allows the patient to identify the axis of clear vision — useful for detecting and roughly quantifying astigmatism. Keratometry measures the curvature of the central cornea directly. The Jackson cross-cylinder is the workhorse of refining the axis and power of the cylinder during subjective refraction.

Presbyopia and Accommodation

UGPG
▼

The Mechanism of Accommodation

Helmholtz’s theory remains the accepted mechanism. At rest, the zonular fibres are under tension and flatten the lens. When the ciliary muscle contracts (a ring of smooth muscle, so contraction reduces its internal diameter), the zonules go slack, and the inherently elastic lens capsule allows the lens to assume a more spherical shape. This increases the central radius of curvature and therefore the dioptric power. The lens can change its power by up to 14D in a young child.

Schachar’s competing theory proposes that equatorial zonules relax while anterior and posterior zonules increase tension, steepening the central lens. This theory is less widely accepted but underlies the rationale for some experimental surgical presbyopia treatments that attempt to alter zonular geometry.

Why Presbyopia Happens

The lens grows throughout life by laying down new fibres from the equatorial epithelium. The older fibres are compressed toward the nucleus, which gradually loses its elasticity. By the early forties the lens cannot deform sufficiently even when the ciliary muscle exerts full contractile force. The amplitude of accommodation falls by roughly 0.25D per year after age 8, and by the time a patient reaches 40–45 years, the remaining amplitude (around 4D) is no longer enough to sustain comfortable near work.

Reading Additions

The principle of the reading addition is to supply the dioptric power the lens can no longer generate itself. The addition is always convex (plus). A simple guide: +1.0D at 40 years, progressing to +2.5D at 55 years and reaching a plateau of around +3.0D by 60–65 years. Beyond +3D there is little practical benefit because the depth of focus of the eye provides the rest.

Optical Solutions

Bifocal lenses have a distance correction in the upper portion and a reading segment (the add) in the lower portion. The most common segments are the D-segment (flat-top) and round segment. Progressive addition lenses (PALs) avoid the visible dividing line and provide a continuous corridor of power from distance at the top to near at the bottom, with the intermediate zone in between. They take more adaptation but are cosmetically preferred. Reading glasses are single-vision plus lenses — suitable when the patient needs no distance correction or is happy to remove glasses for distance.

Contact Lenses — Types, Fitting, and Complications

UGPG
▼

Types of Contact Lenses

Soft contact lenses made from hydrogel (HEMA) or silicone hydrogel are the most commonly prescribed. They drape over the cornea and move minimally with blinking. Silicone hydrogel materials transmit significantly more oxygen than conventional hydrogels, reducing hypoxia-related complications. Daily disposable lenses carry the lowest infection risk. Monthly replacement lenses are economical but require rigorous cleaning and storage discipline.

Rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses sit smaller on the cornea (9–9.5 mm diameter vs 13.5–14.5 mm for soft lenses) and move considerably with each blink. Because they maintain their shape regardless of corneal contour, they create a tear lens that neutralises irregular astigmatism. They are the first-line contact lens for keratoconus and post-surgical corneal irregularity. Scleral lenses, which vault entirely over the cornea and rest on the bulbar conjunctiva, have extended this principle to severe irregularity where RGPs are intolerable.

Fitting Principles

The base-back optic zone radius (BOZR) is chosen in relation to the flat K reading from keratometry. For soft lenses, you typically aim for a slightly flatter fit than the flat K. For RGPs, you trial-fit and observe the fluorescein pattern — an ideal fit shows even fluorescein pooling under the lens with slight pooling at the periphery. The total diameter should allow the lens to centre well and move 1–2 mm with each blink.

Complications

The list of contact lens complications is long, but the ones you must know cold are:

  • Giant papillary conjunctivitis (GPC): A hypersensitivity response to lens deposits on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. Presents with itching, mucous discharge, and large papillae (>0.3 mm). Treat by changing or discontinuing lenses and adding mast cell stabilisers.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis: The organism of extended soft lens wear. Produces a rapidly progressive, aggressive corneal ulcer with a ring infiltrate. Requires intensive topical fluoroquinolones immediately.
  • Acanthamoeba keratitis: Classically in patients who rinse lenses with tap water, swim while wearing lenses, or have poor hygiene. The hallmark is a ring infiltrate and pain that is disproportionately severe compared to the clinical signs. Early disease may look like a dendritic keratitis, leading to misdiagnosis and incorrect antiviral treatment. Treatment is prolonged PHMB and chlorhexidine drops.
  • Contact lens peripheral ulcer (CLPU): A sterile peripheral ulcer from bacterial toxin response, not a true infection. Small, well-defined, usually heals with a short course of topical steroids plus antibiotic cover.
  • Hypoxia and neovascularisation: Chronic oxygen deprivation drives superficial corneal vessels in from the limbus (pannus formation). Using higher-Dk silicone hydrogel materials prevents this.

Refractive Surgery — LASIK, PRK, SMILE, and Phakic IOL

PGHigh-Yield
▼

The Principle

All corneal refractive procedures work by reshaping the anterior corneal surface to change its refractive power. The excimer laser (193 nm argon fluoride) ablates corneal tissue with extraordinary precision — removing approximately 0.25 μm of tissue per pulse without generating heat in adjacent tissue. The amount of tissue removed and its distribution across the optical zone determines the refractive change achieved.

LASIK

Laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis has been the dominant refractive procedure for three decades. A microkeratome or femtosecond laser creates a hinged flap of 110–130 μm thickness. The flap is lifted, the excimer laser ablates the exposed stroma, and the flap is repositioned. Visual recovery is rapid — most patients see well the following day. The 10% rule states that no more than 10% of the corneal thickness should be removed by ablation, and the residual stromal bed (below the flap) should be at least 250–300 μm. This limits LASIK to around −10D for an average cornea. The main complications are flap-related (displacement, striae, diffuse lamellar keratitis — “Sands of Sahara”) and regression of effect.

PRK (Photorefractive Keratectomy)

PRK removes the epithelium first (manually or with dilute alcohol) and then ablates the anterior stroma directly. There is no flap, which eliminates all flap-related complications and makes PRK the preferred option for patients in contact sports or military service. The trade-off is a slower, more uncomfortable recovery (3–5 days of epithelial healing) and the risk of subepithelial haze, particularly for higher corrections. Mitomycin C applied intraoperatively reduces haze formation significantly.

SMILE

Small incision lenticule extraction uses a femtosecond laser to cut a lenticule (a disc of stroma of carefully calculated thickness and diameter) within the intact cornea. The lenticule is then extracted through a 2–4 mm arc incision. No flap is created, and the anterior stroma is largely undisturbed. This preserves more corneal biomechanical integrity than LASIK. Dry eye symptoms are also less common because fewer corneal nerves are transected. SMILE is currently approved for myopia and myopic astigmatism; hypermetropic correction is under development.

Phakic IOL (Implantable Collamer Lens)

For high myopia beyond about −10D, corneal ablation would remove too much tissue. The Visian ICL is a posterior chamber phakic IOL placed between the iris and the crystalline lens, which is left in place. The vault — the clearance between the ICL and the anterior crystalline lens surface — must be adequate: too little causes anterior subcapsular cataract from contact, too much causes pigment dispersion and raised IOP. The ICL is reversible (can be explanted) and produces excellent optical quality because it sits close to the nodal point of the eye.

Contraindications to Corneal Refractive Surgery

  • Keratoconus or forme fruste keratoconus (corneal topography is mandatory pre-operatively)
  • Corneal thickness below 480 μm (insufficient residual bed after ablation)
  • Progressive myopia (refraction not stable for at least two years)
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding (refraction fluctuates)
  • Active ocular surface disease — dry eye, blepharitis, recurrent erosion

Clinical Technique
Streak Retinoscopy Objective Refraction
1
Setup and Principle

Retinoscopy is the objective measurement of a patient's refractive error using a streak retinoscope and trial lenses. You project a streak of light through the pupil onto the retina and observe the movement of the fundus reflex relative to the streak. At the neutral point, the reflex fills the pupil simultaneously with the streak — no movement is visible. The working distance (usually 67 cm or 1 metre) introduces a lens power offset that you subtract from your final result: 67 cm adds +1.5 D, and 1 metre adds +1 D of artefact.

2
With and Against Movement

When the reflex moves in the same direction as the streak, this is "with" movement and means the patient is hypermetropic for that distance (you neutralise with plus lenses). When the reflex moves in the opposite direction, this is "against" movement and means the patient is myopic beyond your working distance (you neutralise with minus lenses). With a myope sitting at 67 cm, you will typically see against movement and add minus lenses until the pupil flashes uniformly at neutrality. Scissors movement indicates irregular astigmatism or keratoconus.

In astigmatism, you will see a break in the reflex and differing movement in different meridians. Neutralise each principal meridian separately, then convert to a standard prescription.
3
Working Distance Correction

Once you find the neutralising power in each meridian, subtract your working distance lens. At 67 cm, subtract −1.50 D (or add −1.50 D algebraically). At 1 metre, subtract −1.00 D. The resulting prescription is your objective refraction, which you then refine with subjective techniques. In children who cannot cooperate with subjective refraction — particularly for detecting amblyopia — retinoscopy under cycloplegia (with cyclopentolate 1%) gives the most accurate result.

Subjective Refraction and Cross-Cylinder Technique Subjective Refraction
1
Fog and Reduce Method

Start from the retinoscopy result. "Fog" the patient by adding plus lenses until the acuity drops to about 6/12 — this relaxes any latent accommodation and ensures the eye is not compensating for your lenses. Then reduce the fog by removing plus (or adding minus) in small steps of 0.25 D, asking the patient whether each change makes the letters clearer or worse. Stop when any further reduction of plus fails to improve acuity. At that point you have the best sphere.

2
Refining the Cylinder with Jackson Cross-Cylinder

The Jackson cross-cylinder (JCC) is a lens with equal and opposite cylinder powers on perpendicular axes (typically ±0.25 D or ±0.50 D). It is used in two flips: one to find the correct axis of the cylinder and a second to find the correct power. For axis refinement, straddle the existing cylinder axis with the JCC and flip it; the patient chooses which flip gives a clearer image. If one flip is clearer, rotate the axis of the trial cylinder 10–15 degrees toward the minus axis of whichever JCC position was preferred, then repeat. For power, align the minus axis of the JCC with the trial cylinder and flip; if one position is consistently clearer, adjust the cylinder power. Add 0.25 D cylinder when the matching flip is preferred, remove it when the other flip wins.

3
Balancing and Final Check

Once the sphere and cylinder are refined monocularly, balance the two eyes so that accommodation is equally relaxed in both. The Humphriss immediate contrast method (fogging one eye by +0.75 D) or prism dissociation method (using a 4 D prism to separate the visual fields) are standard. Check the final binocular acuity, confirm that the near addition (for presbyopes) is comfortable at the patient's working distance, and confirm no prismatic effect is induced by the position of the optical centres.

Keratometry and Corneal Topography Pre-Operative Assessment
1
Manual Keratometry

The Javal-Schiotz keratometer measures the curvature of the central 3 mm of the cornea by projecting mires onto its anterior surface and measuring the distance between them. The instrument reads out in diopters and/or radius of curvature (millimetres). A typical cornea has a central radius of about 7.8 mm (approximately 43 D of refracting power). The difference in keratometry readings between the two principal meridians gives the corneal astigmatism. Manual keratometry is still used for contact lens base curve selection and IOL power calculation, though it has largely been supplemented by topography.

2
Corneal Topography and Tomography

Placido-disc based topographers measure the shape of the anterior corneal surface across thousands of points by analysing the reflection of concentric rings. They are indispensable before any refractive surgery because they detect forme fruste keratoconus — early irregular thinning that looks normal on slit-lamp examination. The pattern to watch for is inferior steepening on the axial curvature map, often described as a "crab claw" or asymmetric bowtie pattern. Corneal tomography (Scheimpflug imaging, as in Pentacam) additionally maps the posterior corneal surface and provides pachymetry — you get a complete three-dimensional model of the cornea rather than just the front surface.

Belin-Ambrosio Enhanced Ectasia Display (BAD-D score) integrates multiple parameters to give a statistical probability of ectasia. A BAD-D score above 1.6 should prompt caution before proceeding with corneal refractive surgery.

Myopia means rays focus in front of the retina. A concave (diverging, minus) lens diverges the rays before they enter the eye so the focal point shifts back to the retina. A convex lens would push the focal point even further forward, worsening the problem. Cylinders correct astigmatism; prisms correct deviation, not refractive error.

Presbyopia typically becomes symptomatic after which decade of life?
UG
A 3rd decade (30s)
B 4th decade (30s–40s)

Presbyopia becomes symptomatic when the near point recedes beyond comfortable working distance, typically around 40–45 years of age. B 1 D

Correct: A

Every 1 mm increase in axial length produces approximately 3 D of myopia, because the retina moves further back from where rays are converging. Normal axial length is about 24 mm. Against-the-rule astigmatism means the horizontal meridian is steeper, corrected with a minus cylinder at 90 degrees. Which of the following best defines emmetropia?
UG
A Parallel rays from infinity focus exactly on the retina with the eye at rest
B The eye can accommodate 10 dioptres of near work without glasses
C There is no difference in refractive power between the two principal meridians
D The far point lies exactly 6 metres from the eye
Correct: A

Emmetropia is the state where no refractive error exists: parallel rays from optical infinity (6 metres or beyond) focus on the retina when the eye is completely relaxed (no accommodation). Option D confuses emmetropia with the clinical definition of distance vision — the far point of an emmetropic eye is at infinity, not 6 metres (though 6 metres is close enough to treat as infinity optically).

A myopic patient's far point is 25 cm from the eye. What is their uncorrected refractive error?
UG
A −1.00 D
B −2.00 D
C −4.00 D
D −6.00 D
Correct: C

The refractive error in dioptres equals 100 divided by the far point distance in centimetres (for myopia). 100 ÷ 25 cm = 4 D of myopia. Equivalently, the far point of a myope is the reciprocal of their refractive error in metres: 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25 metres = 25 cm. The correcting lens must be −4.00 D to shift the far point to infinity.

7
Which type of contact lens is indicated for irregular astigmatism (such as keratoconus)?
UG
A Soft toric lens
B Rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lens
C Extended-wear soft lens
D Scleral lens is contraindicated in keratoconus
Correct: B

RGP lenses vault over the irregular cornea and create a new, regular refracting surface using the tear film between the lens and the cornea. This is why they improve vision in keratoconus far better than soft lenses, which simply drape over and conform to the irregular surface. Scleral lenses (a type of rigid lens that vaults the entire cornea and rests on the sclera) are actually the preferred option for advanced keratoconus — option D is wrong.

8
Accommodation is primarily achieved by:
UG
A Increase in axial length of the eye
B Corneal steepening
C Pupil constriction alone
D Increased curvature (thickening) of the crystalline lens
Correct: D

According to Helmholtz's theory, ciliary muscle contraction releases zonular tension, allowing the elastic lens capsule to mould the lens into a more spherical (thicker, more curved) shape. This increases the power of the lens and moves the focal point forward for near objects. The cornea and axial length do not change during accommodation. Miosis accompanies accommodation as part of the near triad but is not the mechanism of accommodation itself.

9
The conoid of Sturm is associated with which refractive condition?
UG
A Astigmatism
B Myopia
C Presbyopia
D Hypermetropia
Correct: A

In astigmatism, the two principal meridians have different curvatures, so there is no single focal point. Instead, rays converge to two focal lines (anterior and posterior focal lines) separated by the interval of Sturm. The three-dimensional shape of this light bundle between the two lines is the conoid of Sturm. At the circle of least confusion, halfway between the two focal lines, the blur is minimised — this is the best compromise focus.

10
A standard working distance of 67 cm in retinoscopy adds which lens artefact that must be subtracted?
UG
A +1.00 D
B +1.50 D
C +2.00 D
D +3.00 D
Correct: B

Working at 67 cm means the light returning from the patient's retina to the retinoscope acts as if coming from that distance, adding a spurious +1.50 D to the result (1/0.67 = approximately 1.5 D). At 1 metre the artefact is +1.00 D. You must subtract this from your neutralising lens power to get the actual refractive error. In practice, many examiners use a +1.5 D or +1.0 D "working distance" lens in the trial frame so the neutrality point they see is the true far point of the eye.

A 28-year-old man presents for LASIK evaluation. His corneal topography shows inferior steepening with a 2.4 D asymmetry between superior and inferior cornea. Pachymetry at the thinnest point is 494 μm. The most appropriate decision is:
PGClinical
A Proceed with LASIK as corneal thickness is adequate
B Proceed with PRK instead
C Decline surgery; refer for keratoconus workup
D Perform corneal cross-linking first, then LASIK in 6 months
Correct: C

Inferior steepening with significant I-S asymmetry (>1.4–1.8 D is suspicious; 2.4 D is clearly abnormal) is a forme fruste keratoconus pattern. This is an absolute contraindication to any corneal laser refractive surgery, regardless of current thickness. Removing corneal tissue from a weakened, ectatic cornea accelerates ectasia progression, which can be visually devastating. The patient needs a full keratoconus evaluation (Scheimpflug tomography including posterior surface) and counselling about options such as scleral contact lenses or corneal crosslinking.

2
The minimum residual stromal bed (RSB) thickness after LASIK flap creation and ablation should be at least:
PGHigh-Yield
A 250–300 μm
B 100–150 μm
C 400 μm
D 180–200 μm
Correct: A

A residual stromal bed of less than 250 μm is associated with a significantly increased risk of post-LASIK ectasia, where the weakened cornea progressively steepens. Most refractive surgeons set 300 μm as their safety threshold. The calculation is: RSB = Total pachymetry − flap thickness − ablation depth. For example: 520 μm cornea − 110 μm flap − 80 μm ablation = 330 μm RSB, which is acceptable.

3
Which advantage does SMILE have over LASIK in terms of corneal nerve preservation?
PGClinical
A SMILE uses a smaller laser spot size
B SMILE removes more anterior stroma, preserving deeper nerves
C SMILE creates a larger optical zone
D No flap is created, so anterior stromal nerves crossing the optical zone are largely preserved
Correct: D

In LASIK, the hinged flap severs a large proportion of sub-basal and anterior stromal nerve fibres circumferentially. These nerves take 12–24 months to fully regenerate, and during that time aqueous tear secretion (which is partly neurally mediated) is reduced — this is the mechanism of post-LASIK dry eye. SMILE creates only a small 2–4 mm arc incision at the periphery, preserving most of the central corneal nerve plexus and resulting in significantly less post-operative dry eye.

A patient wearing soft contact lenses presents with a painful eye, photophobia, and a ring-shaped corneal infiltrate. They admit to swimming in their contact lenses. The most likely organism is:
PGClinicalHigh-Yield

The combination of contact lens wear, swimming (exposure to freshwater containing Acanthamoeba cysts), ring infiltrate, and pain disproportionate to the slit-lamp findings is classic for Acanthamoeba keratitis. A phakic IOL is preferred over corneal refractive surgery for high myopia mainly because:
PG
A High ablation depths would leave an unacceptably thin residual stromal bed
B The crystalline lens is removed during phakic IOL implantation
C Phakic IOLs have better long-term safety data than LASIK
D Corneal refractive surgery cannot correct any myopia above 6 D
Correct: A

Correcting high myopia (above approximately −10 D) requires removing a large volume of corneal tissue. In most corneas, this would violate the minimum residual stromal bed limit, making the cornea mechanically weak and prone to ectasia. 6
Orthokeratology (Ortho-K) contact lenses primarily work by:
PG
A Permanently reshaping the corneal stroma
B Pharmacologically relaxing accommodation
C Temporarily flattening the central corneal epithelium during overnight wear
D Increasing the refractive index of the tear film
Correct: C

Orthokeratology lenses are reverse-geometry rigid lenses worn overnight. They apply controlled pressure to the central corneal epithelium, causing it to redistribute (thin centrally, thicken mid-peripherally). This flattens the central cornea and temporarily corrects myopia, allowing spectacle-free or lens-free daytime vision. The effect reverses within a day or two if lenses are not worn — the stroma itself is unchanged. There is growing evidence that Ortho-K also slows myopia progression in children by altering the peripheral defocus signal.

The Dk/t value of a contact lens material represents:
PG
A The water content divided by the lens thickness
B The lens diameter to curvature ratio
C The decentration tendency at different temperatures
D Oxygen transmissibility — the ability of oxygen to pass through the lens as worn
Correct: D

Dk is oxygen permeability (D = diffusion coefficient, k = solubility coefficient of oxygen in the material). Dividing by thickness t gives Dk/t, which is the oxygen transmissibility as actually worn — what the cornea experiences. Silicone hydrogel lenses have Dk/t values of 100–175 Barrer/cm, compared to conventional hydrogels at around 20–40. For extended (overnight) wear, the minimum recommended Dk/t is 87. In the Jackson cross-cylinder (JCC) test, the lens is used in two separate steps. Which of the following correctly describes step 1?
PG
A To determine the sphere power
B To refine the axis of the correcting cylinder
C To determine the near addition
D To balance the two eyes
Correct: B

The JCC is used in two steps: axis refinement first, then power refinement. In step 1, the axes of the JCC are placed at 45 degrees to the trial cylinder axis and flipped. Diffuse lamellar keratitis (DLK) after LASIK is also known as:
PG
A Sands of Sahara
B Dry eye syndrome
C Flap striae syndrome
D Induced astigmatism
Correct: A

DLK or "Sands of Sahara" is a non-infectious diffuse inflammation under the LASIK flap, appearing as granular white deposits in the interface. It typically presents within the first week. Which of the following best explains why spectacle lenses for high myopia cause image minification?
PG
A High minus lenses increase the refractive index of air around them
B The lenses reduce illumination reaching the retina
C Minus lenses are diverging and form a virtual, diminished image which subtends a smaller angle at the eye
D High minus lenses increase the effective axial length of the eye
Correct: C

A concave (minus) lens diverges light rays, forming a virtual, erect, diminished image closer to the lens than the object. This image subtends a smaller angle at the nodal point of the eye than the original object would have, so the retinal image is smaller. Higher minus power means greater minification. This is why high myopes switching from thick spectacle lenses to contact lenses (which sit at the cornea rather than 12 mm in front) experience an apparent increase in image size — contact lenses cause much less minification for the same refractive correction.

Three structured long-answer questions for MBBS and DNB examinations. These test your ability to organise and explain concepts in depth.
LA 1 Classify errors of refraction. Describe the clinical features, aetiology, and optical correction of myopia. Show answer outline

Open with the classification: refractive errors divide into spherical (myopia, hypermetropia, presbyopia) and astigmatic (regular and irregular). Myopia is the most common.

Aetiology of myopia is either axial (the most common — increased anteroposterior diameter, every extra millimetre adding approximately 3 D) or curvatural (steeper cornea or lens curvature, rarer). Index myopia refers to a transient increase in the refractive index of the lens, often seen in early nuclear cataract or uncontrolled diabetes.

Clinical features: blurred distance vision, near vision preserved. Far point lies in front of the retina and can be calculated. Fundus findings in high myopia: tessellated (tigroid) fundus, posterior staphyloma, lacquer cracks in Bruch's membrane, Fuchs' spot (subretinal haemorrhage at the macula), lattice degeneration, and increased risk of retinal detachment.

Correction: spectacles (concave/diverging/minus lenses), contact lenses (RGP or soft), or refractive surgery (LASIK/PRK/SMILE for low to moderate myopia; phakic IOL or refractive lens exchange for high myopia). Emphasise myopia control strategies in children: atropine 0.01%, orthokeratology, and peripheral defocus spectacle lenses.

LA 2 Compare LASIK, PRK, and SMILE. Discuss the indications, contraindications, and complications of corneal refractive surgery. Show answer outline

Define the shared principle: all three use excimer or femtosecond laser to reshape the anterior corneal surface and change its refracting power. Compare the three procedures in a structured way: mechanism of tissue removal, flap creation (LASIK yes, PRK no, SMILE no flap but small incision), recovery time, dry eye risk, biomechanical integrity, and range of correction.

Indications: stable refraction for at least two years, adequate corneal thickness (minimum RSB of 250–300 μm after ablation), absence of corneal disease, realistic patient expectations. Age typically above 21.

Contraindications: keratoconus or forme fruste (topographic screening mandatory), thin cornea, progressive myopia, pregnancy, severe dry eye, collagen vascular disease. Discuss why keratoconus is an absolute contraindication.

Complications of LASIK: flap-related (displacement, striae, DLK "Sands of Sahara", epithelial ingrowth), refractive (under/overcorrection, regression), and post-LASIK ectasia. PRK complications: subepithelial haze (reduced with MMC), delayed re-epithelialisation. SMILE: sub-optimal visual recovery early on, incomplete lenticule extraction.

LA 3 Describe the types of contact lenses, their fitting principles, and the complications of contact lens wear. Show answer outline

Classify contact lenses by material (soft hydrogel, silicone hydrogel, rigid gas-permeable), wearing schedule (daily disposable, two-weekly, monthly, extended wear), and design (spherical, toric for astigmatism, multifocal for presbyopia, scleral for irregular corneas).

Fitting principles: base curve must match (closely approximate) the central corneal curvature from keratometry or topography. Too flat — lens moves excessively, causes discomfort and 3 and 9 o'clock staining. Too steep — lens immobile, tear exchange impaired, hypoxia develops centrally. Scleral lens fitting requires impression moulding or scleral topography; the lens vaults the entire cornea and rests on the sclera.

Complications: microbial keratitis (Pseudomonas for extended wear, Acanthamoeba for freshwater exposure), giant papillary conjunctivitis (type IV hypersensitivity to lens deposits), contact lens peripheral ulcer (CLPU, sterile), corneal neovascularisation from hypoxia, superior epithelial arcuate lesion (SEAL), dry eye, and lens deposits (lipid, protein, calcium).

Five previous year questions from MBBS university and NEET PG examinations. These reflect the actual phrasing and depth expected.
Define and classify refractive errors. Describe the clinical features and management of myopia. [MBBS University, commonly repeated]

Begin with definitions — emmetropia vs ametropia. Classify refractive errors into spherical (myopia, hypermetropia, presbyopia) and astigmatic. Then focus on myopia: aetiology (axial, curvatural, index), clinical features (blurred distance vision, far point calculation, fundus changes in high myopia), correction (spectacles, contact lenses, refractive surgery), and myopia control in children. Examiners look for the axial length relationship (1 mm = 3 D), the complications of high myopia, and specific surgical thresholds.

What is astigmatism? How is it classified and corrected? [MBBS University]

Define astigmatism: two principal meridians with different curvatures, so no single focal point, creating a conoid of Sturm. Classify as regular (two principal meridians at 90 degrees to each other) vs irregular (any other arrangement, not correctable with spectacle cylinders). Within regular: with-the-rule (vertical meridian steeper, corrected with minus cylinder at 180 degrees), against-the-rule (horizontal steeper, minus cylinder at 90 degrees), and oblique. Further classify as simple, compound, and mixed astigmatism based on whether one or both focal lines are on the retina. Correction: cylindrical lenses (spectacles or toric contact lenses) for regular; RGP or scleral lenses for irregular.

Write a short note on presbyopia — mechanism, symptoms, and correction. [MBBS University]

Presbyopia is the age-related decline in accommodation due to progressive hardening (sclerosis) of the crystalline lens, reducing its ability to change shape when zonular tension is released by ciliary muscle contraction. Symptoms typically appear after age 40: difficulty reading fine print, need to hold reading material further away, eyestrain and headache with prolonged near work. The near point recedes beyond comfortable working distance (approximately 25–33 cm). Correction: plus (convex) reading addition lenses, the power of which increases with age from about +1.00 D at age 45 to +3.00 D by age 60. Multifocal spectacle lenses (bifocal or progressive) or multifocal contact lenses allow distance and near correction in one lens.

A patient complains that their new spectacles give clear vision at distance but everything is slightly smaller than expected. Explain why and how this could be minimised. [NEET PG applied]

This is spectacle magnification (actually minification for myopia). A high minus spectacle lens diverges light and forms a virtual, diminished retinal image — image size is proportional to spectacle magnification, which for minus lenses is less than 1. The higher the minus power and the further the lens from the eye (vertex distance), the greater the minification. It can be minimised by: (1) wearing contact lenses instead of spectacles — contact lenses sit at the cornea (approximately 0 mm vertex distance vs 12–14 mm for spectacles), dramatically reducing the effective magnification difference; (2) using high-index lens materials that allow thinner, flatter lens forms; (3) choosing smaller frames to reduce lens edge thickness and weight.

What are the essential criteria for selecting a patient for LASIK surgery? What are the absolute contraindications? [DNB / NEET PG]

Inclusion criteria: age ≥21 (refraction stable for 2 years), corneal thickness adequate (total pachymetry ≥480 μm, residual stromal bed after ablation ≥250–300 μm), normal corneal topography/tomography (no ectasia pattern), corrected distance visual acuity sufficient to make the procedure worthwhile, no significant dry eye, and realistic expectations. Refractive range: approximately −1.00 D to −10.00 D of myopia, up to +4.00 D hypermetropia, astigmatism up to about 5 D.

Absolute contraindications: keratoconus or forme fruste keratoconus (topographic inferior steepening, abnormal posterior elevation, low pachymetry), thin corneas below threshold, progressive myopia (refraction changing >0.5 D/year), pregnancy and breastfeeding, active ocular surface disease (severe dry eye, recurrent erosion syndrome, active infection), autoimmune or collagen vascular disease (impairs epithelial healing). Relative contraindications include occupations at high risk for ocular trauma (flap displacement risk) and some systemic medications.
