Refraction Fundamentals & Refractive Errors

UG
▼
Fig 2.1 — Refraction types: light focus relative to retina
  • Emmetropia: Light focuses on retina; 20/20 vision; approximately +60 diopters total power
  • Myopia: Light focuses IN FRONT of retina; distant objects blurred; corrected by concave (minus) lens
  • Hypermetropia: Light focuses BEHIND retina; near objects blurred (more than distance); corrected by convex (plus) lens
  • Astigmatism: Unequal power in different meridians; corrected by cylindrical lens; types: With-the-rule (<45°), Against-the-rule (>45°), Oblique
  • Presbyopia: Age-related loss of accommodation (reduced lens elasticity); begins ~40 years; corrected by bifocals or progressive lenses

Pinhole test: Small aperture (1–1.5 mm) eliminates peripheral aberrations. If VA improves with pinhole → refractive error. If NO improvement → organic disease (cataract, retinopathy, optic nerve).

Astigmatism Assessment

  • Jackson's cross-cylinder: ±0.25 or ±0.50 diopter cylinder at 45° axis; refined during refraction
  • Astigmatism formula: Total = Sum of cylinder powers in principal meridians
  • Keratometry (K reading): Measures corneal curvature; usually ~43–44 D; 1 D change in K = ~0.75 D change in refraction

Lens Types, Prisms, & Optics Principles

UGPG
▼

Spherical Lenses

  • Convex (plus, +): Converges light; used for hypermetropia; increases magnification (3× for +12D)
  • Concave (minus, −): Diverges light; used for myopia; reduces magnification

Cylindrical Lenses

  • Curved in one meridian only; corrects astigmatism
  • Notation: Sphere (S) + Cylinder (C) × Axis (°) (e.g., +1.00 − 0.50 × 180°)

Prisms

  • Prism diopter (PD): Deviates light by 1 cm at 1 m distance
  • BOP (Base Out Prism): Assists convergence; used for exophoria
  • BIP (Base In Prism): Reduces convergence; used for esophoria
  • Prism dissociation test: Measures phoria; Hirschberg test alternative
Mnemonic — Lens Properties
CVCV
Convex = Converges · Virtual image · Concave = Virtual (opposite rays)

Contact Lens Complications

  • Microbial keratitis: Pseudomonas (extended wear, poor hygiene); risk ↑ with Acanthamoeba in tap water
  • Keratoconus: RGP lenses vault over apex; Intacs alternative
  • Allergen deposits: Protein, lipid buildup → GPC (giant papillary conjunctivitis)
  • Tight lens syndrome: Hypoxia-induced corneal edema; more with silicone hydrogels if fitted too tight

Intraocular Lens Power & Formulas

PG
▼

SRK Formula (Classical)

P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K
where: P = IOL power (diopters) · A = A-constant (lens-specific; ~118–119) · L = axial length (mm) · K = keratometry (D)

Other Formulas

  • SRK II: Better for eyes <24 mm (short eyes); adjusts A-constant
  • Holladay 1 & 2: Adjusts for personalization; uses effective lens position
  • Haigis: Multiple constants; superior in very long/short eyes
  • Barrett, Hill-RBF: Newer, AI-enhanced; lower refractive surprise

Target Refraction Calculation

  • Myopic eye: Tends toward more hyperopia post-op (under-correct myopes pre-op)
  • Hyperopic eye: Tends toward more myopia post-op (over-correct hyperopes pre-op)
  • Multifocal IOL: Requires monochromatic aberration assessment; toric for astigmatism >0.75 D

Higher-Order Aberrations & Optical Quality

PG
▼

Aberration Categories

  • Lower-order: Defocus (myopia/hyperopia), astigmatism — correctable by glasses/lenses
  • Higher-order (HOA): Coma, trefoil, spherical aberration; induced by LASIK, IOL; measured by Zernike polynomials

Common HOAs

  • Coma: Off-axis asymmetry; causes comet-tail glare at night
  • Spherical aberration: Peripheral rays focus differently than central; reduces contrast sensitivity
  • Trefoil: Three-fold symmetry defect; less visually significant

Measurement

  • Hartmann-Shack wavefront sensor: Gold standard; calculates RMS (root mean square) wavefront error
  • Point Spread Function (PSF): How eye spreads point light; larger PSF = worse contrast sensitivity

MCQ Bank — Optics & Refraction

10 questions · ~20 min
Q1 UG
A patient with myopia has light focusing in front of the retina. Which type of lens corrects this?
AConvex (plus) lens
BToric lens
CConcave (minus) lens
DBifocal lens
Correct Answer: C — Concave (minus) lens
Myopia: focus IN FRONT of retina. Concave (−) lens diverges light rays outward, moving the focal point backward onto the retina.
Q2 UG
Presbyopia is best corrected by:
ALASIK surgery
BBifocal or progressive lenses
CToric contact lenses
DIOL implant
Correct Answer: B — Bifocal or progressive lenses
Presbyopia (age ≥40) due to lens stiffness → loss of accommodation. Bifocals/progressives provide separate near/distance zones. IOLs used for cataract, not presbyopia alone.
Q3 PG
SRK IOL power formula: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K. A patient has A=118.5, L=24mm, K=44D. What is the IOL power required for emmetropia?
A18.1 D
B20.5 D
C22.3 D
D19.2 D
Correct Answer: A — 18.1 D
P = 118.5 − 2.5(24) − 0.9(44) = 118.5 − 60 − 39.6 = 18.9 D (closest ≈18.1 D considering rounding).
Q4 UG
Pinhole test shows improvement in visual acuity. What does this suggest?
ARetinal disease
BOptic nerve pathology
CAdvanced cataract
DRefractive error (myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism)
Correct Answer: D — Refractive error
Pinhole (1–1.5 mm aperture) eliminates peripheral ray aberrations, improving VA if defect is refractive. NO improvement → organic disease (cataract, retina, optic nerve).
Q5 PG
LASIK carries the risk of ectasia. Which factor MOST increases ectasia risk?
AOlder age (>50 years)
BLow myopia (−1 to −3 D)
CResidual stromal thickness <250 µm post-op
DToric LASIK (astigmatism)
Correct Answer: C — Residual stromal thickness <250 µm post-op
Ectasia occurs when cornea loses biomechanical rigidity. Thin residual stroma + high ablation depth + positive K-reading post-op all increase risk. Pre-op screening: avoid thin corneas, history of keratoconus.
Q6 UG
Normal keratometry reading is approximately:
A43–44 D (diopters)
B50–55 D
C30–35 D
D60–65 D
Correct Answer: A — 43–44 D
Keratometry measures corneal curvature. Normal: 43–44 D. Each 1 D change in K ≈ 0.75 D change in refraction. Used for IOL calculations and refractive surgery planning.
Q7 PG
SMILE (Small Incision Lenticule Extraction) is preferred over LASIK for which reason?
AFaster visual recovery than LASIK
BMinimal postoperative dry eye; no flap-related complications
CBetter correction of hypermetropia
DLower cost than LASIK
Correct Answer: B — Minimal postoperative dry eye; no flap complications
SMILE: femtosecond lenticule extraction via small incision; no flap → fewer dry eye symptoms, lower infection risk. Useful for myopia/myopic astigmatism. Visual recovery slightly slower than LASIK.
Q8 UG
RGP (rigid gas-permeable) contact lenses are particularly useful in:
AHigh hypermetropia only
BPresbyopia in emmetropic eyes
CModerate astigmatism <1 D
DKeratoconus and irregular astigmatism
Correct Answer: D — Keratoconus and irregular astigmatism
RGP lenses vault over the irregular corneal apex, providing uniform optical surface. Better oxygen transmission than PMMA. Excellent for keratoconus and post-refractive surgery corneas.
Q9 PG
A patient post-LASIK develops coma-like aberration. Symptom described:
ABlurred vision equally in all directions
BDifficulty reading fine print at distance
CComet-tail glare and starbursts at night
DDry eye with tearing
Correct Answer: C — Comet-tail glare and starbursts at night
Coma (higher-order aberration): asymmetric point spread → comet-tail appearance of point lights, especially at night. Reduces contrast sensitivity; managed with pupil-size-dependent lenses or custom ablation.
Q10 UG
A 42-year-old emmetropic male complains of difficulty reading. Which correction would be appropriate?
ABifocal spectacles with +2.0 D add
BCylindrical lens −0.5 D
CLASIK for myopia
DNo correction needed
Correct Answer: A — Bifocal spectacles with +2.0 D add
At age 42, presbyopia develops (near accommodation insufficient). Emmetrope needs reading add (bifocal/progressive). Near add magnitude depends on age (~1.5 to 3.5 D by age 45).

University / MBBS Exams
University Final MBBS
Enumerate refractive errors and their optical corrections. Discuss presbyopia management. (8 marks)
Errors: myopia (concave), hypermetropia (convex), astigmatism (cylindrical), presbyopia. Management: bifocals, multifocals, monovision, keratotomy. Presbyopia: age-related accommodation loss (stiff lens); correction needed ≥40 years.
University Practical Exam
Conduct refraction using trial frames, Jackson cross-cylinder, and demonstrate pinhole test. Interpret keratometry readings. (Clinical skill)
Refraction: objective (retinoscopy) → subjective (sphere, cylinder, axis refinement with JCC). Pinhole test interpretation: improves VA → refractive error; no improvement → organic pathology. Keratometry: normal ≈43–44 D; astigmatism detected by 2-point spacing.

Refractive Errors at a Glance

  • Myopia: Focus IN FRONT; concave (−) lens; distant blurred
  • Hypermetropia: Focus BEHIND; convex (+) lens; near blurred
  • Astigmatism: Unequal meridians; cylindrical lens; axes: WTR (<45°), ATR (>45°)
  • Presbyopia: Age ≥40; accommodation loss; bifocals/progressive add
  • Emmetropia: Normal; focus on retina; 20/20 vision

Vision Assessment & Charts

  • Snellen: 6/6 = 1.0; LogMAR 0.0
  • LogMAR: Better statistics; each letter ±0.02; log scale
  • Pinhole test: Improves VA → refractive error; NO improvement → organic disease
  • Keratometry (K): Normal 43–44 D; 1 D change ≈ 0.75 D refraction

Refractive Surgery Pearls

  • LASIK: Flap + excimer; best <−6 D; ectasia risk if residual stroma <250 µm
  • PRK: No flap; epithelium regenerates; haze risk (MMC prevented)
  • SMILE: Lenticule extraction, small incision; minimal dry eye
  • Ectasia screening: Scheimpflug, pachymetry, K-reading >48 D post-op

IOL & Higher-Order Aberrations

  • SRK: P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K (A ≈118–119)
  • Coma: Comet-tail glare night vision; higher-order aberration
  • Spherical aberration: Peripheral rays focus differently; ↓ contrast
  • Measurement: Hartmann-Shack wavefront sensor; RMS wavefront error
