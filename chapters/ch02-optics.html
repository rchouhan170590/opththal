Optics & Refraction

From the physics of lenses and the refracting surfaces of the eye to the clinical management of myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism, and presbyopia — plus contact lenses and refractive surgery.

Errors of Refraction

In emmetropia, parallel rays from infinity focus exactly on the retina with the eye at rest. Any deviation from this is a refractive error.

  • Myopia: Far point lies in front of the retina. Image focused before it reaches the retina. Corrected with a concave (diverging, minus) lens.
  • Hypermetropia: Far point lies behind the retina. Eye needs to accommodate even for distant objects. Corrected with a convex (converging, plus) lens.
  • Astigmatism: Two principal meridians have different curvatures, so no single focal point exists — you get a conoid of Sturm instead. Corrected with cylindrical lenses.
  • Presbyopia: Loss of accommodative amplitude after the age of 40. The near point recedes beyond comfortable reading distance. Managed with a reading addition (convex add).

Accommodation

Accommodation is the eye's ability to increase its refractive power in order to focus on near objects. The mechanism follows Helmholtz's theory.

  • Ciliary muscle contracts, reducing the tension on the zonular fibres.
  • With zonules relaxed, the elastic lens capsule moulds the lens into a more convex shape.
  • A more convex lens has higher dioptric power — near objects come into focus.
  • Amplitude of accommodation is greatest in childhood (around 14D) and declines steadily with age. By 40 years, enough amplitude has been lost to make near work symptomatic — this is presbyopia.
  • The near point is the closest distance at which the eye can maintain clear focus. As amplitude falls, the near point moves farther away.

Myopia — Classification

Most myopia is axial — the eyeball is simply too long. The normal axial length is around 23.5 mm, and every additional millimetre adds roughly 3 dioptres of myopia.

  • Axial myopia: Most common. Axial length exceeds 23.5 mm.
  • Curvature myopia: Steeper than normal corneal or lens curvature.
  • Index myopia: Increased refractive index of the lens (seen in early nuclear cataract — the “second sight” phenomenon).
  • Positional myopia: Anterior displacement of the lens.

By degree: low (<3D), moderate (3–6D), high (>6D). Pathological myopia exceeds 6D and is accompanied by degenerative changes: posterior staphyloma, lattice degeneration, Fuchs’ spot (a pigmented scar from CNV at the macula), lacquer cracks in Bruch’s membrane, and peripapillary atrophy.

Refractive Surgery Options

  • LASIK: A hinged corneal flap (110–130 μm) is created, excimer laser ablates the underlying stroma, flap is repositioned. Fastest visual recovery. Best for myopia up to −10D.
  • PRK (photorefractive keratectomy): Surface ablation after epithelium removal. No flap. Slower healing, risk of subepithelial haze. Better for thin corneas or contact sports.
  • SMILE: Femtosecond laser cuts a lenticule within the stroma; it is removed through a small 2–4 mm arc incision. No flap at all. Best biomechanical stability.
  • Phakic IOL / ICL: A Visian ICL (implantable collamer lens) is placed behind the iris in front of the crystalline lens. Preferred for high myopia (>10D) where corneal ablation would be unsafe.

IOL Power Calculation

Before cataract surgery, you need two measurements: the corneal curvature (by keratometry) and the axial length (by A-scan ultrasonography or optical biometry with IOLMaster). These feed into a formula along with an IOL-specific A-constant.

  • SRK-T formula: The most widely used third-generation formula. Works well for eyes of average length.
  • Haigis formula: Preferred for very short or very long eyes, as it uses three constants and accounts for anterior chamber depth.
  • Target refraction: Aim for emmetropia in most patients. A slight myopic target (−0.5D) is acceptable and helps near vision without glasses post-operatively.

Physical Optics — Light, Lenses, and the Reduced Eye

UGPG
▼

The Behaviour of Light

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform medium, but bends when it crosses the interface between two media of different optical densities — a process called refraction. The optical density of a medium is described by its refractive index (n), defined as the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to its speed in that medium. Water has a refractive index of 1.33, glass around 1.5, and the cornea approximately 1.376. The higher the refractive index, the more the light bends when it enters that medium.

Snell’s Law

Snell’s law states that n1 sinθ1 = n2 sinθ2, where θ is measured from the normal (perpendicular) to the interface. This simple relationship governs everything about how lenses and the eye focus light. When light moves from a less dense to a more dense medium (say, air into cornea), it bends toward the normal — converging. Moving the other way, it bends away — diverging.

Vergence and Lens Power

Vergence describes the degree of convergence or divergence of a bundle of rays and is measured in dioptres (D). Rays from a point at infinity are parallel and have zero vergence. Rays from a point 1 metre away have a vergence of −1D (diverging). A converging lens adds positive vergence. The power of a thin lens is simply P = 1/f, where f is the focal length in metres. So a lens that focuses parallel light at 25 cm has a power of +4D.

The Reduced Eye

The human eye has multiple refracting surfaces — the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, the anterior and posterior lens surfaces — each with slightly different radii of curvature and refractive indices. For most calculations, we simplify this to Listing’s reduced eye: a single refracting surface with a refractive index of 1.336, located 5.6 mm behind the anterior cornea (the nodal point approximation). The total power of the eye in this model is about 60D — the cornea contributing approximately 43D and the lens contributing the remaining 17D. The cornea is the dominant refracting structure. This is why corneal disease and corneal surgery have such a large effect on refraction.

Cardinal Points

Every optical system has six cardinal points: two principal planes (H and H′), two nodal points (N and N′), and two focal points (F and F′). For clinical purposes, the nodal point is the most useful — it is the point through which rays pass without changing their angle of incidence. In the human eye the anterior nodal point is about 7.2 mm behind the corneal apex and the posterior nodal point 7.5 mm behind. Angular subtense calculations for visual acuity use the nodal point as their reference.

Total power of the eye: Cornea contributes ~43D and the crystalline lens ~17D at rest, giving a total of ~60D. During maximum accommodation, the lens adds another 14D (in a young person), taking the total to around 74D.

Emmetropia and Ametropia

UGPG
▼

Defining Emmetropia

An emmetropic eye is one in which parallel rays from a distant object are brought to focus exactly on the retina when the eye is at rest — meaning the ciliary muscle is fully relaxed and the lens is in its flattest state. Emmetropia is the ideal, but it is not the most common state. Most people are mildly hypermetropic at birth and become progressively more emmetropic through childhood (emmetropisation).

Ametropia and Its Types

Ametropia simply means a refractive error exists. The far point — the point conjugate with the retina when the eye is relaxed — is displaced from optical infinity. In myopia the far point is at a finite distance in front of the eye. In hypermetropia the far point is virtual, lying behind the eye. In astigmatism there is no single far point; instead there are two focal lines at different distances (Sturm’s conoid).

The Spectacle Correction and Vertex Distance

The correcting lens is placed at the spectacle plane, conventionally 12 mm in front of the cornea (the vertex distance). Think of it this way: the correcting lens must create a virtual image of infinity at the patient’s far point. For a myope whose far point is at 50 cm, a concave lens of −2D placed at the spectacle plane will diverge parallel rays so they appear to come from 50 cm — exactly where the far point sits.

Vertex distance matters enormously in high prescriptions. A −10D lens at 12 mm from the cornea is not equivalent to a −10D lens held against the cornea. The effective power changes with distance. The formula is F′ = F / (1 − d·F), where d is the vertex distance in metres. For prescriptions above ±4D, always adjust for vertex distance when converting from trial frame to spectacle order.

Exam point: Vertex distance adjustment is a favourite numerical question. If a patient’s trial frame correction is −12D at 12 mm from the cornea, the equivalent contact lens power (at zero vertex distance) will be less minus — approximately −10.71D. Moving a lens closer to the eye reduces the effective minus power.

Myopia — From Classification to Pathological Changes

UGPGHigh-Yield
▼

Classification by Cause

The most common cause is axial elongation. An axial length greater than 23.5 mm in an adult means the image plane falls in front of the retina. Curvature myopia occurs when the anterior corneal surface is steeper than normal — you see this in keratoconus. Index myopia develops when the refractive index of the crystalline lens increases, something that happens in early nuclear sclerosis. This is the so-called “second sight” of incipient cataract, where patients who previously needed reading glasses suddenly find they can read without them. Positional myopia results from anterior displacement of the lens, as can occur after trauma.

Classification by Degree and Clinical Features

Low myopia is under 3D, moderate is 3–6D, and high myopia exceeds 6D. What you will see clinically in a straightforward myope: blurred distance vision, clear near vision, and often a tendency to hold objects very close. The eye itself appears larger because of the increased axial length. The anterior chamber is deeper. There may be a slight divergent tendency (exophoria or even esotropia in very young children, though myopia more classically associates with divergent squint than convergent).

Pathological Myopia

When myopia exceeds 6D with evidence of progressive axial elongation and degenerative fundal changes, it becomes pathological myopia — a distinct disease entity, not merely a strong glasses prescription. The sclera stretches, the choroid thins, and Bruch’s membrane develops cracks (lacquer cracks). The fundal changes include:

  • Posterior staphyloma — an outpouching of the posterior pole where the thinned sclera cannot maintain its shape
  • Peripapillary atrophy (a crescent of depigmentation around the optic disc)
  • Lattice degeneration with or without retinal breaks — important because it predisposes to retinal detachment
  • Choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) breaking through lacquer cracks, which leaves a pigmented scar at the macula called Fuchs’ spot
  • Vitreous degeneration and posterior vitreous detachment at an earlier age than in emmetropes

The term Foster-Fuchs’ spot refers specifically to the end-stage pigmented scar of regressed CNV at the macula. Do not confuse it with the active CNV lesion itself.

Management

Optical correction with concave lenses remains the standard. Contact lenses are an alternative and, by sitting closer to the cornea, actually require slightly less minus power than spectacles (vertex distance effect). Refractive surgery is an option once the refraction has been stable for at least two years. For pathological myopia, the concern is retinal complications — these patients need annual fundus examination and should be warned about symptoms of retinal detachment.

Myopia control has become an active area. Low-dose atropine 0.01% eye drops slow axial elongation in children with minimal side effects. Orthokeratology (wearing rigid moulding lenses overnight) and specially designed soft multifocal lenses also show meaningful control effects.

Hypermetropia — The Eye That Works Too Hard

UGPG
▼

Classification

Like myopia, hypermetropia can be axial (short eye), curvature-based (flatter cornea), index-related, or positional. Axial hypermetropia is most common. A hypermetropic eye is one where the power is insufficient for the length — parallel rays would focus behind the retina if nothing intervened.

Latent, Manifest, and Total Hypermetropia

This distinction is one of the most tested areas in refraction. The young hypermetrope has abundant accommodation and can compensate for some or all of the hypermetropia by continuously accommodating. The part that is masked this way is called latent hypermetropia. The part that remains after maximum voluntary effort (and is detectable with non-cycloplegic refraction) is manifest hypermetropia. Total hypermetropia is the full amount, only revealed by cycloplegic refraction — which is why you should never prescribe for hypermetropia in children without first doing cycloplegic refraction with atropine or cyclopentolate.

Clinical Presentation

A young person with mild hypermetropia may have no symptoms at all because they accommodate without difficulty. What you will see is asthenopia — eye strain, headaches behind the eyes, difficulty sustaining near work. As accommodation falters in the late thirties and forties, distance blur emerges as well. The classic association is with convergent squint (esotropia) in children, because the excessive accommodative effort to clear vision also drives excess convergence through the accommodative convergence reflex.

Amblyopia Risk

Uncorrected hypermetropia in childhood carries a real risk of amblyopia, particularly if it is asymmetric (anisometropia). The blurred retinal image in the more hypermetropic eye fails to provide the visual stimulation needed for normal cortical development. Full cycloplegic correction should be prescribed early, even if the child appears asymptomatic, specifically to protect visual development.

Clinical rule: Always use cycloplegic refraction (atropine 1% for three days, or cyclopentolate 1% for children older than 1 year) before prescribing for hypermetropia in any child under 12. Missing latent hypermetropia means under-correcting, which perpetuates amblyopia risk.

Astigmatism — Two Focal Lines, One Blurred Image

UGPG
▼

The Optics of Astigmatism

In astigmatism, the two principal meridians (usually roughly 90 degrees apart) have different refractive powers. Light entering through the steeper meridian comes to focus closer than light through the flatter meridian. The result is not a point focus but a three-dimensional shape called Sturm’s conoid — two focal lines separated by a circle of least confusion (the interval of Sturm). The circle of least confusion is the point of best compromise focus.

Regular Astigmatism

Regular astigmatism has perpendicular principal meridians and can be corrected with cylindrical lenses. It is further classified by the orientation of the steeper meridian:

  • With-the-rule (WTR): The vertical meridian (around 90 degrees) is steeper. Common in children and young adults. The eye has more power vertically, which happens to be useful because most scenes have more horizontal detail — the vertical power helps resolve horizontal edges.
  • Against-the-rule (ATR): The horizontal meridian is steeper. Becomes more prevalent with age as the cornea flattens vertically. A minus cylinder at axis 180 degrees corrects it.
  • Oblique: Principal meridians lie somewhere between 30–60 degrees and 120–150 degrees.

Irregular Astigmatism

Irregular astigmatism occurs when the principal meridians are not perpendicular, or when the curvature varies across the optical zone in an unpredictable way. The classical cause is keratoconus — a progressive ectasia where the corneal stroma thins and the apex protrudes. Corneal scarring after trauma or infection also produces irregular astigmatism. Spectacles and soft contact lenses cannot correct irregular astigmatism adequately because they present a smooth curved surface to the eye but cannot compensate for local irregularity. Rigid gas-permeable contact lenses are the key: they create a tear lens between their smooth back surface and the irregular cornea, neutralising the irregular optics of the cornea itself.

Detection and Correction

The stenopeic slit is a narrow aperture that, when rotated in front of the eye, allows the patient to identify the axis of clear vision — useful for detecting and roughly quantifying astigmatism. Keratometry measures the curvature of the central cornea directly. The Jackson cross-cylinder is the workhorse of refining the axis and power of the cylinder during subjective refraction.

Presbyopia and Accommodation

UGPG
▼

The Mechanism of Accommodation

Helmholtz’s theory remains the accepted mechanism. At rest, the zonular fibres are under tension and flatten the lens. When the ciliary muscle contracts (a ring of smooth muscle, so contraction reduces its internal diameter), the zonules go slack, and the inherently elastic lens capsule allows the lens to assume a more spherical shape. This increases the central radius of curvature and therefore the dioptric power. The lens can change its power by up to 14D in a young child.

Schachar’s competing theory proposes that equatorial zonules relax while anterior and posterior zonules increase tension, steepening the central lens. This theory is less widely accepted but underlies the rationale for some experimental surgical presbyopia treatments that attempt to alter zonular geometry.

Why Presbyopia Happens

The lens grows throughout life by laying down new fibres from the equatorial epithelium. The older fibres are compressed toward the nucleus, which gradually loses its elasticity. By the early forties the lens cannot deform sufficiently even when the ciliary muscle exerts full contractile force. The amplitude of accommodation falls by roughly 0.25D per year after age 8, and by the time a patient reaches 40–45 years, the remaining amplitude (around 4D) is no longer enough to sustain comfortable near work.

Reading Additions

The principle of the reading addition is to supply the dioptric power the lens can no longer generate itself. The addition is always convex (plus). A simple guide: +1.0D at 40 years, progressing to +2.5D at 55 years and reaching a plateau of around +3.0D by 60–65 years. Beyond +3D there is little practical benefit because the depth of focus of the eye provides the rest.

Optical Solutions

Bifocal lenses have a distance correction in the upper portion and a reading segment (the add) in the lower portion. The most common segments are the D-segment (flat-top) and round segment. Progressive addition lenses (PALs) avoid the visible dividing line and provide a continuous corridor of power from distance at the top to near at the bottom, with the intermediate zone in between. They take more adaptation but are cosmetically preferred. Reading glasses are single-vision plus lenses — suitable when the patient needs no distance correction or is happy to remove glasses for distance.

Contact Lenses — Types, Fitting, and Complications

UGPG
▼

Types of Contact Lenses

Soft contact lenses made from hydrogel (HEMA) or silicone hydrogel are the most commonly prescribed. They drape over the cornea and move minimally with blinking. Silicone hydrogel materials transmit significantly more oxygen than conventional hydrogels, reducing hypoxia-related complications. Daily disposable lenses carry the lowest infection risk. Monthly replacement lenses are economical but require rigorous cleaning and storage discipline.

Rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses sit smaller on the cornea (9–9.5 mm diameter vs 13.5–14.5 mm for soft lenses) and move considerably with each blink. Because they maintain their shape regardless of corneal contour, they create a tear lens that neutralises irregular astigmatism. They are the first-line contact lens for keratoconus and post-surgical corneal irregularity. Scleral lenses, which vault entirely over the cornea and rest on the bulbar conjunctiva, have extended this principle to severe irregularity where RGPs are intolerable.

Fitting Principles

The base-back optic zone radius (BOZR) is chosen in relation to the flat K reading from keratometry. For soft lenses, you typically aim for a slightly flatter fit than the flat K. For RGPs, you trial-fit and observe the fluorescein pattern — an ideal fit shows even fluorescein pooling under the lens with slight pooling at the periphery. The total diameter should allow the lens to centre well and move 1–2 mm with each blink.

Complications

The list of contact lens complications is long, but the ones you must know cold are:

  • Giant papillary conjunctivitis (GPC): A hypersensitivity response to lens deposits on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. Presents with itching, mucous discharge, and large papillae (>0.3 mm). Treat by changing or discontinuing lenses and adding mast cell stabilisers.
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis: The organism of extended soft lens wear. Produces a rapidly progressive, aggressive corneal ulcer with a ring infiltrate. Requires intensive topical fluoroquinolones immediately.
  • Acanthamoeba keratitis: Classically in patients who rinse lenses with tap water, swim while wearing lenses, or have poor hygiene. The hallmark is a ring infiltrate and pain that is disproportionately severe compared to the clinical signs. Early disease may look like a dendritic keratitis, leading to misdiagnosis and incorrect antiviral treatment. Treatment is prolonged PHMB and chlorhexidine drops.
  • Contact lens peripheral ulcer (CLPU): A sterile peripheral ulcer from bacterial toxin response, not a true infection. Small, well-defined, usually heals with a short course of topical steroids plus antibiotic cover.
  • Hypoxia and neovascularisation: Chronic oxygen deprivation drives superficial corneal vessels in from the limbus (pannus formation). Using higher-Dk silicone hydrogel materials prevents this.

Refractive Surgery — LASIK, PRK, SMILE, and Phakic IOL

PGHigh-Yield
▼

The Principle

All corneal refractive procedures work by reshaping the anterior corneal surface to change its refractive power. The excimer laser (193 nm argon fluoride) ablates corneal tissue with extraordinary precision — removing approximately 0.25 μm of tissue per pulse without generating heat in adjacent tissue. The amount of tissue removed and its distribution across the optical zone determines the refractive change achieved.

LASIK

Laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis has been the dominant refractive procedure for three decades. A microkeratome or femtosecond laser creates a hinged flap of 110–130 μm thickness. The flap is lifted, the excimer laser ablates the exposed stroma, and the flap is repositioned. Visual recovery is rapid — most patients see well the following day. The 10% rule states that no more than 10% of the corneal thickness should be removed by ablation, and the residual stromal bed (below the flap) should be at least 250–300 μm. This limits LASIK to around −10D for an average cornea. The main complications are flap-related (displacement, striae, diffuse lamellar keratitis — “Sands of Sahara”) and regression of effect.

PRK (Photorefractive Keratectomy)

PRK removes the epithelium first (manually or with dilute alcohol) and then ablates the anterior stroma directly. There is no flap, which eliminates all flap-related complications and makes PRK the preferred option for patients in contact sports or military service. The trade-off is a slower, more uncomfortable recovery (3–5 days of epithelial healing) and the risk of subepithelial haze, particularly for higher corrections. Mitomycin C applied intraoperatively reduces haze formation significantly.

SMILE

Small incision lenticule extraction uses a femtosecond laser to cut a lenticule (a disc of stroma of carefully calculated thickness and diameter) within the intact cornea. The lenticule is then extracted through a 2–4 mm arc incision. No flap is created, and the anterior stroma is largely undisturbed. This preserves more corneal biomechanical integrity than LASIK. Dry eye symptoms are also less common because fewer corneal nerves are transected. SMILE is currently approved for myopia and myopic astigmatism; hypermetropic correction is under development.

Phakic IOL (Implantable Collamer Lens)

For high myopia beyond about −10D, corneal ablation would remove too much tissue. The Visian ICL is a posterior chamber phakic IOL placed between the iris and the crystalline lens, which is left in place. The vault — the clearance between the ICL and the anterior crystalline lens surface — must be adequate: too little causes anterior subcapsular cataract from contact, too much causes pigment dispersion and raised IOP. The ICL is reversible (can be explanted) and produces excellent optical quality because it sits close to the nodal point of the eye.

Contraindications to Corneal Refractive Surgery

  • Keratoconus or forme fruste keratoconus (corneal topography is mandatory pre-operatively)
  • Corneal thickness below 480 μm (insufficient residual bed after ablation)
  • Progressive myopia (refraction not stable for at least two years)
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding (refraction fluctuates)
  • Active ocular surface disease — dry eye, blepharitis, recurrent erosion
